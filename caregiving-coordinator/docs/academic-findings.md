# Academic findings on family care coordination (first pass)

Written 2026-11-01. **Evidence level for every row: the abstract was read; the full text is saved in `docs/sources/literature/papers/` but not yet read in full.** Numbers are as the abstract states them. Papers are listed in `docs/sources/INDEX.md` section 2o. US data unless noted.

| Finding | Paper (year) | Data and limit |
|---|---|---|
| Few family caregivers work alone. Coordination with medical professionals was rated weak; coordination with paid caregivers and with other family caregivers was rated moderate. Role training, more frequent contact and supportive communication went with higher ratings. | Family Caregiver Experiences Coordinating Care of Older Adults, JAMA Network Open (2025) | 2023 National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS) and linked National Study of Caregiving; 2,811 caregivers of adults 65+ with disabilities. Cross-sectional; ratings are self-reported. |
| About three quarters of older adults got help from only one child; average about 50 care hours a month at baseline. As a parent became frailer, more children helped and the hours became more equal. | Division of Parent Care Among Adult Children, J Gerontology B (2019) | NHATS 2011 to 2017. Counts children who help the parent, not whether children agree it is fair. |
| 52.9% of care networks involved "role-sharing" (family and paid caregivers helping with the same task). It was more common with dementia (48.7% vs 25.6%) and less common for Medicaid enrollees (32.1% vs 49.4%). | Family Care Partners and Paid Caregivers: National Estimates of Role-Sharing in Home Care, Gerontologist (2024) | 440 NHATS 2015 participants who received paid help. Small sample; 2015. |
| Of adult-child caregivers to Medicare beneficiaries 71+: 26.3% live with the parent, 47.4% live 1 to 20 minutes away, 13.1% 21 to 59 minutes, 13.2% an hour or more. Distance was not tied to differences in health-system logistics, advocacy or financial support tasks, or to depression and anxiety. | Caregiving across distances, Gerontologist (2026) | NHATS Round 11 (2021) linked to NSOC IV; 932 caregivers representing over 9 million. Only adult children of people 71+, so not all caregivers. |
| 74.8% of adults with a living parent or adult child had the nearest one within 30 miles; 35.5% had all of them that close. | Spatial Distance Between Parents and Adult Children in the United States, J Marriage and Family (2019) | 2013 Panel Study of Income Dynamics, adults 25+. Distance in miles, not care. |
| Shared online networks could fill communication gaps between home-care providers and families. Barriers: time, policy limits, privacy, security, who owns the information. | Caring in the Information Age, Canadian Geriatrics Journal (2017) | Interviews: 31 providers, 4 family caregivers (Canada). Tiny caregiver sample. |
| Care networks used a shared journal for information acquisition, sharing, strategy development and feedback; caregivers found it useful at the individual, network and relationship level. | "It made me feel like I wasn't alone in the darkness", JAMIA (2022) | 60-day use of one product's journal (CareVirtue) by dementia care networks. One product, small study. |
| Caregivers' main challenge was coordination under uncertainty: timely information, contingency when support fails, financial strain, cumulative planning effort. | Designing trustworthy, low-threshold care coordination, Frontiers in Digital Health (2026) | 13 stakeholders, three workshops, rural Austria (not US). |
| Most dementia caregivers had never used caregiving technology; "easy to install", "easy to learn" and "cost" ranked top. | Sex and gender differences in technology needs, BMC Geriatrics (2020) | 381 caregivers, recruited through Canadian dementia and home-care organisations. Not US. |
| 8 million US workers were family caregivers; they missed more work time (8% vs 4%) and reported more depression (53% vs 32%) and insomnia than non-caregivers. | The burden of family caregiving in the US, J Multidisciplinary Healthcare (2017) | 2013 National Health and Wellness Survey, employed adults. Self-reported. |
| Intensive caregivers (20+ hours a week) were 13 points less likely to be employed and 12 points more likely to quit a job. | Economic Spillover Effects of Intensive Unpaid Caregiving, PharmacoEconomics (2019) | 2013 RAND survey of military and veteran caregivers, 3,876 people. A specific group; may not match all caregivers. |
| Paid sick leave adoption increased care to older parents, mostly where paid family leave was also offered; effect was strongest for women and unpartnered adult children. | Paid Leave Mandates and Care for Older Parents, Milbank Quarterly (2024) | Health and Retirement Study 1998 to 2020, difference-in-differences. |
| Self-assessed abilities and computer skills predicted willingness to adopt technology; perceived value mattered among technology ratings. | Factors Predicting Decisions About Technology Adoption Among Older Adults, Innovation in Aging (2017) | 52 older adults, English and Spanish groups. About older adults, not caregivers. |

## What this changes (for the owner to judge)
- The sibling picture: in the 2019 study the common pattern is one child doing most of the care. That supports the project's assumption, but it says nothing about why or whether other siblings want to help.
- Distance: 13.2% (adult children of Medicare beneficiaries 71+) is a different base from the AARP 11% of all caregivers, so my earlier "about 6.5 million" estimate used a different base and should not be stacked with this one.
- Coordination with medical professionals is rated weakest, which fits the AARP figure that only 58% find coordinating with health professionals easy.
- Care coordination apps' real-world test: only one small study (CareVirtue) of a shared journal; no study found yet on willingness to pay for coordination, only for monitoring (Schulz 2016).
