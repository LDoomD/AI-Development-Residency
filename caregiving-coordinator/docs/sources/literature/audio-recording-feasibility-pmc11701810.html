Abstract

Objective:

The objective of this trial was to determine the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary effectiveness of sharing audio recordings of primary care visits with older adults with multimorbidity.

Methods:

We used a two-arm, randomized, controlled, feasibility trial with 3-month follow-up. Patients aged ≥65 years—with diabetes and hypertension—were recruited from academic primary care settings in New Hampshire, Tennessee, and Texas. Patients were randomized to receive online access to audio recordings of scheduled visits for three-months or care as usual (after visit summaries). Primary outcomes were acceptability and feasibility assessed using several indicators: acceptabilityrecruitment of 90 patients; recording use; and the Appropriateness of Intervention Measure (AIM; >3), feasibility- retention rate; protocol adherence; and the Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM; >3). Interviews were conducted with clinicians (n=14) and patients (n=19). Exploratory outcomes included patient activation, satisfaction, adherence, and quality of life.

Results:

We met recruitment (n=91) and retention (98%) targets and exceeded feasibility (Median FIM 4; IQR 3 – 4) and acceptability (Median AIM 4; IQR 3 – 4) metrics. Fidelity to protocol was high (92%), and 40 of 45 patients (85%) accessed their recordings. Interviewees noted the benefits of visit recording, including greater recall, understanding, and family engagement. Recording had little perceived impact on the visit interaction, and concerns about visit recording were minimal. Exploratory outcomes revealed better PROMIS Mental Health Scores for patients receiving Audio compared to Usual Care at 3 months: 51.5 (SD 7.7) vs. 47.7 (SD 9.9), P=0.04.

Conclusions:

Sharing visit recordings online with older adults is feasible and acceptable. A larger trial is needed to determine the impact of sharing recordings on patient health outcomes.

Practice Implications:

Recording is a highly scalable approach to supporting older adults and their care partners in managing care. Advances in natural language processing may unlock further opportunities for this innovative strategy.

Keywords: Patient Centered Communication, Older Adults, Technology

1. Introduction

Recall of medical information, including counseling provided during a medical visit, is essential for self-management and positive health outcomes.[1], [2] However, up to 80% of visit information is forgotten by patients.[3], [4], [5] Recall is particularly challenging for older adults,[6], [7] who have more complex visits due to multimorbidity,[8] hearing and vision challenges, along with age-related decline in information processing and working memory.[9], [10]

Access to After Visit Summaries (AVS) or clinic notes via patient portals are national strategies to improve information recall.[11] However, processing written information is challenging for many older adults.[3], [12], [13] The contents of the medical record are provider-focused,[14] and while the AVS is associated with patient satisfaction and recall,[15], [16], [17] there are concerns about accuracy, layout, and comprehensiveness.[18], [19], [20]

Audio recordings of clinic visits can augment written information by capturing the clinician’s counseling and recommendations verbatim. This strategy was first explored in the 1970s to improve the communication of medical information. Studies, predominantly in oncology, consistently find that listening to a visit recording is associated with increased patient satisfaction, recall, and understanding of information.[21], [22], [23] With the ubiquity of smartphones, some patients and clinicians are beginning to record clinic visits for patients’ personal use [24], [25] while select clinics have adopted the practice.[26], [27], [28] Evidence from a recent systematic review of 25 articles finds that providing recordings to older adults, 50 years or older, was associated with greater patient satisfaction, recall, and fulfillment of information needs, with few negative experiences reported. Less studied was the impact of recording on health-related outcomes, its use in primary care settings, and with older adults aged 65 or older. [23] Furthermore, there is scant evidence about the experience of accessing visit recordings online versus via a handheld recorder. In this multisite trial, we aimed to determine the feasibility and acceptability of online sharing of audio recordings of primary care clinic visits with older adults as part of routine care, an extension to the prior studies that focused on one-off recordings of visits where a specific diagnosis was discussed. We also explored the association between recording and health outcomes to inform the design of a future efficacy study.

2. Methods

2.1. Design and setting

We conducted a three-site, two-arm, patient-randomized feasibility trial with 3-month follow-up. The study sought to determine the feasibility and acceptability of sharing audio recordings of clinic visits (Audio), compared to the AVS alone (Usual Care/UC), on self-management among older adults diagnosed with diabetes and hypertension. The study was conducted between August 2020 and December 2021 in primary care clinics at Dartmouth-Health Primary Care (D-H; Manchester, NH), Vanderbilt University Medical Center (VUMC; Nashville, TN), and The University of Texas Medical Branch (UTMB; Galveston, TX). This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04344301) and approved by Dartmouth-Health Institutional Review Board (#STUDY02000214) on February 10, 2020. All participants provided written informed consent.

2.2. Participant Recruitment

We recruited primary care clinicians including Medical Doctor (MD), Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine (DO), Physician Assistant (PA), and Advanced Practice Research Nurse (APRN) at each site. Patients were ≥65 years, receiving medication for diabetes and hypertension, with ≥1 clinic visit (including telehealth) in the previous seven months, spoke English or Spanish, and planned on attending the clinic for the subsequent six months. We excluded patients with cognitive impairment (a six-item screener (SIS) cognitive function score ≤4)[29], schizophrenia or psychotic disorders, substance-use disorders, uncorrectable hearing or visual impairment, who lived in skilled nursing homes or hospice, who had audio recorded a visit for their personal use in the previous six months, and patients without email access. After a two-week ‘opt-out’ window, patients were sent an invitation letter and contacted by study staff by telephone to assess eligibility and complete consent. Patient recruitment began in August 2020, and data collection was completed in December 2021.

2.3. Interventions

During the trial, patients received a written AVS as usual. Patients randomized to Audio received usual care plus recordings of scheduled visits with their study clinician (in-person or telehealth) for three months. Recordings were captured in HealthPAL, an online, open-source recording application developed by PJB (Figure 1).[30] Research staff started the recording at the beginning of the visit and left the room. Patient or clinician participants could pause or stop the recording at any time. After the visit, a research assistant demonstrated to the patient participant how to access recordings online, including logging into the platform, navigating to and playing a recording, and resetting a password. Patients in the Audio arm received a listening reminder one day and one week after their primary care visit and three days before any follow-up visit. Our choice of ‘care as usual’ in this stage I trial is supported by recommendations from a NIH Expert Panel on choosing appropriate trial comparators.[31]

2.4. Randomization

Patients were randomly allocated by the research coordinator in REDCap with a 1:1 ratio to Audio or UC using block-randomization, ensuring equal distribution of intervention and control group patients for each participating clinician. Group allocation was not revealed to the study statistician (JO).

2.5. Outcomes

The study’s objective was to determine the acceptability and feasibility of the study protocol and the sharing of visit recordings online with older adults. A priori indicators of acceptability and feasibility of the trial protocol included 1) recruitment of 90 patients; retention rate of >70% at 3 months, fidelity to study protocol (>90%) assessed using a 16-item checklist; 2) evaluation of the recording intervention by the 4-item Appropriateness of Intervention Measure (AIM) at 3 months, where a score of >3 out of a maximum of 5 characterizes the intervention as appropriate [32]; and the 4-item Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) at 3 months where a score of >3 out of a maximum of 5 indicates the intervention is feasible [32]; and recording use rates of >70% in the intervention group.

Exploratory outcomes were patient-reported, and included patient activation[33], medication[34] and general treatment adherence[35], physical and mental quality of life[36], depression[37], anxiety[38], satisfaction[39], and interpersonal process of care[40] (see Supplement 1 for more details). Patients completed outcome assessments independently online, by telephone interview, or in person with a trained research assistant (see supplement 1 for further description of the outcomes). Baseline assessments were completed before group allocation, followed by assessments at one week and three months. Patients received a $30 honorarium for the completion of baseline assessments and a $20 honorarium for the completion of each follow-up assessment (See Table 1).

2.6. Qualitative Interviews and Analysis

Upon completion of the study, we invited patients and physicians from the Audio arm to take part in semi-structured debrief interviews with a trained interviewer to assess their experience with the trial protocol and intervention (Supplements 1 and 2. Interview guides). We expected that our sample size of 6 – 12 for each stakeholder group would be adequate to achieve saturation of key themes.[41] Interviews were 20 – 30 minutes by telephone or video call, and participants received $30. ECS and PJB applied qualitative content analysis [42] to independently code interview transcripts using a template approach.[43] PJB and ECS independently coded a sub-set of transcripts and met to review the coding and discuss any discrepancies. PJB and ECS coded the remaining transcripts with regular quality checks and discussions to ensure consistent application of the codes. Coded data were aggregated into code reports. Code reports were subsequently reviewed by ECS, who wrote analytic memos as a technique to synthesize core meanings and patterns to construct the key qualitative findings presented here. We adhered to Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ) checklist.[44]

2.7. Statistical Analysis

A sample of 10 – 40 participants is considered adequate to determine feasibility and gather preliminary outcome data in a stage 1 trial.[45] We aimed to recruit 30 patients at each of the three sites to estimate the operating diagnostics (e.g., retention rates) and to inform power and sample-size calculations for a future study. We were not powered to detect significant differences in exploratory outcomes. Descriptive analysis is presented for feasibility and acceptability data, and intent-to-treat analysis for outcomes data at one week and three months. We compared participant characteristics and exploratory outcomes at baseline, one-week, and three months using t-tests and chi-square tests. We estimated separate models for our exploratory health-related outcomes, in which group status interacted with follow-up time, to explore if associations with treatment assignment varied by time. Analyses were performed using R, version 3.4.1. (R core team, 2020), Vienna, Austria.

3. Results

We enrolled 16 clinicians of 17 approached; one clinician declined because of they were leaving the clinic. Of 420 potential patients who received a study information letter, we enrolled 91: 120 were ineligible; 137 declined; 65 could not be reached and 7 could not take part for other reasons (Figure 2 – CONSORT). Removing ineligible patients and patients who could not be reached, the recruitment rate was 39% (91/235 patients). Participants had a mean age of 73.7 years (SD 5.7), were primarily female (65%), White (82%) and non-Hispanic (90%) (Table 2).

3.1. Feasibility and acceptability

We exceed thresholds of feasibility and acceptability for both our trial protocol and intervention use. Of 91 patients recruited, only two were lost to follow-up (98% retention), and fidelity to the protocol was high, with 84 (92%) patients completing all protocol elements. All answers for the sample exceeded the prespecified target score of 3 on the FIM (Median 4; IQR 3 – 4) and AIM (Median 4; IQR 3 – 4), with the agreement shown for each question in Table 3. In total, 40 of 45 patients (85%) accessed their recordings online. Five instances of technical difficulties were resolved. These included the primary device stopping recording (resolved through software updates), low volume on playback (resolved through improved device setup procedures), and inadvertent deletion of email listening regime reminders by the participant (resolved through re-training). Most patients (85%) reviewed their recordings within 24 hours of the visit. Of eight patients with a follow-up visit during the data collection period, none listened three days before their next clinic, one listened a week before their upcoming visit, and one patient listened a month before their follow-up visit; patients stated that they forgot or were unaware of this request, forgetting amid other activities as well as not setting aside time to listen.

3.2. Patient and Clinician Perspectives

Follow-up interviews were conducted with 19 patients (median age 74 years [range 65 – 84 years]; 14 females; 16 non-Hispanic White) and 14 clinicians (median age 45 years [32 – 69 years]; 11 females; 5 non-White and 9 non-Hispanic White). Patients and clinicians reported that recordings were of high value, promoting recall and understanding of visit information, especially in emotional visits, ease of sharing with loved ones, and reducing phone calls from patients to the clinic. Specific to this older adult population was the perception that visit recordings could be particularly helpful for patients with cognitive impairment.

“I think there’s a lot of benefit, especially if you have one of those appointments where you get surprising news, and you can’t help but react emotionally. And the minute your emotions get activated, you don’t really hear everything.” (VUpatient018)

“Older people that have a hard time understanding complicated medical issues or have family members that want to partake, but can’t, in visits. I think that’s very beneficial.” (DHpatient018).

“A lot of older patients who have some mild cognitive issues, not severe, so people who are okay to be here on their own but don’t always remember everything perfectly and have family members that then are trying to help them out with their care.” (DHClin03).

While not common, concerns revolved around data privacy and the potential challenges of managing large volumes of recordings over time. “Well, I would think after a while, they would pile up.” (UTpatient009). For clinicians, the few concerns raised included legal issues and possible negative implications of patients revisiting sensitive issues outside of the clinic visit: “I just would worry about them kind of wanting to talk about more sensitive issues…, having worries that those recordings would be heard by other people or …having to kind of go back and listen to them retell their whatever traumatic story.” (DHClin02).

Implementing visit recording in the clinic was considered straightforward, and despite initial apprehension, patients and clinicians expressed that recordings did not impact their clinical interactions or workflow.

“I liked the fact it didn’t interfere with how [my doctor] interacted with me… It looked like everybody just was kind of able to put it out of their mind immediately that, “Hey, this is a recording device. So what? Let’s go ahead with our normal visit.” (UTpatient024).

“I was concerned that it was going to impact what the patients felt like they could divulge to me, and that that might impact their care. My perception is that that was not the case.” (DHClin03).

Finally, patients predominately found accessing and using the recordings online to be straightforward:

“I’m not a very techy person, so I can’t really comment on it too much, but just to say that it was fine. I got from it what I needed to get from it, so that worked out-- it worked pretty well.” (VUpatient021)

“I mean, it was just black and white. You know what I mean? You just press on this, and this will happen. So, I wasn’t intimidated or anything by it. I felt comfortable doing it.” (DHpatient013)

4. Discussion

4.1. Principal Results

In a sample of older adult patients with multimorbidity from three geographically distinct healthcare systems, sharing clinic visit recordings online with patients was feasible and acceptable, with the majority attesting that the recording met their approval and was easy and doable. Concerns were not commonly expressed but included privacy, data management, and how recording might affect openness during the visit. Across all sites, the recruitment target was met, and no participants left the trial due to concerns with the intervention.

4.2. Limitations

Our trial has several limitations. Of 420 patients approached, 87 (20.7%) were not interested or gave no reason for not taking part. We cannot disentangle those not interested in research from those explicitly not interested in recording. Research assistants were aware of group allocation at assessments, however most patients (approximately 69%; 63/91) completed assessments independently online reducing likely impact of assessment bias specifically regarding feasibility and acceptability. Importantly, while representative of the participating healthcare systems, the diversity of the enrolled sample will require enhancement in future studies to increase generalizability of the findings.

4.3. Comparison with Prior Work

This study advances our understanding of the implementation of recording during clinical encounters by demonstrating the feasibility and acceptability of a trial where clinic visit recordings were shared online with older adults managing multimorbidity.[23] We found that older adults use visit recordings and are highly interested, as signified by high recruitment, retention, and intervention use. A common concern of technology trials with older adults is their lack of interest or comfort. Yet, our recruitment rate of 40% is in line with median recruitment rates of older adults more broadly in clinical research.[46] A paucity of potential participants declined participation because they did not want to be recorded, while only 26 (6.2%) potential patients were ineligible due to lack of access to email, including not wanting to create an email address. This study provides evidence supporting that recording in-and-of-itself is not a significant concern for patients and that sharing online recordings of visits is scalable in an older adult population.

In addition, the retention rate was excellent, with 98% of participants remaining in the study and completing all assessments over the study duration, indicating the high acceptability of the trial protocol. Our findings of high acceptability and feasibility are similar to prior studies with younger patients and of single-visit recordings.[21], [22], [23] Exploratory outcomes trended in a positive direction for those who received audio recordings compared to those in the usual care group, notably patient activation, quality of life, general adherence to medical advice and however given the small sample size we were not powered to detect statistically significant differences. The observed exploratory finding of better mental health scores for patients in the recording arm is of interest as it is above the threshold of minimally important change in PROMIS and supports the findings of improved mental well-being reported by Mishra et al.[47] However, these results should be interpreted with caution, given our small sample.[48]

Limitations of written summaries are described in prior literature,[18], [19], [20] and participants noted the additional value of visit recordings. Participants suggested that recordings represent a comprehensive record of the visit conversation, which adds information beyond a written summary. A clinic visit is a multidimensional exchange of information between a patient and clinician. There is information in the tone of voice and speed of conversation that can convey emotional support, concern, and importance of information which is related to patient satisfaction and health outcomes.[49], [50]

Themes identified during the follow-up interviews of greater understanding, recall, and family engagement support those found in other populations.[22] While prior literature focused mainly on oncology settings highlight the benefit of revisiting an emotional diagnosis,[21], [22], [23] our work also highlights the potential for recordings to promote greater treatment adherence in primary care, especially in older adults where cognitive impairments are more common. Less research has reflected clinician viewpoints, and this study’s findings support that recordings do not appear to negatively impact the clinic flow or interaction.[22] Interestingly, the observation that sharing recordings could result in fewer clinic contacts by patients (i.e., phone calls, patient portal messages) has been found in one practice where visit recordings are commonly shared.36 Finally, concerns have been raised by patients and clinicians in prior literature about the negative impact of replaying distressing content in oncology settings,[51] echoing the concern raised about revisiting sensitive experiences. While potentially less common, these important concerns must be considered in future efforts related to recording implementation.

5. Conclusion

While the implementation of recordings has been limited to a small number of clinics and health systems, we are on the precipice of recordings potentially happening at scale across healthcare as both patients and clinicians record visits for their personal use.[28], [52] This study contributes foundational understanding of how recordings can be carried out efficiently in active medical encounters without burdening patients and clinicians. With an aging and increasingly medically complex patient population, many of whom have challenges with cognition, recordings may be an effective and efficient strategy to support disease management, seamless care, fidelity of clinic visit information, and patient and caregiver involvement.

6. Practice implications

The ability of patients to recall medical information is fundamental to successful self-management, yet up to 80% of what is discussed during clinic visits is forgotten. This is especially concerning for older adults who experience complex clinic visits due to multimorbidity. Visit recordings are an emerging strategy to improve communication of visit information. This is the first intervention trial that focuses on the routine provision of recordings to older adults. Recording was feasible, acceptable, and demonstrated preliminary effectiveness in improving quality of life. Recording is a highly scalable approach to supporting older adults and their care partners in managing care; advances in natural language processing will unlock further opportunities for this innovative strategy.
