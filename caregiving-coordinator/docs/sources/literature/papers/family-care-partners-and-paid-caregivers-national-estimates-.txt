Abstract

Background and Objectives

We describe “role-sharing” in home care, defined as family care partners and paid caregivers assisting with the same task(s).

Research Design and Methods

We studied 440 participants in the 2015 National Health and Aging Trends Study receiving paid help with self-care, mobility, or medical care. We describe patterns in receiving paid help only, help from care partners only, and role-sharing. We examine whether sole reliance on paid help or role-sharing differs by Medicaid-enrollment and dementia status.

Results

Half (52.9%) of care networks involved role-sharing. Care networks involving role-sharing more often occurred among older adults with dementia (48.7% vs 25.6%, p < .001) and less often for those who were Medicaid-enrolled (32.1% vs 49.4%, p < .01). Those living with dementia more often experienced role-sharing in eating (odds ratio [OR] 3.9 [95% confidence interval {CI} 1.20, 8.50]), bathing (OR 2.7, [95% CI 1.50, 4.96]), dressing (OR 2.1 [95% CI 1.14, 3.86]), toileting (OR 2.9 [95% CI 1.23, 6.74]), and indoor mobility (OR 2.8 [95% CI 1.42, 5.56]), and less often received help solely from paid helpers with medication administration (OR 0.24, [95% CI .12, .46]). Medicaid-enrollees more often received paid help only in dressing (OR 2.0 [95% CI 1.12, 3.74]), outdoor (OR 2.4 [95% CI 1.28, 4.36]), and indoor mobility (OR 4.3 [95% CI 2.41, 7.62]), and with doctor visits (OR 2.8 [95% CI 1.29, 5.94]).

Discussion and Implications

Role-sharing is common, especially among older adults living with dementia who are not Medicaid-enrolled. Strategies supporting information sharing and collaboration in home-based care merit investigation.

Keywords: Dementia, Family caregiving, Medicaid, Paid caregiving, Shared care

Older adults living with disability in the community often rely on a network of helpers that includes both care partners (i.e., family and other unpaid helpers) and paid caregivers such as personal care aides, home care attendants, and home health aides. Over the past several years, states have undergone efforts to rebalance Medicaid spending from institutions to community settings, resulting in higher acuity among individuals receiving help in the community (Scales, 2020). Population aging and lower birth rates suggest the possibility of future reduced availability of adult children alongside increased demand for paid care (Scales, 2019). The dynamics of shared care between care partners and paid caregivers have important implications for care quality and quality of life (Sims-Gould & Martin-Matthews, 2010) but are poorly understood.

Care partners often coordinate, manage, and direct the role of paid caregivers (Reckrey et al., 2022). They may collaborate with paid caregivers when the needs of care recipients extend beyond paid caregiver availability or when it is more efficient or effective to work together (Sims-Gould & Martin-Matthews, 2010). Paid caregivers and care partners may assist with tasks independent of one another (Franzosa & Emma, 2021), such as one person helping with bathing and another with toileting. Paid caregivers may be preferred for activities that family members are unable to do, feel uncomfortable doing, or where their help is not wanted by the person they assist (Shaw et al., 2021).

This analysis focuses on “role-sharing,” which reflects situations in which care partners and paid caregivers assist with the same task(s). Role-sharing may occur through working together to complete a task, such as a care partner and paid caregiver helping with transferring to the bathroom simultaneously or alternating this responsibility when one helper is not available. Another example of role-sharing is when caregivers set up each other’s responsibilities, such as a care partner leaving a prepared meal for a paid caregiver to deliver (Sims-Gould & Martin-Matthews, 2010).

Role-sharing between care partners and paid caregivers can be beneficial but may also impose challenges. Although care partners and paid caregivers have deep knowledge about those they assist, misalignment in expectations, gaps in information about the older adult’s care plan, and lack of shared understanding of each other’s roles may inhibit relational coordination (collaborating for the purpose of task integration) (Burgdorf et al., 2023; Franzosa et al., 2018; Shaw et al., 2021). Care partner demands and expectations can exceed a paid caregiver’s capability or conflict with what the care plan allows (Drake, 2019; Franzosa et al., 2019; Min et al., 2021; Sterling et al., 2020; Stone & Bryant, 2019).

The prevalence and nature of role-sharing may vary across features of individual characteristics and helping networks. For example, older adults who are enrolled in both Medicare and Medicaid (“dual-enrollees”) are afforded access to Medicaid Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) that are administered through state and waiver programs (Garfield et al., 2015). Medicaid HCBS programs are often subject to strict functional and/or cognitive acuity requirements and caps on the number of individuals who are served. Thus, those receiving Medicaid HCBS may experience limits on the availability and provision of paid services. Older adults living with dementia are overrepresented among those receiving paid care, and role-sharing may be more common due to reliance on larger care networks and progressively more intensive need for support over time (Fabius et al., 2023a; Kasper et al., 2016). Role sharing may also fluctuate with respect to specific activities of variable intensity and time demands (e.g., hands-on assistance with bathing vs medication reminders). Given recommendations that paid caregivers be better integrated in home care team collaborations (Stone & Bryant, 2019), a critical first step includes descriptively understanding how care coordination manifests between family care partners and paid caregivers.

Conceptual Framework

Our analysis is guided by Litwak’s task specificity model. The task specificity model provides a framework for caregiving networks based on the combination of fit between services required and caregiver availability among care partners and paid caregivers (Litwak, 1985b). The care provided by care partners is a result of a combination of several characteristics, including proximity to the care recipient, length of commitment, commonality of lifestyle, and availability of other caregivers. Alternatively, care provided by paid caregivers is thought to be generally motivated by economic incentives, type of work, and level of technical knowledge (Allen & Ciambrone, 2003). How help is distributed is affected by whether an activity requires ongoing contact and substantial time or resources and can be reasonably shared. For example, family care partners and paid caregivers may more often role-share in activities that require physical assistance (e.g., bathing, dressing) by both helping the older adult with a task at the same time. Alternatively, role-sharing may also manifest in family care partners and paid caregivers alternating responsibilities at different times of the day or days of the week. The present study describes the prevalence of role-sharing, where a substantial amount of coordination may take place.

The present study expands our understanding of older adults’ care networks by examining the intersecting roles of care partners and paid caregivers across specific tasks. First, we examine whether and how role-sharing varies by older adult sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, race and ethnicity, Medicaid-enrollment), health and function (e.g., level of assistance, dementia status), and caregiving networks (e.g., hours of care, living arrangement). Second, we describe the prevalence by task of reliance on paid help only, care partners only, or role-sharing. Because dual-enrollees and older adults living with dementia are more likely to use paid care, we specifically examine such patterns by Medicaid-enrollment and dementia status (Fabius et al., 2022; Reckrey et al., 2020a). We expect that care arrangements will fluctuate across tasks and characteristics, and that role-sharing will be particularly pronounced among self-care and mobility activities that are essential activities for daily living. Due to greater reliance on paid care and more expansive care networks (Fabius et al., 2022; Kasper et al., 2016), we also expect that role-sharing will be more prevalent for dual-enrollees and older adults living with dementia.

Method

Data and Sample

We use data from Round 5 (2015) of the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS), a nationally representative study of Medicare beneficiaries ages 65 and older in the contiguous United States. NHATS was fielded in 2011, with annual follow-up interviews. In 2015, the sample (n = 8,334) was replenished (Freedman & Kasper, 2019). In-person interviews are conducted with study participants or proxy respondents if the participant is unable to respond. We excluded decedents (n = 296), those living in nursing homes (n = 403) or residential care settings (n = 565), those not receiving help with self-care (bathing, dressing, eating, toileting), mobility (getting around inside and outside of the house, getting in and out of bed), or medical care (medication administration, help at doctors’ visits) activities (n = 4,070), and those who only received unpaid help only (n = 2,560). The final study cohort included 440 older adults representing 1,668,580 community-dwelling older adults who were receiving paid help with self-care, mobility, or medical care activities.

We construct caregiving network measures from the NHATS Other Person (OP) file. The OP file provides a roster of unique helpers for each respondent with such information as helper relationship to the older adult, activities for which they provide help (self-care, mobility, or medical care), and whether they were paid. The OP roster includes information about family members (e.g., spouses, children, other family) who may or may not live with the older adult. It is possible that children and other family members may be paid for their help, such as through Medicaid self-direction (Ruggiano, 2012). We categorize children who were paid for providing help as care partners in this analysis.

Measures

Older adult characteristics

We examine selected sociodemographic, health/function, and care arrangement characteristics demonstrated to be associated with the receipt of paid and unpaid caregivers (Fabius et al., 2022, 2023a; Parker & Fabius, 2022; Reckrey et al., 2020b; Spillman et al., 2020; Travers et al., 2021). Sociodemographic characteristics include: age (65–74; 75–84; 85+), gender, and race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and other race/ethnicity). Dementia is classified using a validated measure of probable dementia, based on self-report of dementia diagnosis, a score indicating dementia on the AD8 Dementia Screening Interview, or performance on cognitive tests, orientation, and executive function (Kasper et al., 2023). Level of disability refers to the number of routine daily activities older adults received help with, dichotomized by one or two activities versus three or more activities. We include living arrangements (lived alone, with a spouse with or without others, or with others only) to reflect available support. We calculated total weekly hours of care provided by care partners and paid caregivers using a validated methodology that imputes missing hours of care for a small number of older adults based on their age, gender, and reported level of need, and, for care partners, their relationship to the older adult (Freedman & Spillman, 2014). We include a three-category measure that reflects the number of hours of care received per week (20 h or less, 21–39 h, 40 h or more).

Role-sharing

We operationalize role-sharing as in prior work (Spillman et al., 2020), building from the OP file roster to categorize types of task assistance provided by each helper as follows: four self-care tasks (bathing/showering, dressing, eating, toileting), three mobility tasks (getting around inside and outside the house, getting in and out of bed), and two medical care activity tasks (medication administration, receiving assistance at doctors’ appointments). Role-sharing is defined as a care partner and paid caregiver assisting with the same task (Spillman et al., 2020). We constructed dichotomous measures that reflect role-sharing by task, as well as a global measure reflecting any role-sharing across all activities.

Statistical Analysis

We first use Pearson’s chi-square to describe older adults by whether their network involved role-sharing. We also conduct supplemental analyses to describe older adult characteristics across receipt of help from family care partners only and role-sharing among those receiving paid help. Next, we present descriptive statistics providing estimates of older adults receiving help from paid caregivers only, care partners only, or both care partners and paid caregivers (role-sharing) with self-care, mobility, or medical care tasks. Finally, we use unadjusted logistic regression models to assess the association between Medicaid-enrollment and dementia status and experiencing role-sharing or reliance on paid help only by task. All data were analyzed using Stata Version 15 (StataCorp, 2017) and were weighted using NHATS survey weights (Freedman et al., 2019).

Results

Among older adults receiving paid help (weighted n = 1,668,580; Table 1), roughly half of older adults (52.9%) had care networks that involved role-sharing. Older adults whose care networks involved role-sharing were less likely to be Medicaid-enrolled (31.0% vs 49.4%, p < .01), and more likely to be living with dementia (48.7% vs 256%, p < .01) or receiving assistance with three or more activities (91.1% vs 62.9%, p < .01). Older adults whose networks involved role-sharing more often lived with others (40.1% vs 23.3%), were receiving help from four or more helpers (38.8% vs 12.7 %, p < .001), and were receiving 40 or more hours of care per week (63.8% vs 28.7%, p < .001). It should be noted that older adults receiving paid help represented ~12% of those receiving assistance with self-care, mobility, or medical care activities (Supplemental Table 1 in the Supplementary Material). Relative to those receiving help from family care partners only, older adults who experienced role-sharing were more often older, female, living with dementia, and receiving help with three or more activities from a larger helping network and for a greater number of hours per week.

Discussion

Our findings advance knowledge regarding collaboration between family care partners and paid caregivers and are timely, given the growing demand for direct care. We find that, among more than 1.6 million older adults receiving paid help with self-care, mobility, and medical care tasks, more than half receive help from care partners and paid caregivers who share roles. As might be expected according to Litwak’s task specificity model, role-sharing is particularly pronounced for self-care and mobility tasks and in the context of dementia, where assistance is likely needed on an ongoing basis across multiple caregivers (Litwak, 1985a, 1985b; Messeri et al., 1993). Contrary to our hypothesis, dual-enrollees are less likely to experience role-sharing and more likely to receive assistance solely from paid caregivers. Given recent recommendations to increase opportunities to support paid caregivers through training, engagement with clinical care teams, and an expanded scope of practice, the present study yields directions for future work and has implications for policy and practice related to home care delivery.

Findings reinforce the importance of medical care activities in home-based care. Policymakers and practitioners have advocated for greater nurse delegation to support and advance the careers of paid caregivers. Some states have pushed the needle forward on this issue—allowing nurses to delegate specific medical care activities such as medication management and blood glucose monitoring to paid caregivers—whereas others have not (Stone & Bryant, 2019). Although paid caregivers may assist with doctors’ visits (Fabius et al., 2023b; Reckrey et al., 2019), their scope of practice limits the extent in which they can legally and ethically help older adults in doctors’ visits, such as by sharing information with and between care partners and clinicians (McMullen et al., 2015). Most states limit paid caregivers’ involvement with medication administration to reminding care recipients to adhere to medications, rather than having a direct role in dispensing them. Care partners report disagreements about medication management with paid caregivers (Joyce et al., 2014); such responsibilities are complex and nuanced. For example, whereas care partners may administer medications and refill prescriptions, paid caregivers might remind care partners about medication refills, engage in organizing medications, or oversee medication adherence (Shaw et al., 2021). In these circumstances, conflict may arise when paid caregivers feel criticized or overmanaged by family care partners (Franzosa & Emma, 2021). Additionally, family care partners may experience greater burden in situations where they are responsible for managing and coordinating care across multiple individuals and assign tasks (Reckrey et al., 2022). Interventional work is needed to improve family care partner and paid caregiver expectations for services and understanding of one another’s roles. Additionally, future work should assess whether state-level policies on nursing delegation affect help with medical care activities from paid caregivers.

Our finding that older dual-enrollees were less likely to experience role-sharing, due largely to relying solely on paid caregivers for most tasks, merits comment. In the context of Medicaid-funded home-based care, role-sharing is not only affected by the availability of care partners but also by care plan guidelines and maximum allowable hours, which varies by state across differing levels of commitment to Medicaid HCBS. (Ng et al., 2015). Greater reliance on paid caregivers in this population may result from preferences for paid care. Prior work indicates that dual-enrollees receiving paid care are more likely to report adverse consequences due to unmet need (going without completing routine activities due to the unavailability of help) and are particularly prone to experiencing low social engagement (Fabius et al., 2022, 2023a). To date, interventions to improve collaborations between care partners and paid caregivers have been limited to nursing homes and some outpatient settings (i.e., primary care, rehabilitative therapy), resulting in a ripe environment for embedded pragmatic interventions (Bangerter et al., 2016; Rommerskirch-Manietta et al., 2021), including Medicaid HCBS (Fortinsky et al., 2023). These interventions are particularly important for optimizing collaborations between family care partners and paid caregivers in circumstances where the allotment of services (e.g., service type, number of hours) falls short of what a family believes an older adult needs. Additionally, a range of strategies could facilitate improved care and support of paid caregivers, from more expansive training requirements (Fabius et al., 2023a) to better inclusion in care plan development (Franzosa et al., 2019), clarifying roles and improving information sharing between family care partners and paid caregivers, and better familiarizing paid caregivers with care recipient and family care partner preferences for care delivery (e.g., daily routines) at the outset of services (McBride et al., 2011).

Role-sharing was more pronounced for people living with dementia, emphasizing the substantial level of involvement of care partners in home care delivery for this population (Fabius et al., 2023b). Older adults living with dementia are more likely to receive paid care and are overrepresented in Medicaid Home and Community-Based Services (Fabius et al., 2023a; Konetzka et al., 2020; Reckrey et al., 2020a). As is the case with dual-enrollees, those living with dementia may have needs that go beyond available services. Recent federal recommendations identify opportunities for future interventions to strengthen the direct care workforce that consider care partners, including assessing the effect of care partner and paid caregiver collaborations on care quality and assurance of person- and family-centered care (National Research Summit on Care, Services, and Supports for Persons Living with Dementia and Their Care Partners/Caregivers, 2023; Larson & Stroud, 2021).

Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations. This is a small cross-sectional analysis, and our results cannot be interpreted as causal. We are unable to consider nuances of paid care that may affect our results, such as the type of services that people are receiving (e.g., nursing vs personal care), as well as the amount of time someone has been receiving care. Future studies should consider longitudinal analyses and qualitative methods to examine these specific factors related to care delivery. Future studies should also consider varying models of paid care, such as Medicaid HCBS-funded self-directed services, where care recipients are responsible for the hiring and supervision of help (Applebaum & Mahoney, 2016). Next, our relatively small sample size cannot support multivariable models adjusting for the full range of relevant characteristics that might contribute to role-sharing. However, the NHATS data enables us to observe the involvement of paid and unpaid sources of home care in specific tasks. Finally, our sample is reflective of the receipt of care prior to the COVID-19 global pandemic, and more recent data may show varying estimates of paid care receipt and role-sharing. Despite these limitations, the present study yields important findings for understanding role-sharing among older adults receiving paid care.

Conclusion

Findings from this study demonstrate that role-sharing with routine daily activities is common. Results have implications for ongoing efforts to improve coordination and engagement between paid caregivers and family care partners. These efforts include policy initiatives and revisions under Medicaid HCBS to increase paid caregiver compensation and strengthen family care partner supports (Burns & Mohamed, 2023; Fabius et al., 2023a), as well as federal initiatives like the Recognize, Assist, Include, Support, and Engage Family Caregivers Act, which provides guidance to local and state organizations to strengthen policies and programs directed at empowering and supporting care partners and paid caregivers (2022 National Strategy to Support Family Caregivers, 2022; Executive Order on Increasing Access to High-Quality Care and Supporting Caregivers, 2023). Our findings demonstrate that role-sharing between care partners and paid caregivers is common, especially among those who are living with dementia and not enrolled in Medicaid. Future directions include interventions that align with national recommendations to increase understanding of and strengthen relationships between care partners and paid caregivers.
