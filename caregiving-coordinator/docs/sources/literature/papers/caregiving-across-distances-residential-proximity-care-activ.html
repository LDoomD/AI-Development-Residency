Abstract

Background and Objectives

Adult children increasingly provide care to parents from varying distances, yet the relationship between proximity and caregiver mental health is not well understood. This study characterizes caregiving activities of adult-child caregivers based on residential proximity to their parent and examines associations with depression and anxiety symptoms.

Research Design and Methods

Data from the 2021 National Health and Aging Trends Study Round 11 was linked with National Study of Caregiving IV. The sample included adult-child caregivers of Medicare beneficiaries aged 71 and older. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression models assessed associations between proximity (travel time between the caregivers and their parents), caregiving activities, and symptoms of depression and anxiety. Analyses were weighted to produce nationally representative estimates.

Results

Among 932 caregivers representing more than 9 million caregivers nationwide, 26.3% co-reside with care-recipients, 47.4% live 1–20 min away, 13.1% live 21–59 min away, and 13.2% are long-distance caregivers living ≥1 hr away. Co-residing caregivers were more likely to perform health management, health care, Activities of Daily Living, and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living tasks. However, proximity was not associated with differences in health system logistics, patient advocacy, and financial support activities. Proximity did not predict of depression or anxiety in adjusted models.

Discussion and Implications

Adult children engage in diverse caregiving activities regardless of distance, especially in logistics, advocacy, and financial support. Factors beyond proximity likely influence caregiver mental health. Findings highlight the need for tailored policies and services supporting both distance and proximal caregivers of older adults.

Keywords: Long-distance caregivers, Residential proximity, Caregiving activities, Mental health

Introduction

In the United States, over 54 million people are caregivers to a family member or friend age 50 years or older with a health problem or physical or cognitive disability. Nearly half (47%) are adult children caring for their parents or parents-in-law (National Alliance for Caregiving and AARP, 2025). While most (75%) caregivers of older adults live within 20 min of the care recipient, 13% live between 20 min and 1 hr away, and another 11% live more than 1 hr away, representing a substantial number of distance caregivers nationally (National Alliance for Caregiving and AARP, 2025). Long-distance caregivers—previously defined as those who live an hour or more away by any means of travel (National Institute on Aging, 2023)—are a relatively understudied sub-group of caregivers (Douglas et al., 2016; Zucchetto et al., 2022).

Several factors have prompted the need for deeper engagement related to long-distance caregivers in recent years. First, the COVID-19 pandemic impacted proximity between adults caring for their parents, both by bringing families closer together into quarantine pods, or alternatively, by reducing contact to protect parents from illness (Wenner Moyer, 2020), particularly in 2020 and 2021 before the widespread adoption of the vaccine. Second, legislation and technology to support telehealth and video conferencing capabilities has been widely adopted, particularly since the COVID-19 pandemic (Shaver, 2022). The 21st Century Cures Act (Public Law No. 114-225), which was signed into law in 2016 and made effective in June of 2020 created opportunities for open access of provider notes and laboratory results directly to patients, through the customary use of electronic health record patient portals (Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology, 2020). This change has expanded electronic communication of medical information online, including both formal and informal proxy portal access (i.e., access by patient-designated family members and friends) (Reynolds et al., 2023). As a result, it has made it more efficient for long-distance caregivers to engage with care recipients and their health care providers (Reynolds et al., 2023). As of 2025, 38% of caregivers reported using software to track a care recipient’s finances, and 25% reported using apps, videos, wearable devices, or other remote monitoring to check in with their care recipient (National Alliance for Caregiving and AARP, 2025). Finally, the U.S. Caregiver Advise Record Enable (CARE) Act–which calls for hospitals to engage patients in conversations about who will be caring for them at home, record the name of identified caregivers in the medical record, and notify and train caregivers in preparation for the care of the patient, particularly around the time of transitions of care (e.g., from hospital to home)—codified the importance of the caregiver role to the U.S. health system (Applebaum et al., 2026). In light of these recent changes, it is more important now than ever to revisit the role of long-distance caregivers, not only because what these caregivers can do to support older adults with chronic disease and disability is changing over time, but also to understand what distance caregivers are doing given differences in travel time to the care recipients’ community-based residences.

In 2022, 21.4% of the US population reported symptoms of depression, while 18.2% reported symptoms of anxiety, a significant increase from 2019 (7% and 6%, respectively) (Terlizzi & Zablotsky, 2024). For caregivers the prevalence is higher; a recent analysis suggest that nearly one-third of caregivers experience symptoms of depression (33.35%) and anxiety (35.25%) (Soh et al., 2025). Proximity to the care recipient also seems to play a role in the mental health of caregivers. Previous research suggests that caregivers who live an hour or more away are more likely to experience increased stress due to communication barriers with the care recipients’ health care team since they are often unable to attend health appointments and rely on secondhand information from the care recipient or local caregivers (Douglas et al., 2016). A previous study on adult children long-distance caregivers of parents with cancer demonstrated increased anxiety symptoms and increased total mood disturbance compared to local caregivers (Mazanec, 2012). Other studies have indicated that caregivers who provide tangible, in-person caregiving tasks like Activities of Daily Living (ADL) and Instrumental ADLs tend to experience higher perceived stress (Lyons et al., 2015) and sleep disturbance (Brewster et al., 2024). These mixed findings, which span a decade in which there has been expansive innovation in remote technology use for individuals and health care, preclude a clear understanding of the association between proximity and mental health. This study advances our understanding by investigating two common mental health conditions, depression and anxiety, for adults who care for their parents while considering caregiving distance.

The stress process model and caregiving context

Our investigation of the role of caregiver proximity on caregiver mental health outcomes is theoretically guided by Pearlin et al. (1981, 1990) Stress Process Model. Pearlin’s model highlights chronic stress pathways in family caregiving arising from contextual factors (socioeconomic factors, family networks, caregiving history, and resource access), primary stressors of family caregiving, and secondary stressors. The secondary stressors arise from roles and activities beyond caregiving responsibilities (i.e., work and other family relationships) as well as individual level strains related to the loss of self-esteem or sense of mastery (Pearlin et al., 1981, 1990). In considering the role of caregiver proximity within the Stress Process Model, it can be mapped onto both contextual factors and primary stressors related to family caregiving, even if the direction of the relationship between proximity and caregiving related stress cannot be determined. For some family caregivers, the decision to live with, near, or far from the person receiving care is motivated by personal socioeconomic and health status, or relational concerns (i.e., greater experiences of stress may prompt a move to gain distance, and/or caregiving from a distance may lead to increased feelings of stress). Our investigation will help us understand these contextual factors more fully. Living with, near, or far from the person receiving care–in this case, their parent–may have both detrimental and beneficial impacts, and our question centers on the mental health impact of caregiving proximity.

Our investigation also aligns with Young et al.’s review of the social determinants of health and family caregiving, which integrates the Social Ecological Model with the realities of family caregiving, including contextual factors such as living with, near, or far from the care recipient, financial resources, and community resources (Bronfenbrenner, 1979; Young et al., 2020). Other levels of the model Young et al. describe include caregiver characteristics, caregiving activities, and care recipient characteristics. Our investigation addresses each level of this model and will help us understand more fully how caregiver characteristics, caregiving activities, care recipient characteristics, and contextual factors related to caregiver proximity may impact the mental health of family caregivers.

The aims of this study are (a) to describe caregiving activities provided by adult children caregivers to a representative sample of older adults by four levels of proximity to care recipient residence and (b) to examine whether caregiver proximity to their care recipient is associated with caregiver depression and anxiety. We hypothesize that long-distance caregivers will be more likely to have depression and anxiety compared to those living with or near their parents.

Method

Data sources and study population

This was a cross-sectional analysis of the 2021 National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS) Round 11 linked with the National Study of Caregiving IV (NSOC IV) (Freedman et al., 2022). NHATS samples Medicare beneficiaries age 65 and older, with older ages and Black individuals oversampled (Hu et al., 2023). The cohort for NHATS Round 11 is part of a longitudinal panel initiated in 2011 and replenished in 2015 to restore sample sizes by age and race lost due to mortality (Freedman & Kasper, 2019). NSOC caregivers are identified by NHATS participants as family or unpaid individuals who provide help with mobility, self-care, household activities, transportation, or medically oriented tasks. NSOC samples up to five eligible caregivers per NHATS participant. For NSOC IV, Round 11, 1,938 interviews with caregivers were completed by telephone and/or web, and the final response rate was 60.3% (Freedman et al., 2022). For this study, the analytic sample is comprised of adult-children (daughters, sons, children-in-law, and stepchildren) who are unpaid caregivers of living Medicare beneficiaries who, based on cohort start date, were ages 71 and older in 2021. Parent care recipients were living in the community or in residential care (not a nursing home).

Measures

Proximity of caregiver to care recipient residence

The NSOC survey asks caregivers how long it normally takes to travel to the care recipient’s home from where they live; continuous responses are provided in hours or minutes. For this study, proximity is defined by caregiver-reported travel time by any means of travel, separated into four categories: 0 min (co-reside), 1–20 min, 21–59 min, 1 hr or more. These categories were chosen based on practical significance (e.g., traveling 1–20 min is possible during a lunch break), cell size in each proximity category, and categories used in previous studies (Douglas et al., 2016; MetLife Mature Market Institute, 2004; Schoeni et al., 2022).

Caregiver characteristics

Caregiver sociodemographic variables included age (continuous), gender (female vs. male), a combined ethnicity/race variable (Black non-Hispanic; White non-Hispanic; Hispanic; and Other, which included those who marked more than one race or refused to answer), marital status (married/partnered vs. unmarried), and employment (employed or owns farm or business vs. not employed). Education was a three-category variable (≤12 years, 13–15, 16+) used because of the importance of education in intergenerational proximity, meaning the distance between parent and child residences (Choi et al., 2021; Schoeni et al., 2022). Income (continuous) in U.S. dollars for the prior calendar year was imputed using guidelines from the NSOC Technical Paper (Mengyao & Freedman, 2024).

Caregiver health status variables included self-rated general health (Excellent/Very Good/Good vs. Fair/Poor) and number of common chronic conditions (continuous). Caregiver chronic health conditions included heart attack, heart disease, high blood pressure, arthritis, osteoporosis, diabetes, lung disease, cancer, serious difficulty seeing and serious difficulty hearing.

Caregiving activities

Hours of care per week and duration of care provided in years are treated as continuous variables. Individual caregiving activity questions (yes/no) are derived from NSOC; one activity—“sitting in during visits to doctors”—is derived from a care-recipient question in NHATS. We grouped caregiving activities into categories: (a) health management (which included hands on daily help with special diet or exercises, care for teeth, feet, and skin care including wounds) (Liu et al., 2022; Wolff et al., 2016); (b) medication and medical tasks which included medication management (keeping track of medications, help taking shots or injections, help ordering prescribed medications) and managing medical tasks (ostomy care, IVs or testing blood); (c) health system logistics (help making medical appointments, changing/adding health insurance or prescription drug plan, help getting mobility devices, help making home safer by adding railing, grab bars, etc., help finding paid helper to do chores or personal care, intermittent care to arrange services or schedules); (d) patient advocacy (speak to medical providers about care recipient care, sit in with care recipients during their visits to doctors, view online medical information, coordinate care across more than one provider) (Ornstein et al., 2017; Wolff et al., 2016) and navigating complex health systems to advocate for and ensure timely, high-quality care. This category was in part previously referred to as “care coordination,” defined as “direct interaction with health care professionals”. We also included a novel category for (e) financial support (paying for medications or medical care, mobility devices [e.g., walker, cane, wheelchair], home safety equipment [e.g., railing, ramp, grab bars, seat for shower/tub, emergency call system], other assistive devices including things to help care recipient see, hear, reach, hold or pick up things, in home helpers), and separate categories for (f) ADL; help with personal care [e.g., eating, bathing, dressing, toileting], getting around home, and transportation), and (g) Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL; help with chores [e.g., laundry, cleaning, making hot meals], shopping, bills, and banking) not already included in other categories. The first four categories are consistent with prior approaches (Liu et al., 2022) noted. In our study, we added the financial support category and kept ADL and IADL activities as separate categories, based on the idea that these tasks may be particularly dependent on geographical proximity. For example, caregivers who co-reside or live close by may spend more time doing hands-on care like ADLs or IADLs, whereas those who live at a distance might more easily research paid help options, support logistics, view online medical information, or help pay for things like medications or mobility devices.

Care recipient characteristics

Care recipient variables included age, gender, the number of health conditions, homebound status, and functional status. Among the health conditions, probable dementia (dementia vs. possible/no dementia) was constructed based on NHATS technical paper guidance (Skehan & Spillman, 2013). Heart attack reflects care recipients who reported the health event in the past 12 months. Other health conditions included existing or new (within the past 12 months) diagnoses of heart disease, high blood pressure, arthritis, osteoporosis, diabetes, lung disease, stroke, and cancer. The number of health conditions is based on adding the total number of new or existing health conditions out of the ten displayed. Homebound status is informed by the Medicare Home Health Care Benefit definition of “homebound” (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, 2024) and was created by considering how often the care recipient goes outside their home, whether they need help leaving their home, and if they leave home alone but with great difficulty (Ornstein et al., 2015). We use two categories: Homebound (never/rarely leaves home), vs. “Not homebound” which included those who leave home but never by themselves, leaves home with help or has difficulty, and those who are fully independent. Decreased functional status is defined as having difficulty with two or more ADLs in the last month (eating, dressing, bathing, toileting) (Wolinsky et al., 2011).

Mental health outcomes

Caregiver-reported symptoms of depression and anxiety were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire for Depression and Anxiety (PHQ-4); caregivers screen “positive” for symptoms of either depression or anxiety when each two-question sub-scale of the PHQ-4 met or exceeded a cutoff score of 3 (Kroenke et al., 2009). Psychometric testing of the PHQ-4 shows good construct validity when comparing symptom severity with functional decline, and good internal reliability (Cronbach α > 0.80) (for more details about the psychometrics of the PHQ-4 see Kroenke et al. [2009]). In this study, we distinguish depression from anxiety because we recognize that each contributes uniquely to a persons’ functioning (Kroenke et al., 2009) and because other studies on distance caregivers have demonstrated differences in the significance and magnitude of depression and anxiety symptoms by proximity to the care recipient (Kindratt et al., 2023; Mazanec, 2012).

Analysis

After checking multicollinearity among caregiver and care recipient characteristics, bivariate associations between proximity groups and categorical variables were analyzed by Pearson’s χ2 test and continuous variables by linear regression models. We calculated the weighted proportion of caregivers who do individual care tasks. We calculated the sum and mean number of caregiving tasks by caregiver proximity.

Associations between caregiver proximity to care recipient and caregiver depression and anxiety, both scored as binary outcomes, were examined using multivariable logistic regression with robust standard errors. We built multivariable models based on our theoretical model that caregiver mental health is associated with residential proximity when adjusted for caregiver characteristics (Model 1), caregiving activities (Model 2), and care recipient characteristics (Model 3).

In the multivariable models, we excluded care recipient health conditions (heart disease, osteoporosis, and lung disease) which were moderately correlated with total number of care recipient health conditions (∣r∣ between 0.43–0.51). We used the total number of caregiving tasks per category in the multivariable analyses.

We included only complete cases in analysis. All analyses were done with StataNow/SE 18.5 using complex survey weighting consistent with the NSOC survey design, incorporating primary sampling units, strata, and sampling weights. All reported results provide estimates representative of the U.S. population, with 95% CI.

Results

The unweighted analytic sample included 938 adult child caregivers, representative of a population of 9,214,274 adult child caregivers in the U.S. The weighted proportions by proximity to the care recipient by any means of transportation included 26.3% of caregivers who co-resided (live in the same household, i.e., 0 min away), 47.4% lived 1–20 min away, 13.1% lived 21–59 min away, and 13.2% lived 1 hr or more away.

Caregiver characteristics by proximity

Caregiver demographics and general health status varied significantly by proximity to the care recipient’s residence (Table 1). Co-residing caregivers were younger, more likely to be Black or Hispanic, and had lower educational attainment and household income compared to those living farther away. They also reported poorer general health, with 28.1% rating their health as fair or poor, versus only 7.0% among those living an hour or more away. Caregivers living ≥1 hr away were more likely to be employed (63.7%) and have higher education (72.0% with 16+ years) and income levels (mean $126,449). Marital status also differed, with co-residing caregivers less likely to be married or partnered (41.0%) compared to those living 21–59 min away (84.4%). Despite these differences, gender and the average number of caregiver health conditions did not vary significantly by proximity.

Caregiving activities by proximity

Overall, the weighted sample provided 13.9 hr of care on average, with co-residing caregivers spending 29.2 hr per week, compared to those living at a distance who spend 7.4 hr on average per week (p < .001) (Table 2). Mean duration of care was 7 years, which did not vary significantly across groups. Examining the overall categories of caregiving activities, we found significant differences in the weighted percentage of caregivers who do at least one activity per category by residential proximity for health management (p = .002), medication and medical tasks (p < .001), ADLs (p < .001), and IADLs (p < .001) (Figure 1, Supplementary Table A). Specifically, co-residing caregivers more commonly help with special diets, dental, foot and skin care, tracking and ordering medications, and ADLs and IADLs such as transportation, chores and shopping compared to all other groups (Table 2).

Notably, there were not significant differences by residential proximity for activities related to financial support (p = .72), patient advocacy (p = .92) and health system logistics (p = .20) (Figure 1, Supplementary Table A). Those living at a distance, including those living an hour or more away, are just as commonly providing financial support, engaging in patient advocacy, and handling health systems logistics (Table 2).

Care recipient characteristics by proximity

There were no significant differences across caregiver proximity categories in care recipient characteristics like age, gender, total number of health conditions, and homebound status (Supplementary Table B). There were no significant differences in most health conditions including dementia; the exceptions were lung disease, stroke, and decreased functional status. For lung disease and decreased functional status, there were similar prevalence rates for co-resident caregivers and those living >1 hr away, with lower prevalence in the middle-distance caregivers. Care recipient stroke was most prevalent for caregivers living >1 hr away.

Caregiver mental health outcomes

Overall, 17.0% of the sample reported symptoms of depression, and 15.9% had symptoms of anxiety (Supplementary Table C). In the bivariate analysis, there were significant differences for depression (p = .04), with 25.7% of co-residing caregivers reporting depression compared to 12.8%–15.5% of caregivers who live further away. Although the proportion of anxiety for co-residing caregivers was highest at 22.4%, differences across proximity were not statistically significant (p = .14).

Multivariable logistic regression examining caregiver depression and anxiety by proximity

Across the three models, which adjusted for caregiver demographics and health (Model 1), caregiving activities (Model 2), and care recipient characteristics (Model 3), no significant association was found between proximity and depression (Table 3) or proximity and anxiety (Table 4). Across all adjusted models, caregiver characteristics (education, marital status, employment and general health) were stronger predictors of depression, while gender and general health were stronger predictors of anxiety.

Providing financial support was also a significant predictor of anxiety in Model 3 when controlling for caregiving characteristics, caregiving activities and care recipient characteristics (OR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.31–3.51, p = .003) (Table 4). Discussion

This analysis suggests that proximity does not play a significant role in the mental health of caregivers. In the unadjusted analysis, there is a significant difference between proximity groups for the outcome of depression but not for anxiety. However, in the adjusted analysis, we see no significant differences in either outcome by proximity group when controlling for caregiving characteristics, or when adding caregiving activities, or care recipient characteristics. This contrasts with previous studies that showed distance caregivers have higher rates of anxiety, while co-residing caregivers have higher rates of depression (Mazanec, 2012). Still, our findings demonstrate slightly lower rates of depression (17% vs 21.4%) and anxiety (15.9% vs 18.2%) compared to the general population during the same period (2021–2022). It is possible that caregiver characteristics which factor into decisions about how near or far away one lives from their parent—such as education, marital status, employment, and general health–may be better predictors of mental health outcomes than proximity alone, as suggested by our models. Future analysis should further test the role of these intersectional factors.

Importantly, this analysis provides a new understanding of how adult-child caregivers contribute to care, regardless of how far they live from of their parents. Long-distance caregivers, who live an hour or more away from the care recipient, provide similar levels of care in the area of health systems logistics, financial support, and patient advocacy as those who live closer, while co-residing caregivers take on a higher proportion of some key caregiving activities (e.g., health management, medication and medical tasks, ADLs, IADLs). This finding underscores that physical distance between caregivers and care recipients does not necessarily reduce the extent or nature of some types of caregiving activities, namely health system logistics, financial support, and patient advocacy. This highlights the need for support mechanisms that address the unique challenges faced by both local and long-distance caregivers.

Distance has the potential to complicate caregiving tasks, leading to uncertainty and challenges in ensuring adequate care and communicating with doctors and other health care providers from afar. Though communication quality cannot be examined with this dataset, this can be a challenge for all caregivers. Other factors may also influence caregivers’ mental health. One such factor is the caregiving role itself. As we find, distance caregivers often perform various types of tasks similar to those of proximate or co-residing caregivers. Other factors, like whether a distance caregiver is the primary/sole caregiver versus if there is another local caregiver who is also contributing, could affect the difficulties encountered by distance caregivers; though we did not examine this in our analysis (Bevan & Sparks, 2011). Additionally, the quality of the relationship (Enright et al., 2020) between the caregiver and the care recipient, as well as sociocultural norms of caregiving are crucial considerations, which cannot be captured by this dataset. A strong, positive relationship and a sense of filial piety (Pan et al., 2022) can mitigate the perceived burden, while a strained relationship and social expectations for children to provide care for their parents (Cagle & Munn, 2012) can exacerbate emotional distress regardless of physical proximity. Other situational and socioeconomic factors, such as financial strain, can also be associated with caregiver perceived burden and mental health. Caregivers with lower financial stability may have less flexibility to travel and access resources related to caregiving, which can increase care-related stress (MetLife Mature Market Institute, 2004). Future research should examine how caregiver proximity interacts with other factors that contribute to burden and emotional distress among those providing care from a distance.

Intergenerational proximity typically varies over the life course and is influenced by a number of factors including age, gender, marital status, education, employment, and race (Choi et al., 2021). Policies and programs supporting adult-child caregivers’ general health status and that address structural factors that create disparities (e.g., employment, income) are imperative to improve caregiver reported symptoms of depression and anxiety, regardless of proximity to their care recipient. Potential supports cross the social- ecological spectrum (Bronfenbrenner, 1979; Young et al., 2020) from federal and state policies to assessment and intervention by caregivers’ primary care providers. For example, some states offer paid family leave or paid sick leave policies which allows for employed caregivers to care for both care recipients and themselves without risk to job attachment or financial stability (Democratic Staff of the Joint Economic Committee United States Congress, n.d.; Pomeranz et al., 2022; Romig & Bryant, 2021; U.S. Department of Labor, 2026), and we advocate for this to be expanded across the U.S. One example of a comprehensive model of support for the veteran population and their caregivers in the U.S. is the Veteran Affairs’ Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregiver (PCAFC). This program offers caregiver education, training, and mental health counseling including access to telehealth therapy, along with a monthly stipend, access to health care benefits, certain legal and financial planning assistance and respite care (U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs, 2026). In the absence of a unified national health care system, health insurers and payors could consider whether providing benefits–such as training offered by the PCAFC to reduce caregiver burden –would support their mission while saving costs (Kaufman et al., 2026). Health systems could contribute by providing a systematic way to identify caregivers in electronic health records (Ma et al., 2022) and to assess their mental health needs (Ma et al., 2022); this is often seen in the literature as a strategy for hospitalized patients who are being discharged to reduce preventable hospital readmissions, but there would likely be positive impacts on the health of the caregivers as well. On an interpersonal level, clinicians can start by assessing caregiving roles and associated stressors (American Medical Association, 2018; Griffin et al., 2022; Riffin et al., 2020, 2021), and recommend supportive therapies or behavioral interventions to improve resilience, purpose-in-life and diverse social connections which have all been shown to be protective factors for caregiver mental health outcomes (Musich et al., 2023). In addition, the literature is full of evidenced-based interventions that may support the mental health of caregivers, and are often tailored for disease-specific challenges (see for example Cheng et al., 2019).

Limitations

There are additional limitations to our analysis beyond those noted above. First, the results are not causal and merely offer correlative evidence. Second, there may be some issues of endogeneity that are not captured by our models: e.g., care recipients with higher needs may have children who move closer to them (Schoeni et al., 2022). However, in both the bivariate and multivariable analysis, we did not see differences based on certain care recipient health conditions like dementia—though we did not differentiate between care recipients in different stages of dementia—which might have been expected based on copious literature showing increased stress for dementia caregivers compared to other conditions (Inanc, 2025). Future work may consider making use of instrumental variable approaches or other causal techniques.

There may also be differential effects on mental health for primary and secondary caregivers (e.g., as in the case of multiple siblings who share caregiving responsibilities) or based on the dyadic relationship (e.g., mother-daughter pairings). Future research should explore comparisons of caregiver mental health and proximity while controlling for whether they are caring solo or with others, and based on relational or gendered experiences. Third, because our measure of distance from the care recipient is captured as minutes traveled, we do not currently observe how different modes of travel (e.g., walking, driving, flying, etc.) influence the caregivers’ mental health outcomes. In many ways, this is in line with current literature, especially that which suggests a caregiver’s perception of distance is more important than the actual duration or mode of travel (Mazanec, 2012). Still, some health and income dimensions may be at play when considering the mode of travel; future work might consider the interaction between distance traveled and mode of transportation. Fourth, we note that some confidence intervals around race-related results are relatively wide. Sample size precluded subgroup analysis by race. We highlight several unobservable variables given the data at hand: we are unable, for instance, to observe within-family dynamics, which may complicate caregiving relationships. Finally, the distal measures of depression and anxiety may not be adequate to capture differences in caregiver stress and burden by distance.

Conclusion

Although adult-child caregivers who are co-residential report more depression than other adult-child caregivers, adjusted analyses did not show associations between caregiver proximity and depression or anxiety. We report new knowledge about the important contributions of long-distance caregivers, particularly when it comes to health systems logistics, financial support, and patient advocacy.
