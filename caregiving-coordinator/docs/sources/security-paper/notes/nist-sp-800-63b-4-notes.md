# Notes: NIST SP 800-63B-4, Authentication and Authenticator Management

- **Source:** Temoshok, D., Fenton, J. L., Choong, Y. Y., Lefkovitz, N., Regenscheid, A., Galluzzo, R., & Richer, J. P. (2025). *Digital identity guidelines: Authentication and authenticator management* (NIST Special Publication 800-63B-4). National Institute of Standards and Technology. https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-63B-4 (author order matches the "How to Cite" box in the front matter and the cover).
- **Label for the paper:** official government guidance (tier 1). Final, July 2025. It supersedes SP 800-63B (June 2017, updated 2020). Approved by the NIST Editorial Review Board on 2025-05-30. Companion to the main volume, see `nist-sp-800-63-4-notes.md` for the risk method and assurance-level ideas; this file does not repeat them.
- **Local copy:** `caregiving-coordinator/docs/sources/security-paper/nist-sp-800-63b-4/NIST.SP.800-63B-4.pdf` (129 PDF pages), the text extraction `63b4.txt` (same folder, made with pdftotext -layout), and NIST's status page `status.html` (same folder). The status page (checked 2026-11-18) says: "Date Published: July 2025"; final posted 07/31/25; supersedes SP 800-63B (03/02/2020); a planning note dated 10/06/2026 says an Implementation Resource Hub for SP 800-63-4 is available. It lists no newer edition and no withdrawal.
- **Page numbers below are the printed page numbers.** Front pages use Roman numerals. Printed page 1 is PDF page 12, so PDF page = printed page + 11. I checked this: PDF page 12 shows the "1. Introduction" heading with the footer "1", and PDF page 129 is printed page 118 (the last page).
- **Read in full (2026-11-18):** I read all of `63b4.txt` (5,549 lines) in order with the Read tool: cover and copyright pages, abstract, Table of Contents, list of tables and figures, Preface, Sections 1 to 8, References, and Appendices A to E (Appendix B is normative). I did not view the PDF page images. Figures 1, 2 and 3 are drawn images; the extracted text kept their labels and Figure 3's table of dots, and the surrounding prose describes Figures 1 and 2, so I used that text.
- **Scope:** the guidelines are written for government online services. The main volume says private-sector use is voluntary. This volume says the same in its front-matter Authority box (unnumbered page before printed p. i): it "may be used by nongovernmental organizations on a voluntary basis". Many rules below say "federal agencies SHALL". I name those cases so they are not read as rules for a private product.
- **Wording:** SHALL = required to conform; SHOULD = recommended; MAY = allowed (printed p. 2 to 3). Sections 2, 3, 4, 5 and Appendix B are normative; Sections 1, 6, 7, 8 and Appendices A, C, D, E are informative (p. 3).

## What the pages say (my words)

| What the pages say (my words) | Printed page |
|---|---|
| Authentication means the person proves they control the secret(s) bound to the account. The protections are meant to stop credential theft. They are not meant to stop a person who chooses to give away their own credentials, and NIST says there are very few technical controls that can spot or stop that willful sharing. | p. 1 |
| Three levels. AAL1 gives basic confidence and needs one factor, but NIST says services should offer multi-factor options. AAL2 needs two different factors and the service must offer a phishing-resistant option. AAL3 needs a phishing-resistant cryptographic authenticator with a key that cannot be exported. Step-up authentication can raise a session's level. | pp. 1 to 2 |
| **AAL1 (p. 4 to 6).** Allowed: password, look-up secret, out-of-band device, single-factor or multi-factor one-time password (OTP), single-factor or multi-factor cryptographic authenticator. A fixed overall time limit for reauthentication is required and "SHOULD be no more than 30 days"; an inactivity timeout is optional. | pp. 4 to 6 |
| **AAL2 (p. 6 to 7).** Use one multi-factor authenticator, or two single-factor ones that include one physical item ("something you have") plus a password or a biometric match. At least one authenticator must resist replay. Intent should be shown (SHOULD). The verifier must offer at least one phishing-resistant option and should encourage it. Reauthentication: overall limit should be no more than 24 hours; inactivity limit should be no more than 1 hour. After an inactivity timeout, a password or biometric plus the session secret may be enough to continue. | pp. 6 to 7 |
| **AAL3 (p. 8 to 9).** Needs a cryptographic authenticator with a non-exportable private key, phishing resistance, replay resistance and shown intent, plus a password or activation factor. The overall timeout must be no more than 12 hours; the inactivity timeout should be no more than 15 minutes; reauthentication is as strict as the first sign-in. Syncable authenticators SHALL NOT be used at AAL3 because their keys can be exported. | pp. 8 to 9 |
| Table 1 sums up the three levels: allowed authenticator types, reauthentication times (30 days / 24 hours with 1 hour idle / 12 hours with 15 minutes idle), and which of phishing resistance, replay resistance, intent and key exportability are required, recommended or banned. It is marked non-normative. | p. 11 |
| Anti-fraud signals (for example a sign-in from an unexpected place or from a cloud IP range) may be used at every level to add checks. They do not change the level or replace a factor. The provider must test them for effect and for harm to its users, and include them in its privacy risk assessment. | p. 4 |
| Federal agencies must use at least AAL2 when personal information is made available online (Executive Order 13681). This is a federal rule, not a rule for private products. | p. 4 |
| The verifier must use tailored security controls from the SP 800-53 moderate baseline "or an equivalent federal or industry standard". It must follow its records retention policy, or run a risk process and tell the subscriber the retention policy if it keeps records by choice. | p. 9 |
| Redress: the service must give people an easy-to-find way to report problems with signing in, and must check that it works. | p. 10 |
| Shared devices. People often share a device used for authentication, such as a family phone that receives codes. In public-facing apps, providers SHOULD NOT block one device from being registered by several people, but MAY cap how many accounts one device can serve to prevent large-scale fraud. | p. 12 |
| **Passwords: length.** A password used alone must be at least 15 characters (SHALL). A password used only as one part of multi-factor sign-in may be shorter but at least 8 (SHALL). Verifiers SHOULD allow a maximum of at least 64 characters. | p. 14 |
| **Passwords: characters.** Verifiers SHOULD accept all printing ASCII characters and the space, and SHOULD accept Unicode; each Unicode code point counts as one character. If Unicode is accepted, SHOULD normalize it with NFC before hashing. The whole submitted password must be checked, never cut short. | pp. 14 to 15 |
| **Passwords: no composition rules, no forced rotation.** Verifiers SHALL NOT impose other composition rules (such as mixing character types). They SHALL NOT require people to change passwords on a schedule. They SHALL force a change if there is evidence of compromise. Hints that an unauthenticated person can see, and security questions (knowledge-based authentication), are banned. | p. 14 (and p. 15 for the full-password rule) |
| **Passwords: blocklist.** When a password is set or changed, the verifier SHALL compare the whole password to a blocklist of commonly used, expected or compromised values (breach lists, dictionary words, context words such as the service name and username). If found, the person must pick another and must be told why. The list should be big enough to catch likely guesses but NIST says an excessively large list adds little, because online guessing is already throttled. The verifier must also give guidance on choosing a strong password. | p. 15 |
| **Passwords: managers and usability.** Verifiers SHALL allow password managers and autofill, and SHOULD allow paste. They SHOULD offer an option to show the password while it is typed. They MAY make small allowances for typing slips, such as trimming leading and trailing spaces. | pp. 15 to 16 |
| **Passwords: storage.** Passwords SHALL be salted and hashed with a suitable password hashing scheme (salt at least 32 bits). The cost setting should be as high as practical and raised over time. Verifiers SHOULD add a keyed hash with a secret key stored apart from the hashes, ideally in a hardware-protected area. Passwords are not phishing-resistant. | pp. 13, 16 to 17 |
| **Rate limiting.** The verifier must limit consecutive failed attempts for an authenticator on one account to no more than 100, by disabling that authenticator (stricter limits allowed). A disabled authenticator must be re-bound. Ways to reduce lockout abuse: a bot-detection challenge, waits that grow with each failure (for example 30 seconds up to an hour), and risk-based checks. After a successful sign-in the verifier SHOULD reset the count. | pp. 30 to 31 |
| Look-up secrets (such as saved recovery code lists): random, at least 6 decimal digits (or equivalent), each used once, stored hashed. | pp. 17 to 18 |
| Out-of-band (a code or approval sent to a separate device): the person must type the secret across the channels. The old "tap approve" method is no longer acceptable because of authentication-fatigue attacks. Codes must expire in 10 minutes and be used once. Email SHALL NOT be used for out-of-band authentication. Confirmation codes sent to verify an email address, and recovery codes, are not affected by that ban. Text or voice (the phone network, PSTN) is a "restricted" authenticator: the service must offer an alternative, give plain notice of the risk, and have a migration plan. Providers SHOULD limit push notifications. | pp. 19 to 23, 37 to 38 |
| One-time passwords (OTP, for example an authenticator app code) and cryptographic authenticators are described. OTP is not phishing-resistant. A time-based OTP value changes at least every two minutes. | pp. 23 to 26 |
| **Phishing resistance.** Means the protocol stops secrets from reaching a fake site without relying on the person being careful. It needs cryptographic authentication. Codes typed by hand (out-of-band, OTP) are never phishing-resistant, because a fake site can pass them on. Two methods count: channel binding and verifier name binding. WebAuthn (the standard behind passkeys and FIDO2) is the named example of verifier name binding. | pp. 35 to 36 |
| **Multi-factor.** A multi-factor authenticator is "something you have" unlocked by "something you know" or "something you are". Each use needs the unlock step. A biometric alone is not an authenticator: it must be paired with a physical authenticator, an alternative non-biometric option must always be offered, and voice comparison SHALL NOT be used. Biometric data must be treated as sensitive personal information. Face recognition must have presentation attack detection. | pp. 6 to 7, 31 to 33 |
| Activation secrets (the local PIN that unlocks an authenticator): at least 4 characters, SHOULD be at least 6, with at most 10 failed tries before the authenticator is disabled. | p. 38 |
| **Syncable authenticators / passkeys (Appendix B, normative).** Syncable authenticators copy their keys to a sync service (such as a cloud account) and on to other devices. Keys in the sync service must be encrypted, should be encrypted with a secret the user controls, must be reachable only by the signed-in user, and access to the sync service must be protected by sign-in equal to AAL2. Private-key operations must happen on the local device. Authenticators SHOULD show users which services have synced keys and where. They can be used up to AAL2 but not AAL3. For public apps, the service should not refuse a key because it is synced, and should not demand attestation, because that would push people to weaker options. | pp. 91 to 94 |
| Sharing syncable keys. NIST says historical advice was to never share authenticators, but sharing happens, and some syncable authenticators now have built-in sharing. Public-facing services "should assume that all syncable authenticators may be subject to sharing". NIST adds that any authenticator, including a password, can be shared by someone determined to share it. | p. 94 to 95 |
| WebAuthn flags in Appendix B: User Present (SHOULD be checked), User Verified (verifier SHALL ask for it and inspect it; if not verified, treat the key as single-factor), Backup Eligible and Backup State (may be used for policy; do not refuse access on Backup State in public apps). | p. 93 |
| Syncable-key risks (Table 5): keys shared to other people's devices, a hacked sync service, weak cloud recovery, and the difficulty of centrally revoking keys that are tied to each service. Mitigations include showing users their keys, AAL2 to add a new authenticator, notifying users of recovery, and a user-controlled secret to encrypt keys. | pp. 96 to 98 |
| **Adding authenticators.** The provider SHOULD encourage at least two separate ways to sign in to reduce recovery needs, and SHALL allow several authenticators per account. Adding one needs sign-in at the lower of the account's top level and the new authenticator's level. The person must be told through a separate channel when one is added. | p. 44 |
| Binding an authenticator from another device uses a one-time binding code: at least 40 bits with an entered identifier, otherwise at least 112 bits; valid at most 10 minutes; never sent over email. The provider must give clear steps for a binding mistake. | p. 45 |
| **Recovery.** Four kinds are recognized: saved recovery codes, issued recovery codes, recovery contacts, and repeated identity proofing. The service must support at least one and base any extra method on a documented risk analysis. Replacing a forgotten password when the person can still sign in another way is not "recovery", it is adding a new authenticator. Recovery is expected to be less convenient than signing in and may involve waits. Every recovery sends a notification. | pp. 47 to 50 |
| Saved recovery code: at least 64 random bits, stored hashed, throttled, replaced after use. Issued code: at least 6 decimal digits (or equivalent); valid at most 21 days by post in the contiguous US, 30 days by post elsewhere, 10 minutes by text or voice, 24 hours by email; new recovery addresses must be verified; the person must be able to set at least two. | pp. 47 to 48 |
| Recovery contacts (a trusted person who receives the code): the service must let the person view and manage them and SHOULD send a yearly reminder to review the list. Codes sent to them can stay valid 24 hours longer so the contact can pass them on. | pp. 48 to 49 |
| Recovery by level: without identity proofing (AAL1 accounts), only a saved code, issued code or recovery contact. At AAL2: two codes from different methods, or one code plus a single-factor authenticator on the account, or repeated identity proofing. | pp. 49 to 50 |
| Lost or stolen authenticators must be suspended, invalidated or destroyed promptly. The service SHOULD offer a backup way to report a loss. Authenticators must be invalidated when the account ends, when the person asks, or when compromised. NIST says wrongly invalidating one costs less than leaving a compromised one working. | pp. 50 to 51 |
| **Account notifications.** Events such as adding an authenticator or recovery SHALL send a notice to the addresses on the account. At least two notification addresses must be supported. The notice must say what to do if the person did not do it. | pp. 51 to 52 |
| **Session management.** Sessions continue by a secret issued after sign-in. NIST says sessions beat re-asking for credentials because poor usability leads to workarounds. Session secrets: random from an approved generator, at least 64 bits, erased at logout, sent only over a protected channel, not exposed to intermediaries, time out per the level. They SHOULD NOT be stored in HTML5 Local Storage because of cross-site scripting (XSS). A session SHALL NOT fall back from https to http. Requests that change data SHALL carry a session identifier checked against cross-site request forgery (CSRF). Services SHOULD give an easy log-off. "Remember my browser" features must not replace authentication, except for the AAL2 reauthentication case. | pp. 53 to 55 |
| **Cookies for sessions.** SHALL be HTTPS-only and limited to the fewest hostnames and paths. SHOULD be HttpOnly, use the "__Host-" prefix with Path=/, set SameSite Lax or Strict, expire near the session's end, and hold only an opaque identifier, never plain personal information (that last point is SHALL NOT for cleartext personal information). Expiry flags must not be relied on to enforce timeouts. | p. 55 |
| Access tokens (for example OAuth) may stay valid long after the person has left, so the service SHALL NOT treat one as proof the person is present. | p. 55 |
| **Reauthentication.** Required periodically to confirm the person is still there. Two timeouts: overall and inactivity. When either expires, the session SHALL end. The service MAY warn first. Limits depend on level, setting, device and the app. In a federated sign-in, the relying party is the one that decides if reauthentication is met. | pp. 56 to 57 |
| **Session monitoring** (watching for fraud during a session, for example by usage pattern, device or location) is optional. It has privacy effects and must be part of the privacy risk assessment. | p. 57 |
| Threats table (Table 3) lists theft, duplication, eavesdropping, offline cracking, phishing, social engineering, authentication fatigue, online guessing, endpoint compromise, unauthorized binding, latent keys on old devices, proliferation of keys, insecure key transfer and insider threats. Table 4 maps each to controls. One insider example is a customer-support person who colludes with an attacker. | pp. 58 to 64 |
| Recovery is called "the weak point in many authentication mechanisms". One factor must not be usable to get a different kind of factor (a password must not be enough to get a new list of look-up secrets). Human-assisted recovery adds social engineering risk. | p. 65 |
| **Privacy.** Authentication rules and optional session monitoring require a privacy risk assessment for records retention. If the provider uses attributes for non-identity purposes, it must keep use predictable and manageable (notice, consent, selective disclosure). If it asks for consent, consent for the extra use SHALL NOT be a condition of the sign-in service. Biometric data held centrally raises privacy concerns. The Privacy Act and PIA parts are federal-agency rules. | pp. 9 to 10, 31 to 33, 57, 66 to 67 |
| **Usability.** Authentication should be "easy to do the right thing, hard to do the wrong thing, and easy to recover". Poor usability causes workarounds that weaken controls. Offer several authenticator types and let people choose. Use plain language. Show typed text on request. Give at least 10 entry attempts for manually typed outputs. Tell people how many tries are left and how long to wait. Warn before timeouts (for example two minutes before). Support autofill and paste. Do not use padlock-style icons that look like browser security signs. | pp. 68 to 72 |
| Customer success: a single authenticator type will not suit everyone. NIST lists mismatches: no phone signal for SMS, small codes hard to read for low vision, voice codes hard for hearing loss, hardware cost, dexterity problems, intellectual or learning difficulties, older devices, and less technical skill. Services must give people a way to report problems and suggest alternatives. | pp. 79 to 80 |
| Appendix A explains why: length matters most, composition rules make people choose predictable passwords ("Password1!"), blocklists plus hashing plus rate limiting work better, and phishing, keylogging and social engineering ignore password strength. | pp. 87 to 90 |
| Change log: "memorized secrets" became "passwords"; single-factor password minimum raised; the approve-by-tap out-of-band method disallowed; syncable authenticator and wallet rules added; phishing resistance defined; recovery rules and notification rules revised; cookie rules and session monitoring added. | pp. 117 to 118 |

## Useful for the paper

Topic numbers follow the labels in my task brief (topic 7 = best practices and tools, topic 8 = application to the proposed caregiving coordinator). I did not re-check the paper's topic list.

- **Topic 7 (best practices):** this volume is the detailed source behind password and sign-in advice. Points the paper can cite with page numbers: at least 15 characters for a password used alone, or at least 8 when it is one part of multi-factor (p. 14); no composition rules and no scheduled changes (p. 14); check against a blocklist and say why a password is rejected (p. 15); allow password managers and paste (p. 15); salted, hashed storage (p. 16); rate limiting with a cap of 100 failed tries per authenticator (p. 30); phishing-resistant options and why typed codes are not phishing-resistant (pp. 35 to 36); no email for out-of-band codes (p. 20); recovery as the weak point (p. 65).
- **Topic 7, sessions:** the cookie list (p. 55), 64-bit random session secrets, no Local Storage for session secrets, CSRF identifier, no http fallback (pp. 53 to 54), and the idea of short overall and inactivity timeouts (pp. 5 to 9, 56).
- **Topic 8 (application, as a plan only):** (a) the proposed coordinator's sign-in could be planned around password plus a second factor and a phishing-resistant option such as a passkey; the paper must say this is a plan to evaluate, not something built. (b) The "shared credentials" risk is addressed head on: NIST says the controls cannot stop willful sharing (p. 1), that shared family devices exist and should not be blocked by default (p. 12), and that syncable keys may be shared (pp. 94 to 95). This supports a design in which each caregiver and family member has their own account and invitation, instead of one shared login. The text does not say that outright; it is my inference and should be labelled as such. (c) Recovery matters for caregivers who lose a phone: saved codes, a second authenticator, a recovery contact, and notifications on every change (pp. 44, 47 to 52). A recovery contact could be a trusted family member, but the proposed coordinator would need to decide this. (d) Usability and customer success (pp. 68 to 80) fit users who are stressed, older or have limited tech skill.
- **Topic 6 (what teams do):** support staff are named as an insider and social engineering risk (pp. 61, 64 to 65); redress and plain-language help are part of the job (pp. 10, 79 to 80).
- **Topic 4 and 5:** the "authenticator lifecycle" (bind, maintain, recover, invalidate, expire) on pp. 43 to 52 is a clear way to show what authentication work includes beyond a login screen.

## Conflicts with older sources

- **OWASP Proactive Controls 2024, C7 (my notes in `owasp-proactive-controls-2024-notes.md`, lines 21 and 33 to 34):**
  - *Rotation:* C7 lists "Enforce password rotation." SP 800-63B-4 says verifiers SHALL NOT require periodic password changes and SHALL force a change only on evidence of compromise (p. 14). The two disagree. SP 800-63B-4 is newer (July 2025) and a formal standard; C7 is older.
  - *Length:* my C7 notes say it asks for at least 8 characters with multi-factor and at least 10 without. SP 800-63B-4 sets at least 8 as one part of multi-factor and at least 15 when the password is used alone (p. 14). So the "without multi-factor" numbers disagree (10 versus 15). I did not re-read C7 itself; I rely on the existing notes file for what C7 says.
- **OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 (my notes in `owasp-asvs-5-0-0-notes.md`):**
  - *6.2.10* says a password stays valid until found compromised or changed, with no periodic rotation (existing notes, p. 46 of ASVS). This matches SP 800-63B-4 p. 14. No conflict.
  - *6.2.5* (no composition rules) matches p. 14. *6.2.9* (at least 64 characters allowed) matches "SHOULD permit at least 64" on p. 14.
  - *6.2.1* (at least 8, with at least 15 strongly recommended) is looser than NIST's "SHALL be at least 15" for a single-factor password but is consistent with NIST's 8 for the multi-factor case. The paper should say which standard it is quoting.
  - ASVS notes that NIST treats email as not acceptable for authentication. SP 800-63B-4 says email SHALL NOT be used for out-of-band authentication (p. 20) but confirmation and recovery codes sent by email are allowed (p. 20, and recovery on p. 48). The paper should not say "email is banned from recovery".
- **Main volume (`nist-sp-800-63-4-notes.md`):** no conflict. That file warned not to use it for password rules; this volume now supplies them.
- **Change from the earlier edition:** the earlier SP 800-63B-3 required AAL3 hardware authenticators. This version requires non-exportable keys instead (p. 117 change log). I did not read 63B-3, so I rely on the change log for that point.

## Limits and cautions

- Written for government services. Many requirements (FIPS 140 validation, SAOP review, Privacy Act, FISMA moderate sync services, mobile device management) apply to federal agencies or "federal enterprise" systems only. The text says federal enterprise does not cover government-to-consumer or public-facing use (pp. 34, 92). A private product is not bound by any of it. Citing it supports "following published guidance" and does not support "compliant".
- It sets rules for the party that runs authentication (a credential service provider). It does not say how Supabase Auth or any other product behaves. I did not read any Supabase or Vercel document, so the paper must not claim the stack meets any rule here.
- AAL choice comes from the risk process in the main volume. This volume does not say which level a caregiving product needs, and it does not discuss caregiving or health data.
- Rules for biometrics, hardware authenticators, wireless pairing and PIV cards (pp. 31 to 34, 38 to 42) have little bearing on a web app and were read but not used in the table beyond a short mention.
- The 15-character rule is a requirement of the document. NIST gives Appendix A as the reason, but a paper should avoid saying 15 characters makes passwords "safe".
- NIST says controls cannot prevent willful credential sharing (p. 1). A caregiving product that expects shared access cannot rely on authentication rules alone to solve it.
- Page citations are printed page numbers; some table rows span two pages and are marked with ranges.
