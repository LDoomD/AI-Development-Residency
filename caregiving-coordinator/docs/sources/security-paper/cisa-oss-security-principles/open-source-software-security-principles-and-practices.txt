       Introduction
       Today’s software is largely made up of existing components that have been hewn together, rather than
       built from scratch. This approach to software development improves efficiency by reducing the need to
       recreate existing functionalities and processes for each new product. Many of the components that
       software producers elect to use (rather than developing their own versions) are open source software (OSS)
       components. Modern software systems heavily rely on OSS, 1 and all U.S. federal agencies have OSS in
       their systems and networks.
       OSS has many advantages, including visibility into the code base, flexibility that enables customization,
       lower costs driven by the absence of licensing fees, and dynamic communities of developers collaborating
       on improvements. These advantages give downstream software consumers (organizations that use OSS
       components) greater control over their software. For example, instead of submitting feature requests to an
       external organization or relying on service agreements for bug and vulnerability fixes, software consumers
       can implement changes themselves. This direct access to the software means that OSS consumers are
       responsible for managing security updates and other support throughout the software lifecycle. Agencies
       should adjust their processes for acquiring, developing, and maintaining software to benefit from OSS
       while using it securely and sustainably.
       Executive Order 14144, “Strengthening and Promoting Innovation in the Nation’s Cybersecurity,” highlights
       the benefits of OSS for federal agencies. Executive Order 14306, “Sustaining Select Efforts To Strengthen
       the Nation’s Cybersecurity and Amending Executive Order 13964 and Executive Order 14144" reinforces
       the need to secure federal networks. CISA developed this guidance on secure OSS practices to fulfill its
       responsibilities under 6 U.S.C. § 652 and to align with the direction established in Executive Orders 14144
       and 14306.
       This guidance outlines considerations and best practices for agencies to engage with OSS securely,
       responsibly, and sustainably. OSS is software; therefore, established best practices for software security
       apply. 2 Recommendations in this guidance align with existing best practices for secure software
       development and operation and address OSS-specific matters, such as contributing to, and publishing,
       OSS projects.

       What Is Open Source Software?
       OSS is software that anyone can access, use, modify, and share. 3 OSS is distinguished from proprietary
       software by its permissive licensing, which allows for free viewing, use, modification, and distribution of the
       source code. Agencies can identify OSS by its associated license—see the Open Source Initiative’s list of
       open source licenses. 4 Generally, OSS is developed through open source development practices;
       individuals collaborate to develop and update OSS through projects hosted in online repositories where
       they propose updates, fix issues, and discuss improvements. Within these projects, some individuals act as
       maintainers, facilitating the project’s development by approving suggestions and merging changes to the
       codebase. 5 Others act as contributors, submitting suggestions to OSS projects and engaging in discussions
       on issues and updates. Contributors may participate regularly or sporadically. Together, maintainers and
       contributors form the community that drives the development of an OSS project. Many individuals active in
       OSS communities operate anonymously or using pseudonyms (under a username rather than their legal
       name). Organizations around the world—from small businesses to large multinational corporations—also
       take part in OSS communities. Software developed through closed development practices can later
       become open source software, but software developed in this way may lack the visible development history
       that open source development practices generate.
       OSS projects do not follow a single “typical” architecture or design strategy. Some OSS projects, such as
       the Apache HTTP Server Project or the Linux Kernel, have a well-established history, a robust community
       supporting them, and established processes and policies that guide how maintainers, contributors, and
       software consumers interact with the project. On the other extreme are abandoned projects, such as those
       students created for school assignments and never revisited. In addition, some projects have thousands of
       downstream software consumers, while others have only a handful. Openness is the commonality across
       OSS projects.

       Benefits of Open Source Software
       Federal agencies are responsible for executing complex and critical missions and delivering critical
       services to the public. Agencies can use OSS to meet mission needs and realize the following benefits:
              Security through transparency: Open code allows independent researchers and agencies to verify
               security properties directly rather than trusting vendor attestations.

       Open Source Software Considerations for Supply Chain Risk Management
       Although many security practices used for proprietary software also apply to OSS, failing to account for key
       differences between OSS and proprietary software can create inefficiencies and overlook OSS-specific
       risks. OSS has several unique characteristics that require agencies to modify software practices, including:
              Community-driven software development: Individuals from around the world collaborate to develop
               and improve OSS.
              Transparency: Software consumers can view the product codebase and other project metadata.
              Individual anonymity: Online repository users can act as maintainers and contributors for OSS
               projects using an alias or pseudonym.
              Customization: Access to the source code allows software consumers to modify the software
               directly.
       These characteristics present trade-offs that have implications for how agencies produce, choose, and
       consume OSS. To navigate these trade-offs, agencies should strategically adapt their software practices to
       take advantage of OSS while mitigating its risks.
       For example, the community-driven software development model means agencies can help shape the
       software and benefit from improvements contributed by other experts and practitioners. However, this
       advantage comes with a trade-off: No single entity is responsible for pushing feature upgrades or security
       patches. Agencies may assign staff or contract with a third party to handle security patches and feature
       upgrades, but independent OSS project maintainers do not have an obligation to provide updates or
       patches.


       Using Open Source Software Solutions
       Agencies use software to improve capacity and efficiency, enabling them to better fulfill their mission.
       Despite these benefits, agencies must consider risks when selecting software solutions and manage them
       throughout the software lifecycle. The cybersecurity principles that drive risk management for proprietary
       software are equally relevant to OSS. However, agencies must adapt their implementation to address the
       unique characteristics of OSS.
       With proprietary software, the software producer is responsible for developing and distributing security
       patches. In contrast, OSS project maintainers are not the equivalent of a software producer. Updates to the
       project—both feature-related and security-related—come from best-effort open source development
       processes, and it is up to downstream users to ensure their dependencies are up to date. By embedding

       OSS-specific considerations into every stage of their software management processes, agencies can fully

       Select Software Solutions
       Federal agencies face varied, sometimes niche, capability needs to meet mission goals and deliver critical
       services to the public. To fill capability gaps, agencies can assess available software, acquire new software
       solutions, or initiate a project to deliver custom-developed code. 6 The community-driven, decentralized
       development processes inherent in OSS-generated components produce a wide variety of software that
       meets unique and complex requirements. Rather than purchase a proprietary software solution to meet a
       need, agencies may find that there is an existing, suitable OSS solution. 7 By considering OSS—in addition
       to proprietary software—when considering possible software solutions, agencies can lower costs while
       maintaining high standards for performance quality.
       Agencies should establish a process to review and approve OSS that supports staff in using solutions that
       best meet their needs while still managing risks. Standardizing this process enables agencies to use
       automated software tools to support human-driven analysis. The level of review required should reflect
       organizational risk-acceptance posture. For example, an agency’s Office of the Chief Information Officer
       (OCIO) may delegate approvals for software libraries (collections of pre-developed software components) to
       the program level, while retaining approval authority for higher-risk components, such as an operating
       system. Review requirements may also vary based on potential risk exposure. For example, automated
       tools may suffice to review a software library, but the agency may require more in-depth analysis and
       documentation for an operating system.

       Evaluate Trustworthiness
       Before approving an OSS component for use in agency software, the agency should evaluate the
       trustworthiness of the OSS project. This evaluation helps the agency consider relevant risk factors and
       determine that the project meets its risk tolerance. See the C4 Framework in Appendix A for how to
       evaluate OSS project trustworthiness. Agencies should tailor the criteria for trustworthiness to their mission
       areas and to each software development project, as risks and risk tolerance may vary. Agencies can
       identify a minimum set of risk considerations that all software development projects must use.
       Agencies should use automated tools to assist in the evaluation of OSS components. These tools take
       advantage of the open source development process characteristic of OSS projects and draw insights about
       risks from the project metadata. Examples of these tools include Hipcheck and CHAOSS.
       Agencies should rigorously evaluate the trustworthiness of OSS components both before adoption and
       throughout the time the component is in use. Ongoing re-evaluations are essential to maintaining strong
       software security practices and ensuring vigilance against emerging risks. Agencies should determine a

       Review License for Compliance
       As part of the OSS component evaluation process, agencies should consult legal counsel to confirm that
       the component’s license permits the agency’s intended use. Agency legal counsel may provide additional
       guidance on standardizing the process for obtaining the necessary legal reviews and approvals.

       Monitor Risk
       No software is risk-free. Agencies should continuously monitor their software for novel risk information,
       such as novel vulnerabilities or end of support. 8 Asset management is critical for effectively monitoring
       risks, as it enables agencies to understand what new risk information is relevant to them. 9 For example,
       the Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) Program disseminates information about software
       vulnerabilities. If an agency knows what software is on their networks, they can determine whether (and
       how) they must respond to a novel CVE. Security assessments can reveal additional risk information.

       Track Open Source Software in Agency Asset Inventories
       As with all software, agencies should track OSS in their asset management repositories. An up-to-date
       asset inventory enhances risk management by giving agencies a clear view of which software components
       are on their networks and where those components are located. Agencies can use asset inventories to
       prioritize risk management resources when responding to incidents. For example, if multiple software
       components have the same dependency, responding to new risk information for that dependency would be
       higher priority.
       Participating agencies can use CISA’s Continuous Diagnostics and Mitigation (CDM) Program tools,
       integration services, and dashboards to improve their respective security postures. The program
       summarizes data from endpoint device scans in agency-level dashboards that produce customized reports
       that alert IT managers to cybersecurity risks.
       Exploits like log4shell 10 and xz utils 11 underscore the need for agencies to understand the dependencies
       embedded within their software components. Software bills of materials (SBOMs) can illuminate an
       organization’s software supply chain, enabling them to know not only what components are deployed but
       the dependencies of those components. For proprietary software, agencies can request an SBOM directly
       from the software producer. Agencies can generate an SBOM for OSS using SBOM tools if needed.
       Agencies can reference CISA’s SBOM resources for additional guidance.

       Regularly Assess Security of Open Source Software Components
       Agency security assessments should be able to identify issues in both OSS and proprietary components.
       Regular security assessments of software systems are one of the ways agencies can discover suspicious
       behavior from deployed software components. When coupled with asset management best practices,
       security assessments can help agencies quickly target specific software components and reduce the
       system’s overall risk. Automated tools, including artificial intelligence (AI) tools, can support agency security
       assessments, such as security analysis of source code. One challenge for agencies remains obtaining
       sufficient visibility into relevant software supply chains to identify and monitor dependencies.
       As the lead U.S. cybersecurity agency, CISA supports federal agencies in securing their networks and
       reducing risk to critical software systems. CISA’s Critical Infrastructure Assessments and Cyber Hygiene
       Services can be additional resources for agencies in conducting security assessments on their networks
       and software systems. Aligned with but separate from those services, CISA also scans and analyzes public
       OSS repositories maintained by federal agencies for vulnerabilities and then reports the findings to the
       agency.
       OSS’s open source development practices offer agencies additional insight into the security of a
       component. By regularly evaluating OSS projects as described above in the Evaluate Trustworthiness
       section and following asset management best practices, agencies can monitor changes to characteristics
       of interest, such as CVEs, or changes to the number of maintainers.

       Follow Established Patching Principles
       Agencies should follow established principles for reporting vulnerabilities and implementing fixes, with the
       understanding that some processes will need to be adapted for OSS. The CVE Program and the Known
       Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) Catalog apply to OSS as well as proprietary software. However, OSS project
       maintainers are not the OSS equivalent to the software producers of proprietary software. Agencies can
       expect or request patches from proprietary software producers, but, for OSS, agencies should expect to
       collaborate with OSS communities to develop patches and fixes and be prepared to implement the patches
       for custom-developed software.
       In the proprietary software model, software modifications flow in one direction: downstream from the
       software producer to the software consumer. In the OSS model, changes to the software can flow in both
       directions: upstream from the software consumer to the OSS project and downstream from the OSS project
       to the software consumer. In general, agencies should apply the following two principles in approaching
       fixes or patches for OSS: (1) collaborate, don’t demand; (2) push fixes upstream. See Contributing to OSS
       Projects below for additional details on pushing fixes upstream.

       When an Open Source Software Project Issues a Patch
       In active OSS projects, maintainers and contributors can collaborate to develop a patch for newly reported
       vulnerabilities. Once the maintainers have merged the update into the codebase, any user should be able
       to update their software to implement the patch. For custom-developed software, agencies (or their
       contractors) are responsible for implementing patches and validating patch compatibility. The validation
       step is necessary to confirm the updated version of the component with the patch does not break
       downstream functionalities.

       When There Is New Vulnerability Information but No Available Patch
       If an agency becomes aware of a new vulnerability in OSS that does not have a CVE identifier (CVE ID), the
       agency should follow coordinated vulnerability disclosure procedures under CISA’s Coordinated
       Vulnerability Disclosure Program. These procedures allow CISA to analyze the vulnerability report;
       determine whether it requires a CVE ID; engage with relevant stakeholders, including project maintainers,
       to respond; and publicly disclose the vulnerability and any available mitigations.
       If an agency discovers a vulnerability in OSS with an associated CVE ID, the agency should follow the
       project’s vulnerability disclosure policy (VDP) or security policy, if available. A project’s VDP or security
       policy outlines how to notify maintainers of a vulnerability and what information to provide. In providing the
       information requested by a project’s VDP or security policy, agencies should follow relevant guidance and
       privacy checks to protect sensitive data. If a project does not have a published VDP, agencies should
       contact the maintainer(s) to report the vulnerability and propose a fix, if possible. Security contact
       information may be in a SECURITY.md file. If no security contact information is available or there is no
       response, agencies should consider developing, implementing, and submitting their own fix upstream.
       Lack of response to new vulnerability information from project maintainers should trigger a re-evaluation of
       OSS component use. Unresponsive maintainers may indicate that the OSS project is end-of-support. 12 A
       component’s security support status (whether someone is actively monitoring and responding to new risk
       information) is a key factor in whether a software component is trustworthy. Patches for vulnerabilities are
       critical for maintaining software security. If re-evaluation of the OSS component, according to the process
       outlined above in the Select Software Solutions section, finds the component to no longer be suitable, the
       agency should replace it with an alternative component. As a last resort, agencies may take on
       maintenance of a fork of the component until a suitable alternative is identified.

       Patch Management at Scale
       In 2026, the best available automation, including AI frontier models, has significantly increased the volume
       of discovered vulnerabilities and the speed of patch development. To benefit from the faster patch
       development and mitigate the impact of the newly discovered vulnerabilities, agencies should automate
       dependency management and patch management workflows. Automated workflows for applying and
       testing patches can be a part of a continuous integration/continuous delivery (CI/CD) pipeline. 13 Such
       workflows can also automatically generate artifacts, such as SBOMs, that can further support software risk
       management.

       Contributing to Open Source Software Projects
       The collaborative nature of the open source development process allows agencies to engage directly in the
       development of the software. By contributing to OSS projects, agencies can flag security issues and
       propose updates. Contributing fixes upstream not only provides value back to the public by making the fix
       publicly available but also lowers costs for other agencies by reducing duplication of efforts to develop a
       security fix.
       Agencies should contribute to the OSS projects they use. Doing so supports OSS communities and makes
       government-funded improvements available to the public and other federal agencies. When agencies
       contribute changes upstream to the project, those changes are available to everyone. Agencies can use the
       following practices to incentivize OSS contributions:
               Note contributions to OSS projects in Contractor Performance Assessment Reports (CPAR) for
                software development activities.
               Recognize outstanding engineering by agency developers’ efforts through excellence awards.
               Include upstream deliverables as part of documented goals, plans, and performance measures for
                programs and for individuals, where appropriate.
       Contributions to OSS projects are public and thus require internal agency review before publication.
       Agencies should establish review processes that adhere to agency standards for public content review and
       that are sensitive to an OSS-specific context. Contributions may be high volume with quick turnarounds,
       such as when participating in asynchronous discussions or responding to questions. Many organizations
       have found that an Open Source Program Office (OSPO) is an effective organizational tool for integrating
       these unique considerations into agency processes. 14
       Contributions are not limited to lines of code. OSS projects generally welcome participation in discussions,
       both synchronously through scheduled meetings or asynchronously through pull requests and issue
       discussions in the online repository. Contributions from individuals with different equities and skills
       strengthen OSS projects and reduce the burden placed on individual maintainers.

       Perform Privacy Review
       Agencies should review contributions to OSS projects for privacy concerns and train staff on what
       information is appropriate to contribute. Security, development, and operations (SecDevOps) tools can
       conduct automated privacy checks on contributions. Although source code is generally not considered
       sensitive from a privacy standpoint, it can still contain sensitive information embedded in the code,
       configuration, or documentation.
       Performing an OSS contribution privacy review is especially important given agencies’ role in safeguarding
       individuals’ data and carrying out critical functions. For example, a test case written for a code change,
       which uses a piece of sensitive data as the basis for the test, should not be committed upstream.
       Privacy review should focus on identifying and securing sensitive artifacts to prevent their inadvertent
       disclosure. Sensitive information that may be mistakenly included in a contribution includes:
               Security keys
               Passwords
               Configuration information
               Hostnames and Internet Protocol addresses
               Authentication credentials


       Producing Open Source Software
       As software consumers, agencies benefit from software producers’ use of open source development
       practices. Transparent development lets agencies extract security-relevant insights from project repository
       metadata that demonstrates how the software was built.
       When agencies act as software producers, adopting open source development practices for custom-
       developed software provides additional benefits, including:
               Code quality: Developers write better code when they know it will be publicly visible.
               Commit history as a security and audit asset: Full provenance of how the software evolved
                strengthens security reviews, supports incident response, and clarifies design decisions.
               Talent attraction: Visible OSS contributions help agencies build relationships with the broader
                developer community, which can contribute to attracting better engineering talent.

       By releasing custom-developed software as OSS, agencies benefit the public and help other federal
       agencies reduce costs by avoiding duplicate software development. 15 In addition, agencies gain a greater
       return on investment through lower publishing costs. Although software developed through closed
       development practices can be published as open source, starting with open source development avoids
       expensive project review to remove sensitive data such as keys.

       Use the “Default Open Source” Approach to Custom-Developed Software
       Agencies should adopt a “default open source” approach to custom-developed software. This approach
       (1) shifts the decision of whether the code should be made open source to the project design phase; and
       (2) requires justification for why the code should not be open source rather than why it should. Addressing
       how widely the code will be shared during project planning, rather than at the end of the project, reduces
       the effort required to prepare it for public release. Instead of requiring staff to justify publishing code as
       OSS, agencies should require them to identify and document any applicable exemption from OSS
       publication. Agencies can standardize the default open source approach through the source code inventory
       process.
       A default open source approach to government custom-developed software should also encourage the
       developers to use open source development best practices. 16 Knowing that the work will be public can
       reduce credential leaks, sensitive comments, architecture details, and other problems embedded
       throughout the commit history.

       Maintain Source Code Inventory
       An up-to-date source code inventory facilitates government-wide reuse of custom-developed code;
       additionally, Office of Management and Budget (OMB) Memorandum (M) 16-21 requires agencies to
       maintain such inventories. These inventories should indicate whether the code is available for government-
       wide reuse, is available publicly as OSS, or is not available due to a specific exception listed in OMB M-16-
       21. Agencies should complete this information based on the metadata schema published by OMB.
       Agencies should have a process for inventorying source code for custom-developed software that
       integrates with their existing software asset management processes. These processes should include:
           Identify custom-developed source code projects.
           Identify usage type for each project.
           Gather project metadata.
           Create, verify, and submit the file.

       Identify Custom-Developed Source Code Projects
       Agencies must identify all custom-developed source code. According to OMB M-16-21, custom-developed
       source code refers to code that is first produced in the performance of a federal contract or otherwise fully
       funded by the federal government. This includes:
               Code, or segregable portions of code, for which the federal government could obtain unlimited
                rights under Federal Acquisition Regulations (FAR) Pt. 27 and relevant agency FAR supplements.
               Code developed by agency employees as part of their official duties. 17
       Custom-developed code may include, but is not limited to, code written for software projects, modules,
       plugins, scripts, middleware, and application programming interfaces (APIs). It does not include code that
       is truly exploratory or disposable in nature. For example, code written by a developer experimenting with a
       new language or library is not custom-developed code.

       Identify the Usage Type for Custom-Developed Source Code Projects
       Agencies should determine the usage type of each custom-developed source code project before the
       project begins. The metadata schema specification has three general usage types for software: open
       source, government reuse, and exempt. The usage type determines who can access and use the code and
       the types of reviews the code must undergo.
       Projects intended to be open source or released for government-wide reuse should be tagged as follows:
               openSource: Project is intended for public release.
               governmentWideReuse: Project is intended for use only within the federal government.
       Projects that are not intended for public release should have an approved exemption as outlined in OMB
       M-16-21. Exemptions include:
               exemptByLaw: The sharing of the source code is restricted by law or regulation, including—but not
                limited to—patent or intellectual property law, export asset regulations, the International Traffic in
                Arms Regulations, and the federal laws and regulations governing classified information.
               exemptByNationalSecurity: The sharing of the source code would create an identifiable risk to
                the detriment of national security, confidentiality of government information, or individual privacy.
               exemptByAgencySystem: The sharing of the source code would create an unacceptable,
                identifiable risk to the stability, security, or integrity of the agency’s systems or personnel.
               exemptByAgencyMission: The sharing of the source code would create an undue identifiable risk
                to agency mission, programs, or operations.
               exemptByCIO: The agency’s chief information officer believes it is in the national interest to
                exempt sharing the source code.
               exemptByPolicyDate: The release was created prior to the M-16-21 policy (Aug. 8, 2016).

       Gather Project Metadata
       Agencies should document custom-developed source code project metadata in a machine-processable
       data format, such as JavaScript Object Notation (JSON). The metadata schema specification defines both
       mandatory and optional metadata fields for source code projects. Source code project metadata must
       contain the mandatory metadata fields as defined by the metadata schema specification.
       Agencies may provide additional agency-specific guidance for how to fill out metadata fields. For example,
       an agency may provide specific guidance to its components with instructions on how to complete the
       “releases.laborHours” field. To review examples of required metadata fields, see Appendix B for the
       Department of Homeland Security (DHS) requirements from the DHS Source Code Inventory Process
       (SCIP). Source code projects should also aim to include any additional optional metadata schema
       specification metadata fields.
       Agencies may use automated software tools to glean metadata from source code projects. If using an
       automated tool, agencies should ensure that the tool’s output includes the required mandatory metadata
       fields and supplement the tool’s output with manual input where necessary to complete the mandatory
       fields.

       Create, Verify, and Submit the File
       Agencies should develop, document, and implement consistent guidelines for submission of JSON files for
       custom-developed code written or maintained by that agency. One approach is to require the creation of a
       single JSON metadata file documenting all custom-developed source code written or maintained by an
       agency. See Appendix C for examples of source code inventory metadata.

       its lifecycle.

       Follow Appropriate Security Practices for Open Source Software
       Agencies should integrate automated processes—typically implemented using continuous integration
       tools—to detect and help prevent sensitive data or vulnerabilities in all software, including OSS. For
       example, secret detection in a continuous integration pipeline can prevent publication of sensitive data,
       and a dependency scan can identify vulnerabilities. When agencies follow secure software development
       practices, they minimize the danger of spilling sensitive information when publishing a project, but
       agencies must also complete security reviews as an added layer of protection.
       Although removing the commit history from a project (“squashing”) may appear appealing from a security
       perspective, agencies should avoid this practice for the following reasons:
               Squashing does not remove the commit history of derivative project repositories forked from the
                original project and so may not be effective in containing sensitive information.
               A lack of commitment to preserving history may discourage OSS stakeholders from engaging with
                the project after it is published.
       If an agency must squash commit history for security reasons to publish the project, the agency should
       preserve the full history in the original source location for audit purposes.

       Determine Appropriate Licensing
       Agencies should use a permissive license such as Creative Commons Zero (CC0), Massachusetts Institute
       of Technology (MIT), Internet Systems Consortium (ISC), or Berkeley Software Distribution (BSD)-3, though
       circumstances may necessitate a different license. Identifying a license for a project is an established best
       practice for all OSS projects. Agencies should consult with legal counsel to determine the appropriate
       licensing for each custom-developed source code project. Relevant legal considerations may include
       applicable export controls, privacy concerns, and contract terms.

       Release Open Source Software Projects on Public Repository
       Agencies should publish source code identified for public release as an OSS project on a widely used online
       code repository. Online code repositories allow developers to host and contribute to projects on the
       platform. When conducting official business, agency staff should use accounts affiliated with their .gov
       email and include their name and agency affiliation in their user biography. To promote productive
       engagement, agency projects should include:
               A README.md file that contains an overview of the project and states that the project was
                developed by or with funding from a U.S. federal agency.
               A LICENSE file that identifies the project’s associated license(s).
               A CONTRIBUTING.md file that outlines guidelines for contributing to the project.
               A SECURITY.md file that includes the project’s vulnerability disclosure policy and lists contact
                information for a security point of contact.

       accessible for an open source license to be applicable. Without this transparency, agencies cannot

       What Are Open Source Artificial Intelligence Systems?
       AI systems are software systems. AI is “a machine-based system that can, for a given set of human-defined
       objectives, make predictions, recommendations, or decisions influencing real or virtual
       environments. Artificial intelligence systems use machines and human-based inputs to—
           A. Perceive real and virtual environments;
           B. Abstract such perceptions into models through analysis in an automated manner; and
           C. Use model inference to formulate options for information or action.” 23
       Organizations—as they do with all software—identify open source AI by the associated license. OSI identifies
       licenses that comply with the definition of OSS. 24 OSI’s definition for open source AI is “a system made
       available under terms that grant software consumers the freedoms to:
           1. Use the system for any purpose and without having to ask for permission.
           2. Study how the system works and understand how its results were created.
           3. Modify the system for any purpose, including changing its output.
           4. Share the system for others to use with or without modifications, for any purpose.” 25

       Manage Risk for Open Source Artificial Intelligence Systems
       Agencies should obtain sufficient transparency into the relevant components of an AI system before
       treating the software as OSS for risk management purposes. Transparency is central to OSS risk
       management; it allows agencies to review project data and determine whether the software is trustworthy
       or not. AI software has unique characteristics that require specific security considerations. For example, AI
       model files can be purposefully corrupted to evade detection by a model scanner, AI model weights can be
       manipulated to change the model’s behavior or performance, and the underlying data or labels of an AI
       model’s datasets can be modified to poison the model. 26
       Open source AI licenses do not require that software producers make the model’s training data publicly
       available. Instead, these licenses require only that “sufficiently detailed information about the data used to
       train the system so that a skilled person can build a substantially equivalent system” be freely available. 27
       Information about the datasets provides some visibility but does not allow an agency to fully assess an AI
       model’s risks. If an agency cannot access a model’s training data, the trustworthiness evaluations for OSS

       The Impact of an Artificial Intelligence Model’s Training Data
       The minimum transparency required by the OSI open source licenses for AI systems does not allow
       agencies to make risk-informed decisions about their software. Agencies must have access to the full set
       of training data to understand the software, study the software, analyze the software for vulnerabilities,
       and create fixes for any found vulnerabilities. Data access includes the full training pipeline, such as
       training methodology, training code, and tuning parameters. NIST’s Cybersecurity Supply Chain Risk
       Management Practices for Systems and Organizations (NIST SP 800-161 Rev. 1) identifies visibilities into
       the system’s components or services and the information that it processes, stores, and transmits as
       critical.
       Agencies need full access to AI software system training data to find and fix vulnerabilities. AI researchers
       and engineers have demonstrated that cyber actors can embed a backdoor in a model by manipulating the
       training data—performing a type of supply chain attack unique to AI training pipelines—and these
       backdoors are computationally impossible to find without access to the training data. 28 For AI systems,
       identifying the location of a vulnerability is critical for preventing its exploitation. There is no way of
       systematically preventing exploitation of vulnerabilities, as is available for some other cybersecurity
       vulnerabilities. For example, database code injections can be prevented by query re-writing, but model
       backdoors inserted by manipulation of training data cannot be systematically mitigated.
       Although it is not possible to systematically prevent all cybersecurity vulnerabilities in each program,
       access to a program’s source code enables independent researchers to study it sufficiently to find and fix
       vulnerabilities and malicious code injections. In contrast, without access to an AI model’s training data,
       network and software systems’ defenders lack the information necessary to effectively prevent and
       respond to vulnerabilities.
       An open source label alone is not sufficient: agencies should treat any AI model that does not provide access to its
       training data and training pipeline as if it is not open source and conduct risk management accordingly. In other
       words, agencies should treat it as proprietary software with incomplete provenance. Agencies must confirm that
       the training data and training pipeline are accessible before treating an AI system as OSS.


       Conclusion
       OSS offers agencies a powerful tool for expanding their software capabilities. With missions ranging from
       national security to essential services and critical infrastructure, agencies require diverse and adaptable
       software solutions. While OSS is already present in agency systems and networks, agencies benefit more
       by thoughtfully integrating OSS considerations throughout the software lifecycle.

       Appendix A: The C4 Framework
       Open source software (OSS) is no more or less risky than other software, and all software introduces risks
       to a system. Agencies should evaluate the trustworthiness of a software component before integrating it
       into their system, confirming that its risks align with their risk tolerance. For OSS, risk assessment should
       focus on how the software was produced rather than who produced the software.
       Open source development practices are community-driven and transparent. Projects may have any number
       of maintainers and contributors, some of whom choose to remain anonymous and participate under
       usernames. Additionally, because maintainers and contributors are not bound by contracts or delivery
       obligations, assessing trustworthiness based on their identity, reputation, or location is ineffective. Instead,
       agencies should assess OSS risk by examining its development process. OSS transparency makes this
       possible. Agencies can access publicly available OSS codebase and project development metadata for
       review.
       The C4 Framework outlines how to leverage OSS transparency to extract security-related insights about an
       OSS project to inform risk management decisions. The framework uses risk considerations derived from
       Acquisition Security Framework (ASF) 29 practices for proactively managing software supply chain risk.
       “Software chooser” refers to the person in charge of determining whether a given component is suitable for
       the intended use or the person to whom that responsibility has been delegated. The Office of the Chief
       Information Officer (OCIO) typically performs the role of software chooser, determining the software that the
       organization consumes and operates. OCIO (or organizational component performing similar functions)
       may delegate software selection to other organizational components as appropriate. The C4 Framework
       groups risk considerations into four categories:
               Codebase: The source code and dependencies that make up the software component of interest;
                o   Examples: Date of most recent commit, presence of Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures
                    (CVE), out-of-date dependencies.
               Community: The organization or group of individuals that maintains and contributes to the project;
                o   Examples: The number of maintainers, whether the project is part of a foundation, maintainers’
                    affiliation with organizations of concern.
               Conduct: Expected behavior from maintainers and contributors as outlined in the project’s
                guidelines and observed behavior from project metadata;
                o   Examples: Whether there is a vulnerability disclosure policy (VDP), whether the project requires
                    code review, whether maintainers accept their own commits, the associated license, code of

       The C4 Framework is applied in five steps, as follows:
           1. Identify Criteria: Select risk criteria from the C4 categories (codebase, community, conduct,
              configuration) that will be the basis for evaluating software trustworthiness.
       The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) SP 800-218: Secure Software Development
       Framework (SSDF) Version 1.1: Recommendations for Mitigating the Risk of Software Vulnerabilities can
       help organizations identify relevant criteria. The risk manager or project manager should identify criteria in
       collaboration with their stakeholders through facilitation and elicitation of requirements.
       Criteria must be measurable. An example of an unmeasurable criterion is “the project follows software
       component transparency practices.” A measurable alternative is “the project includes a software bill of
       materials (SBOM) for each version.” Software choosers should systematically consider possible risks for
       the project’s codebase, community, conduct, and configuration and identify measurable criteria.
           2. Determine Risk Tolerance: Decide how much overall risk is acceptable given the intended use and
              deployed environment of the component and assign weights to each individual criterion.
       Risk tolerance depends on several factors, including where the component will be used in the agency, the
       environment in which it will run, what it is meant to do, and how much access it will have in the overall
       system. For example, risk tolerance for a component that will interact with personally identifiable
       information (PII) may be relatively low, whereas risk tolerance for a component that will support graphic
       design development may have a relatively high risk tolerance. The identified risk tolerance should be
       determined with a specific project in mind.
       Software choosers should assign each identified criterion a weight proportional to its impact on overall risk.
       Analysis may find that a component satisfies some criteria and fails others. The criteria weights will help
       compare the component’s overall measured risk to the risk tolerance. This process can include selecting
       some criteria as red flags, meaning that failure of any one red flag criterion renders the component not
       trustworthy, even if all other criteria are met.
           3. Collect Observations: Derive the metrics needed for each criterion.
       Information collection should be repeatable and consistent across individual risk managers within the
       agency. The agency can collect information manually or with automated tools. Automated tools support
       consistency, scalability, and repeatability when they are available. Tools such as Hipcheck, CHAOSS, and
       Scorecard are freely available to gather information about some likely criteria of interest. These tools can
       collect information about criteria such as presence of an SBOM and a project’s active contributors.
       Software choosers should carefully consider the details of the information tools provide. Scorecard, for
       example, can verify whether the project has an SBOM, but it will not look at the content of the SBOM.
           4. Evaluate Against Criteria: Compare observations to each individual criterion and determine whether
              the component satisfies or fails each one.
       Each individual criterion should be assessed and the results documented for later review. For example, if
       one of the criteria is that there must be at least two active maintainers but there is only one, the

       identified).
          5. Compare Observed Risk to Risk Tolerance: Looking at how the component fared against the
             criteria, determine whether the component’s risk meets or exceeds the identified risk tolerance.
       In some cases, the results are clear, and it is obvious whether the component meets or exceeds the risk
       tolerance. This can happen when the component fails a red flag criterion or when it either meets all criteria
       or fails all of them. Using the criterion weights can help show whether the component’s overall risk is above
       or below the acceptable level. Based on these results, agencies decide whether to use the component,
       avoid it, gather more information, or move forward only with added safeguards.
