 1   Abstract
 2   This document is a high-level guide to help executive leadership in both the public and private
 3   sector understand key cybersecurity topics, including governance, risk management,
 4   information exchange, security awareness and training, contingency planning, configuration
 5   management, and incident response. It follows the general organization of the NIST
 6   Cybersecurity Framework to emphasize the relationships between these core concepts and
 7   points to other NIST publications for more detailed information and implementation guidance.

 8   Keywords
 9   Cybersecurity; cybersecurity supply chain risk management (C-SCRM); governance; NIST
10   Cybersecurity Framework (CSF); risk management.

137   1. Introduction
138   NIST Special Publication (SP) 800-100, Information Security Handbook: A Guide for Executive
139   Leadership, provides high-level summaries of key cybersecurity topics in selected NIST
140   frameworks and points to other NIST publications for more detailed information. Its goal is to
141   help executive leadership in both public- and private-sector organizations understand the
142   relationships between core concepts in cybersecurity using the familiar structure of the NIST
143   Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 [CSF 2.0]. The material in this handbook provides general
144   information on key topics, including information security, privacy, cybersecurity supply chain
145   risk management, and enterprise risk management (ERM). Organizations should refer to the
146   cited publications for additional details and guidelines to address their own unique security
147   postures and business requirements.

148   1.1. Purpose
149   This document provides high-level descriptions of cybersecurity concepts, actions, and
150   procedures that those in information security and risk management roles (e.g., organizational
151   leadership, Chief Information Officers [CIOs], Chief Information Security Officers [CISOs]) will be
152   expected to implement and oversee in their respective organizations. Subsections that are
153   dedicated to specific topics will point to NIST publications for more detailed information and
154   technical implementation.
155   The topics in this document are organized based on the Functions and Categories of the CSF to
156   facilitate a consistent approach to establishing and refining information security programs, and
157   many of the topics apply to both public- and private-sector organizations.

170                o Ensures that senior agency officials within the organization are given the

246   2.1. Risk Management Framework (RMF)
247   SP 800-37, Risk Management Framework for Information Systems and Organizations: A System
248   Life Cycle Approach for Security and Privacy [SP 800-37r2], provides a comprehensive, flexible,
249   repeatable, and measurable seven-step process that organizations can use to manage their
250   unique information security and privacy risks, and leverages a portfolio of additional resources
251   to support implementation. The Risk Management Framework (RMF) can be applied to new
252   and existing systems or technologies (e.g., IoT, control systems) and within any type of
253   organization, regardless of size or sector. Key terminology and concepts in the RMF include:
254       •    Risk management strategy: A strategy that addresses risk tolerance and how
255            organizations plan to assess, respond to, and monitor risks.
256       •    Authorizing official: A senior official or executive with the authority to formally assume
257            responsibility and accountability for operating a system and the only organizational
258            official who can accept security and privacy risks to organizational operations,
259            organizational assets, and individuals.
260       •    Authorization boundary: Components of a system to be authorized for operation (i.e.,
261            the scope of the “system” for RMF implementation).
262       •    Authorization to operate (ATO): Periodic or ongoing determination by the authorizing
263            official on whether the system can continue to operate based on the effectiveness of
264            risk management efforts and organizational risk tolerance.
265       •    Risk tolerance: The level of risk that an organization is willing to assume to achieve a
266            potential desired result.

267       •    Security and Privacy Controls: Descriptions of the safeguards for achieving security and
268            privacy risk management objectives. [SP 800-53r5] provides a catalog of security and
269            privacy controls divided into 20 topic areas referred to as families (e.g., Access Control,
270            Configuration Management, Risk Assessment, Personally Identifiable Information (PII)
271            Processing and Transparency, Supply Chain Risk Management). The current SP 800-53
272            catalog is maintained in the Cybersecurity and Privacy Reference Tool (CPRT).
273       •    Tailoring: The process by which control baselines and controls are modified to meet
274            organizational risks and constraints [SP 800-53B].
275   The risk-based approach of the RMF includes the following steps:
276       •    Prepare for risk management with essential activities for program design and
277            implementation. Related tasks include defining risk management roles, creating a risk
278            management strategy and continuous monitoring strategy, determining the scope of
279            the system’s authorization boundary, and conducting risk assessments.
280       •    Categorize systems and information based on an impact analysis. Related tasks include
281            documenting a description of the system and identifying potential adverse impacts
282            related to the confidentiality, integrity, and/or availability of information stored,
283            processed, or transmitted by the system.
284       •    Select [SP 800-53r5] controls to protect the system based on its security categorization
285            and completed risk assessments. Related tasks include selecting the corresponding SP
286            800-53B security control and/or privacy baseline, tailoring the control baseline to align
287            with organization specific security and privacy objectives, allocating additional controls
288            to the system, documenting planned control implementation, and developing and
289            implementing a system-level continuous monitoring strategy.
290       •    Implement the controls, and document how the controls are deployed. Related tasks
291            include documenting the as-implemented controls.
292       •    Assess the control implementation to determine whether the controls are in place,
293            operating as intended, and producing the desired results to manage risks. Related tasks
294            include developing a control assessment plan, conducting control assessments,
295            developing assessment reports, and developing a plan of action and milestones
296            (POA&M) to track risks through remediation or acceptance.
297       •    Authorize the system to operate; the system is put into operation after the authorizing
298            official accepts residual risk and determine it is within the organizational risk tolerance.
299            These tasks include assembling the authorization package for review by the authorizing
300            official; reviewing, analyzing, and responding to risks; and having the authorizing official
301            make an authorization decision.
302       •    Continuously monitor the effectiveness of control implementations and identify any
303            changes to the risks to the system. These tasks include monitoring system environment
304            for new components, identifying configuration changes to existing system components,
305            conducting ongoing control assessments, implementing risk responses, reporting on

310   2.2. Cybersecurity Framework (CSF 2.0)
311   The Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 is designed to help organizations of all sizes and sectors
312   — including industry, government, academia, and nonprofit — manage and reduce their
313   cybersecurity risks alongside their other organizational risks (e.g., financial, supply chain,
314   reputational, regulatory). The CSF describes desired cybersecurity outcomes that are sector-,
315   country-, and technology-neutral to give organizations the flexibility to prioritize the
316   cybersecurity outcomes that best address their unique risks, technologies, and
317   business/mission considerations.
318   The CSF includes the following components:
319       •    CSF Core: A taxonomy of high-level cybersecurity outcomes that are organized
320            hierarchically into Functions, Categories, and Subcategories that detail each outcome.
321            The Core describes what desired outcomes an organization can aspire to. Additional CSF
322            resources and existing controls catalogs can assist organizations in determining how to
323            achieve the desired outcomes.
324       •    CSF Organizational and Community Profiles: An Organizational Profile is a mechanism
325            for describing an organization’s current and/or target cybersecurity posture in terms of
326            the CSF Core’s outcomes. Profiles can help an organization prioritize the CSF Core
327            outcomes to achieve its target state. A Community Profile is a baseline of CSF outcomes
328            that is created and published to address shared interests and goals among
329            organizations. It is typically developed for a particular industry sector or subsector,
330            technology, threat type, or other use case. An organization can use as-is or tailor a
331            Community Profile as the basis for its own Target Profile.
332       •    CSF Tiers: Provide context for how an organization views cybersecurity risks and the
333            processes in place to manage those risks. Tiers can also be applied to CSF Organizational
334            Profiles to characterize the rigor of an organization’s cybersecurity risk governance and
335            management practices.

340   development in both federal agencies and the private sector. In 2017, NICE created the
341   Workforce Framework for Cybersecurity (NICE Framework), which helps organizations describe
342   their cybersecurity efforts through three conceptual building blocks: tasks, knowledge, and

359   2.4. Privacy Framework
360   The NIST Privacy Framework provides a common language for understanding, managing, and
361   communicating privacy risk with both private and public stakeholders. Designed as a voluntary
362   tool, it is intended for broad applicability across organizations of all sizes and is agnostic to any
363   particular technology, sector, law, or jurisdiction. The Framework is adaptable to any
364   organization’s role in the data processing ecosystem and assists in the identification and
365   prioritization of actions for reducing privacy risks. Ultimately, it serves as a valuable tool for
366   aligning policy, business, and technological approaches to effectively manage risks. Use of the
367   NIST Privacy Framework can help:
368       •    Establish or improve a Privacy Program

      NIST SP 800-100r1 ipd (Initial Public Draft)                           Information Security Handbook
      October 2026                                                          A Guide for Executive Leadership

369       •    Strengthen accountability
370       •    Prioritize and transform privacy outcomes by integrating privacy into the system
371            development life cycle (SDLC)
372       •    Align privacy risk management by communicating requirements to internal and external
373            stakeholders
374       •    Inform decision-makers about buying decisions
375   Even when fully compliant with applicable laws and regulations, unintended consequences can
376   affect an organization’s brand, finances, and prospects for growth. Privacy risk assessments can
377   help organizations analyze their practices, detect gaps, and identify areas for improvement. By
378   utilizing a risk- and outcome-driven approach, the NIST Privacy Framework supports the
379   development of innovative and effective solutions that enhance outcomes for individuals and
380   organizations, while ensuring relevance in the face of evolving technology. Additional detailed
381   information regarding the NIST Privacy Framework can be found at Privacy Framework.

382   2.5. Secure Software Development Framework (SSDF)
383   The Secure Software Development Framework (SSDF) defines high-level practices that
384   organizations can integrate into existing software development procedures, including
385   acquisition and collaboration with third-party suppliers. These practices are organized into four
386   general categories:
387       1. Prepare the Organization (PO): Ensuring that an organization’s people, processes, and
388          technologies are prepared to perform secure software development
389       2. Protect the Software (PS): Protecting components of software from tampering and
390          unauthorized access
391       3. Produce Well-Secured Software (PW): Producing well-secured software with minimal
392          security vulnerabilities
393       4. Respond to Vulnerabilities (RV): Identifying residual vulnerabilities and addressing them
394          appropriately to prevent future occurrences
395   The SSDF does not prescribe implementation but focuses on intended outcomes, which allows
396   organizations to tailor these practices to their unique environments and needs.
397   SP 800-218, Secure Software Development Framework (SSDF) Version 1.1: Recommendations
398   for Mitigating the Risk of Software Vulnerabilities [SP 800-218], describes these categories in
399   greater detail, including tasks, notional implementation examples, and additional references.

425   2.7. Cybersecurity Supply Chain Risk Management (C-SCRM)
426   Cybersecurity supply chain risk management (C-SCRM), while not technically a NIST framework,
427   describes a systematic process for managing exposure to cybersecurity risks throughout supply
428   chains and developing appropriate response strategies, policies, processes, and procedures
429   [CSF 2.0]. All technologies rely on a complex, globally distributed, and interconnected supply
430   chain ecosystem of public- and private-sector entities that interact throughout the life cycle of a
431   product or service. Effective C-SCRM requires both internal and external stakeholder
432   engagement to actively collaborate, communicate, and take collective action to secure
433   favorable C-SCRM outcomes and understand the potential ramifications of cybersecurity risks
434   throughout the supply chain.
435   C-SCRM is integrated into enterprise-wide risk management processes (e.g., acquisition, system
436   development life cycle) so that organizations can identify priorities and develop solutions. As
437   the exposure to supply chain risks differs across and within enterprises, business- and mission-
438   specific strategies and policies should set the tone and direction for C-SCRM across the
439   enterprise [SP 800-161r1].

440   2.8. Relationships Between Key NIST Frameworks
441   Together, these frameworks and processes form a complementary ecosystem for addressing
442   distinct but associated risk domains based on common structural principles. Since they are
443   designed to be technology and sector-neutral, they can be layered onto one another and
444   tailored to organizational needs. Moreover, they explicitly reference one another’s components
445   to ensure a well-rounded approach to defining outcomes, processes, and workforce
446   capabilities.
447   The operational foundation of these NIST frameworks is the RMF, which describes procedural
448   guidelines on governance and risk management. Its seven-step life cycle can be applied to both
449   new and existing systems in any organization, regardless of size or sector. It also directly aligns
450   with and references other key NIST frameworks and resources. For example, the RMF’s “Select”
451   step explicitly draws from the control catalog in [SP 800-53r5], and the “Implementation” step
452   is hardened by SSDF practices, which in turn intersect with C-SCRM (e.g., third-party
453   collaboration, acquisition). The RMF describes a repeatable implementation process for
454   achieving the target states defined in other frameworks.
455   The CSF’s Functions, Categories, and Subcategories then layer on the RMF to describe
456   cybersecurity outcomes. For example, an organization can use the CSF’s Organizational Profiles
457   to set its target cybersecurity posture and then implement the RMF’s process to achieve that
458   posture. This enables strategic, organization-wide prioritization that considers financial, supply
459   chain, and reputational risks. The Privacy Framework also supplements the RMF to specifically
460   address the privacy risks that can arise, even when an organization is fully compliant with
461   applicable laws and regulations. It feeds into the control selections [SP 800-53r5] for the RMF to
462   give organizations a privacy-specific vocabulary that mirrors how the CSF addresses
463   cybersecurity. The AI RMF is another domain-specific extension of the RMF that addresses AI-
464   specific risks and trustworthiness characteristics. Like the CSF’s Organizational Profiles and the
465   Privacy Framework’s prioritization mechanisms, the AI RMF’s functions define the target states
466   and priorities that the RMF’s life cycle helps implement.
467   While the RMF, CSF, Privacy Framework, and AI RMF focus on outcomes and processes, the
468   NICE Framework addresses how people carry out those activities. Its Task, Knowledge, and Skill
469   (TKS) model describes the work that needs to be done, the knowledge and skills required to do
470   it, and the language for communicating about it. Its focus on workforce development supports
471   the implementation of every other framework.
472   Together, these flexible, agnostic frameworks help executive leadership understand the people,
473   processes, and technologies that are needed to help system-level management align security
474   activities with mission objectives.

476   Governance refers to the set of responsibilities and practices that are exercised by
477   organizations with the express goal of[SP 800-39]:
478       •    Setting the criteria and expectations by which risk management will be conducted [IR
479            8286Cr1] (see Sec. 3.1.1),
480       •    Establishing cybersecurity governance roles and responsibilities (see Sec. 3.2), and
481       •    Establishing and updating organizational cybersecurity governance policies (see Sec.
482            3.3).
483   Governance activities ensure that organizational missions and objectives are met, resources are
484   used in a responsible manner, and protections and safeguards are incorporated into the
485   organization’s broader security, privacy, and cybersecurity strategies. Section 3.4 identifies
486   specific federal governance requirements that may be useful for other organizations.
487   In addition to cybersecurity risk management (CSRM), privacy risk management (see Sec. 2.4)
488   and cybersecurity supply chain risk management (see Sec. 2.7) are critical elements of
489   organizational governance.

490   3.1. Risk Management Fundamentals
491   While striving to realize business and mission objectives, organizational leadership is expected
492   to manage a variety of risks (e.g., legal, financial, reputational, regulatory) across the enterprise
493   under the umbrella of governance, including clearly expressing expectations for how risk is
494   managed [IR 8286Ar1]. Executives prioritize risk management as a core component of
495   investment strategies to ensure that adequate resources are allocated based on risk
496   management priorities. By employing practices and procedures that are designed to foster
497   informed decision-making, senior leadership facilitates the protection of information assets
498   that support the organizational mission.
499   Key Risk Management Concepts and Definitions
500   According to SP 800-39, Managing Information Security Risk: Organization, Mission, and
501   Information System View, the components of risk management include:
502       •    Framing risk: Establish a risk context by providing a common perspective on how
503            organizations manage risks. A business impact analysis (BIA) can provide information
504            about asset valuation and criticality that factor into understanding the risk impact and
505            adequately resourcing risk responses.
506       •    Assessing risk: Identify, prioritize, and estimate the likelihood that a threat event will
507            occur and the consequences of an adverse impact to the organization and operations.
508            Risks can be cataloged in a risk register to support organization-wide understanding of
509            identified risks across the entire asset portfolio.
510       •    Responding to risk: Identify, evaluate, decide on, and implement appropriate courses of
511            action to accept, avoid, mitigate, share, or transfer risk.


                                                        11
      NIST SP 800-100r1 ipd (Initial Public Draft)                            Information Security Handbook
      October 2026                                                           A Guide for Executive Leadership

512       •    Monitoring risk: Verify that planned risk response measures are implemented and
513            determine the ongoing effectiveness of the risk response over time.
514   A key output from these components is the risk management strategy, which addresses risk
515   appetite, risk tolerance, assumptions, constraints, and how the organization intends to assess,
516   respond to, and monitor risks. The risk management strategy also considers the following:
517       •    Risk appetite: Defined at the highest level of the organization and provides a guide to
518            the types and amount of risk that senior leaders are willing to accept in pursuit of
519            mission objectives [IR 8286Ar1]
520       •    Risk tolerance: Represents the specific level of risk deemed acceptable within the risk
521            appetite set by senior leadership. Organizations may choose to express risk tolerance
522            qualitatively (e.g., Very Low, Low, Moderate, High, or Very High) [IR 8286Ar1]
523   Risk appetite and risk tolerance are related but distinct. While risk appetite statements define
524   the overarching risk guidance, risk tolerance statements define the specific application of that
525   direction. This means that risk tolerance statements are always more specific than the
526   corresponding risk appetite statements. Together, risk appetite and risk tolerance statements
527   represent and communicate risk limits that can assist organizations in managing risk [IR
528   8286Ar1].

531   responding to risks throughout the entire portfolio of organizational systems and services,

534   their potential impacts. Effective strategic planning requires understanding organizational
535   context, including:

537            inform enterprise risk management
538        •   The organization’s stakeholders and the critical organizational outcomes or services

540        •   Relevant laws, regulations, and contractual requirements that influence the

692   4.1. Asset Management
693   A key element of asset management is maintaining an accurate inventory of all organizational
694   assets (e.g., hardware, software, data, facilities). The inventory may also include services
695   provided by external parties as well as documentation of organizational data flows. Asset
696   inventory is not a one-time task. Processes should be established to regularly maintain an
697   accurate inventory of organizational assets.
698   Identified assets may also need to be managed throughout their life cycles, from development
699   and acquisition to disposal. An important component of managing assets is configuration
700   management (see Sec. 5.4), which is a process for monitoring and managing the configuration
701   settings of assets to maintain system integrity and prevent unauthorized changes to assets.
702   Organizations may benefit from establishing processes for updating the asset inventory to
703   record authorized changes to systems, provide required maintenance to assets, and securely
704   dispose of assets when they have reached end of life. For more information about asset
705   management, see SP 1800-5, IT Asset Management [SP 1800-5].

706   4.2. Risk Assessment
707   Risk assessments are a critical component of an organization’s risk management process (see
708   Sec 3.1). Risk assessments help identify, estimate, and prioritize risks to organizational
709   operations (i.e., mission, functions, image, and reputation), organizational assets, individuals,
710   other organizations, and the Nation resulting from the operation and use of information
711   systems. Risk assessments inform decision-makers and support risk responses by identifying
712   relevant threats, vulnerabilities, the potential impacts of exploiting vulnerabilities, and the
713   likelihood that harm will occur. The result is a determination of risk to the organization [SP 800-
714   30r1].

715   To be effective and useful, organizations should conduct risk assessments on an ongoing basis
716   and at various stages of a system’s life cycle. Risk assessments are composed of 4 steps:
717       1. Prepare for the assessment — Identify the purpose, scope, and constraints of the
718          assessment as well as the methodology to be used.
719       2. Conduct the assessment — Analyze threats, vulnerabilities, impacts, and likelihoods to
720          produce a list of information security risks that can be prioritized by risk level and used
721          to inform risk response decisions.
722       3. Communicate assessment results — Ensure that decision-makers across the
723          organization have the appropriate risk-related information needed to inform and guide
724          risk decisions.
725       4. Maintain the assessment — Maintain risk assessments to incorporate any changes
726          detected through risk monitoring.
727   The results of risk assessment help organizations respond to (i.e., identify, evaluate, decide on,
728   and implement appropriate courses of action to accept, avoid, mitigate, share, or transfer risks)
729   and monitor risks (i.e., verify that planned risk response measures are implemented and
730   determine the ongoing effectiveness of the risk response over time). For more information
731   about risk assessments, see SP 800-30r1, Guide to Conducting Risk Assessments [SP 800-30r1].

732   4.3. Information Exchange
733   Information exchange refers to accessing or transferring data outside of authorization
734   boundaries to accomplish a mission or business function. Significant benefits can be achieved
735   through the exchange of information, such as decreased operating costs, greater functionality,
736   improved efficiency, centralized data access, the reduction of duplicative datasets, and
737   enhanced relationships between collaborating organizations.
738   An important component of information exchange is system interconnection, which is defined
739   as a direct connection between two or more systems in different authorization boundaries for
740   the purpose of exchanging information and/or allowing access to information, information
741   services, and resources. Organizations establish formal agreements that specify the technical
742   and security requirements of the interconnection, define the responsibilities of the
743   participating organizations, identify the risks inherited from the participating organization, and
744   specify the rules that govern these interconnections.
745   SP 800-47, Managing the Security of Information Exchanges [SP 800-47r1], provides guidelines
746   for planning, establishing, maintaining, and discontinuing information exchange and access
747   between systems that are owned and operated by different organizations or that cross
748   authorization boundaries.

788   5.1. Zero Trust Architecture
789   Zero trust architecture (ZTA) is a cybersecurity paradigm that eliminates implicit trust by
790   assuming that threats exist both inside and outside of traditional network boundaries. It
791   focuses on protecting resources (e.g., assets, services, workflows) rather than network
792   segments through strict per-request authentication and authorization for all users and devices.
793   ZTA replaces the concept of implicit trust with a requirement for continuous verification,
794   segmentation, and the principle of least privilege [SP 800-207]. It is a strategic approach to
795   aligning with the RMF and CSF 2.0 outcomes by adhering to seven core tenets:
796       1. All data sources, services, and devices are considered resources and must therefore be
797          protected, regardless of their location or ownership.
798       2. All communication must meet the same security requirements, even if they originate
799          from an enterprise-owned network.
800       3. Access to resources should be based on least privilege and granted on a per-session

814   5.2. Awareness and Training
815   Security awareness and training programs are critical for disseminating security information
816   across an organization and providing the workforce with the tools they need to protect vital
817   information resources. These programs ensure that personnel at all levels of the organization
818   understand their information security responsibilities to properly identify, respond to, and
819   manage risks. While such programs vary across organizations, they share fundamental elements
820   that can be used to create a Cybersecurity and Privacy Learning Program (CPLP), which
821   encompasses cybersecurity and privacy awareness, training, and education [SP 800-50r1].
822   Typical CPLP activities that are implemented throughout the program’s life cycle include
823   awareness promotion, experiential learning, and training[SP 800-50r1]. SP 800-37r2 (Revision
824   2), Risk Management Framework for Information Systems and Organizations: A System Life
825   Cycle Approach for Security and Privacy [SP 800-37r2][SP 800-37r2], identifies the typical roles
826   associated with cybersecurity and privacy training programs. The NICE Framework [SP 800-
827   181r1]also offers guidance for identifying those with responsibilities for managing the CPLP.
828   Depending on the organization, a single person may serve multiple functions, or multiple
829   people may have overlapping responsibilities[SP 800-50r1]. The roles and responsibilities for
830   key positions in a CPLP should be documented in the program plan. Many of the roles
831   associated with awareness and training will likely have been identified during information
832   security governance activities (see Sec. 1.2).
833   Additionally, the NICE Framework (see Sec. 2.3) recommends creating and referencing TKS
834   statements that describe both the work that needs to be accomplished and the users who will
835   complete it[SP 800-181r1]. TKS statements become a core element of NICE Framework
836   Competencies, which describe tasks, track workforce capabilities, and identify team
837   requirements. The goal of a CPLP is to ensure that all personnel are aware of their roles and
838   responsibilities for protecting information and assets and can take appropriate actions to
839   respond to a variety of cybersecurity and privacy risks. This may include requiring personnel to
840   agree to Rules of Behavior (RoB) that establish standards of conduct for accessing an
841   organization’s information systems. Security and privacy training can directly map to RoB
842   provisions to educate personnel on specific behaviors to integrate into day-to-day activities.
843   The Federal Information Security Modernization Act [FISMA] requires federal agencies to
844   implement awareness activities and role-based training as security controls to minimize the
845   inherent risks that result from the people who use, manage, operate, and maintain information
846   systems and networks. SP 800-50r1, Building a Cybersecurity and Privacy Learning Program [SP
847   800-50r1], provides detailed guidelines for federal and non-federal organizations to develop a
848   CPLP that works within a larger organizational program to address risks, including physical

851   5.3. IT Contingency Planning
852   Information technology (IT) contingency planning refers to the process of recovering
853   information system services and data after a disruption of operations due to natural,
854   technological, or human-driven events. Organizations establish policies and procedures for
855   interim measures that help ensure the safety and security of personnel and property; inform
856   crisis communication with personnel, law enforcement, and the public; and mitigate long-term
857   impacts.
858   Contingency planning typically requires a suite of specific plans to effectively respond to and
859   recover from disruptive events. Each type of plan has its own scope, purpose, and activities that
860   vary depending on the organization. SP 800-34r1, Contingency Planning Guide for Federal
861   Information Systems[SP 800-34r1] [SP 800-34r1], describes the types of plans that organizations
862   can utilize to protect their systems. The following common plans are listed in alphabetical
863   order[SP 800-34r1]:
864       •    Business continuity plan (BCP): Focuses on sustaining the organization’s mission and
865            business processes (e.g., payroll, customer service). BCPs are typically used by non-
866            federal organizations.
867       •    Continuity of operations (COOP) plan: Focuses on restoring the organization’s mission-
868            essential functions (MEF) at an alternate site for up to 30 days before a return to normal
869            operations. COOP plans are typically used by government organizations and may be
870            mandated by FCD 1, Federal Executive Branch National Continuity Program and
871            Requirements [FCD1].
872       •    Crisis communications plan: Documents procedures for internal and external
873            communications after a disruption. These procedures may include guidance on
874            dissemination reports and statements (e.g., templates) and the designation of a specific
875            individual to provide information to the public.
876       •    Critical infrastructure protection (CIP) plan: Describes policies and procedures for
877            protecting and recovering vital national assets (i.e., critical infrastructure) and mitigating
878            risks and vulnerabilities. The CIP plan defines roles and responsibilities and implements
879            the risk management framework of the National Infrastructure Protection Plan (NIPP)
880            [NIPP].
881       •    Disaster recovery plan (DRP): Focuses on addressing major disruptions that affect the
882            primary facility’s infrastructure and restoring operability at an alternate site. A DRP is
883            site-specific and includes procedures for moving operations to a temporary location. It
884            may be part of a BCP or COOP plan.
885       •    Information system contingency plan (ISCP): Establishes procedures for assessing and
886            recovering a system after disruption. The ISCP identifies roles and responsibilities;
887            details assessment, recovery, and testing procedures; and includes inventory


                                                         23
      NIST SP 800-100r1 ipd (Initial Public Draft)                             Information Security Handbook
      October 2026                                                            A Guide for Executive Leadership

888            information. The ISCP is intended for system recovery regardless of location and can be
889            implemented at the primary site or after the DRP has successfully transferred systems to
890            an alternate location.
891       •    Occupant emergency plan (OEP): Outlines first-response procedures in the event of an
892            incident that threatens the health or safety of personnel, the environment, or property
893            (e.g., fire, medical emergency, bomb threat). An OEP is site-specific and includes shelter-
894            in-place and evacuation procedures. While it is executed as a first response, it may be
895            included in a BCP or COOP.

896   5.4. Configuration Management
897   Configuration management (CM) identifies the processes for establishing the initial
898   configuration of a system (i.e., baseline configuration), controlling changes to the configuration,
899   and monitoring the configurations throughout the system’s life cycle. Organization-level,
900   security-focused policies support system-level plans to provide a comprehensive strategy for
901   managing the configuration of components that are required for system operations.
902   The configuration of a system may change in response to:
903       •    New threats and evolving tactics by existing threat actors
904       •    Updates to system security, privacy, or supply chain risk management requirements
905            resulting from changes to mission objectives, business functions, regulations, standards,
906            and suppliers
907       •    Improvements to hardware and software capabilities
908       •    The identification of flaws and vulnerabilities in components of the existing environment
909            of operations
910   Changes can be initiated by numerous individuals (e.g., users, system owners) or identified
911   through audit findings or other reviews. An impact analysis can identify the effects that the
912   change may have on the system or other interrelated systems. As changes are made to baseline
913   configurations, the new baseline becomes the current version. The previous baseline is no
914   longer valid but is retained for traceability during configuration reviews and control
915   assessments. For example, if there are issues with a production release, the retention of
916   previous versions of the configuration allows system operators to roll back or restore a previous
917   secure and functional version of the baseline configuration.
918   The system owner serves as the authority for all CM processes and is responsible for developing
919   functional requirements and verifying that the requirements are implemented appropriately.
920   Reviews of the configuration baseline against the implemented configurations can identify gaps
921   in the CM processes, including changes that were not approved, that have not been
922   implemented correctly, or whose impact was greater than originally discussed during the
923   analysis and approval of the change.
924   The organization can also identify additional roles for the approval process based on the
925   configuration item, the type of change, and the criticality of the change (e.g., mission-critical,

926   moderate-impact, low-impact). A Configuration Control Review Board (CCRB) with various
927   organization- and system-level risk management related roles may be responsible for discussing
928   and resolving change requests that require additional funding or resources, ensuring that
929   change requests do not adversely affect any systems or services, and evaluating CM metric
930   information on other related issues.
931   SP 800-128, Guide for Security-Focused Configuration Management of Information Systems [SP

937   6. Continuous Monitoring
938   Information security continuous monitoring (ISCM) analyzes the effectiveness of security and
939   privacy controls in accordance with organizational risk tolerance. It incorporates processes to
940   help risk managers:
941       •    Maintain situational awareness of all systems across the organization
942       •    Understand security, privacy, and cybersecurity supply chain threats
943       •    Review the status and effectiveness of all implemented security, privacy, and supply
944            chain risk management controls
945       •    Provide actionable communication on the risk management status across all tiers of the
946            organization
947       •    Ensure that organizational officials actively manage risks

 991   7. Detect, Respond, and Recover
 992   An effective risk management program requires the ability to detect and effectively respond to
 993   incidents that may compromise organizational systems. These efforts involve the coordination
 994   of many people with specific roles and clear responsibilities[SP 800-61r3], including leadership
 995   (see Sec. 1.2), incident handlers, technology professionals, asset owners, and personnel in legal
 996   offices, public affairs, human resources, and physical security and facilities management. A
 997   well-defined incident response plan (see Sec. 7.2) enables organizations to define their
 998   capability to detect incidents, minimize loss and destruction, identify weaknesses, and restore
 999   operations as quickly as possible.
1000   SP 800-61r3, Incident Response Recommendations and Considerations for Cyber Risk
1001   Management [SP 800-61r3], describes an incident response model that considers the increased
1002   frequency, breadth, and capabilities of modern cybersecurity incidents and recognizes the need
1003   to integrate response activities across the organization.
1004   SP 800-160v2r1, Developing Cyber-Resilient Systems: A Systems Security Engineering Approach
1005   [SP 800-160v2r1], provides guidelines on developing the ability to anticipate, withstand,
1006   recover from, and adapt to adverse conditions, stresses, attacks, or compromises on systems
1007   that use or are enabled by cyber resources.

1008   7.1. Incident Response Life Cycle Model
1009   The incident response life cycle model has three primary stages[SP 800-61r3]:
1010       1. Detect: Event and activity logs generated by system assets, network service activities,
1011          and personnel activity are analyzed to find anomalies, indicators of compromise, and
1012          other potentially adverse events. System auditing capabilities provide the information
1013          necessary to establish a baseline for normal operations and deviation thresholds that
1014          may indicate aberrant system behavior. System monitoring and vulnerability
1015          management (e.g., endpoint detection and response [EDR], security information and
1016          event management [SIEM], security orchestration, automation, and response [SOAR],
1017          network detection and response [NDR]) tools enable the capture of information about
1018          the operating state of system assets, including the identification of exploitable software
1019          defects or hardware vulnerabilities, or ongoing attack activities from an advanced
1020          persistent threat (APT).
1021       2. Respond: When an adverse event is identified, system personnel focus on containing
1022          the threat by analyzing its impact and mitigating damage. Detection and response tools
1023          may automatically respond to a detected event based on the limits of the tool
1024          capability. This includes maintaining active communication with stakeholders, deploying
1025          countermeasures, and coordinating efforts with internal teams and external partners.
1026       3. Recover: After a cybersecurity incident, critical functions are recovered and activities
1027          are returned to normal operations. This typically includes confirming that systems are
1028          functioning properly and remediating vulnerabilities to prevent the recurrence of similar
1029          adverse events.

1051   policies on preparing for and responding to breaches of PII.

1053   managing cybersecurity incidents[SP 800-61r3]. It includes statements that clearly define its
1054   purpose and objectives, scope, the roles and responsibilities of both internal and external
1055   stakeholders, the types of incidents to be addressed, categorization criteria, response priorities,

1343   Appendix B. Glossary
1344   authorizing official
1345   Senior (federal) official or executive with the authority to formally assume responsibility for operating an
1346   information system at an acceptable level of risk to organizational operations (including mission, functions, image,
1347   or reputation), organizational assets, individuals, other organizations, and the Nation.

1348   authorization boundary
1349   All components of an information system to be authorized for operation by an authorizing official and excludes
1350   separately authorized systems, to which the information system is connected.

1351   authorization to operate
1352   Official management decision given by a senior Federal official or officials to authorize operation of an information
1353   system and to explicitly accept the risk to agency operations (including mission, functions, image, or reputation),
1354   agency assets, individuals, other organizations, and the Nation based on the implementation of an agreed-upon set
1355   of security and privacy controls. [SP 800-53r5]

1356   configuration management
1357   A collection of activities focused on establishing and maintaining the integrity of information technology products
1358   and systems, through control of processes for initializing, changing, and monitoring the configurations of those
1359   products and systems throughout the system development life cycle. [SP 800-128]

1360   continuous monitoring
1361   Maintaining ongoing awareness of information security, vulnerabilities, and threats to support organizational risk
1362   management decisions.

1363   Cybersecurity Supply Chain Risk Management (C-SCRM)
1364   A systematic process for managing cyber supply chain risk exposures, threats, and vulnerabilities throughout the
1365   supply chain and developing risk response strategies to the risks presented by the supplier, the supplied products
1366   and services, or the supply chain.

1367   enterprise architecture
1368   The description of an enterprise’s entire set of information systems: how they are configured, how they are
1369   integrated, how they interface to the external environment at the enterprise’s boundary, how they are operated
1370   to support the enterprise mission, and how they contribute to the enterprise’s overall security posture.

1371   federal enterprise architecture
1372   A business-based framework for government-wide improvement developed by the Office of Management and
1373   Budget that is intended to facilitate efforts to transform the federal government to one that is citizen-centered,
1374   results-oriented, and market-based.

1375   governance
1376   Activities for managing risks through strategic planning, including establishing and adjusting a risk management
1377   strategy, understanding organizational context, identifying roles and responsibilities, documenting policies, and
1378   managing the organization’s supply chain. [CSF 2.0]

1379   impact
1380   The magnitude of harm that can be expected to result from the consequences of unauthorized disclosure of
1381   information, unauthorized modification of information, unauthorized destruction of information, or loss of
1382   information or information system availability.

1383   incident response plan
1384   The documentation of a predetermined set of instructions or procedures to detect, respond to, and limit
1385   consequences of a malicious cyber attacks against an organization’s information system(s). [SP 800-34r1]

1386   likelihood
1387   A weighted factor based on a subjective analysis of the probability that a given threat is capable of exploiting a
1388   given vulnerability or a set of vulnerabilities.

1389   measurement
1390   The process of obtaining quantitative values using quantitative methods.

1391   measures
1392   Quantifiable and objective values that result from measurement.

1393   metrics
1394   Measures and assessment results designed to track progress, facilitate decision-making, and improve performance
1395   with respect to a set target.

1396   quantitative assessment
1397   The use of a set of methods, principles, or rules for assessing risk based on numbers where the meanings and
1398   proportionality of values are maintained inside and outside of the context of the assessment.

1399   risk appetite
1400   The types and amount of risk, on a broad level, an organization is willing to accept in its pursuit of value.

1401   risk executive (function)
1402   An individual or group within an organization that helps to ensure that: (i) security risk-related considerations for
1403   individual information systems, to include the authorization decisions for those systems, are viewed from an
1404   organization-wide perspective with regard to the overall strategic goals and objectives of the organization in
1405   carrying out its missions and business functions; and (ii) managing risk from individual information systems is
1406   consistent across the organization, reflects organizational risk tolerance, and is considered along with other
1407   organizational risks affecting mission/business success.

1408   risk management strategy
1409   Strategy that addresses how organizations intend to assess risk, respond to risk, and monitor risk—making explicit
1410   and transparent the risk perceptions that organizations routinely use in making both investment and operational
1411   decisions.

1412   risk tolerance
1413   The level of risk an entity is willing to assume to achieve a potential desired result.

1414   system development life cycle
1415   The scope of activities associated with a system, encompassing the system’s initiation, development and
1416   acquisition, implementation, operation and maintenance, and ultimately its disposal.

1417   tailoring
1418   The process by which security control baselines are modified by identifying and designating common controls;
1419   applying scoping considerations; selecting compensating controls; assigning specific values to agency-defined
1420   control parameters; supplementing baselines with additional controls or control enhancements; and providing
1421   additional specification information for control implementation. The tailoring process may also be applied to
1422   privacy controls. [SP 800-37r2]

1423   threat
1424   Any circumstance or event with the potential to adversely impact organizational operations (including mission,
1425   functions, image, or reputation), organizational assets, individuals, other organizations, or the Nation through an
1426   information system via unauthorized access, destruction, disclosure, modification of information, and/or denial of
1427   service.

1428   vulnerability
1429   Weakness in an information system, system security procedures, internal controls, or implementation that could
1430   be exploited or triggered by a threat source.

1431   zero trust architecture
1432   An enterprise’s cybersecurity plan that utilizes zero trust concepts and encompasses component relationships,
1433   workflow planning, and access policies. Therefore, a zero trust enterprise is the network infrastructure (physical
1434   and virtual) and operational policies that are in place for an enterprise as a product of a zero trust architecture
1435   plan. [SP 800-207]
