 Abstract

 Objectives:

 To understand patients’ and caregivers’ experiences with and perceptions of a federally-mandated program within the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) that provides educational and monetary support to family caregivers of post-9/11 Veterans.
 Methods:

 Twenty-six Veterans and their family caregivers were recruited to participate in individual and dyadic interviews. Interviews lasted between 60 and 90 min and took place between August 2016 and July 2018 in Oregon and Louisiana. Interviews were recorded, transcribed and coded by multiple team members. Recurrent themes and categories were identified through close examination of coded text and comparison within and across transcripts.
 Results:

 Three main themes emerged in the data: 1) appreciation of the caregiver program for validating and compensating family caregiver work; 2) perception that some caregiving activities are less visible, and thus go unrecognized and uncompensated; 3) concern about loss of benefits.
 Conclusions:

 Implications and policy recommendations for programs to support family caregivers, both within the VA and in the context of the broader national movement to support family caregivers, are discussed.
 Keywords: Family caregiving, Disability, Veterans, Qualitative
 1. Introduction

 More than forty million Americans provide unpaid or “informal” care to a disabled or ill adult, care with an estimated economic value of $470 billion. 1 Caregiving comes with substantial economic and emotional stressors for families, 2 – 6 and policymakers are beginning to grapple with how best to support family caregivers. 1 As evidence that caregiving has become a major national priority, President Trump signed into law the Recognize, Assist, Include, Support, and Engage (RAISE) Family Caregivers Act in 2018, mandating the development of a national caregiver strategy to direct governmental and health systems efforts to recognize and support family caregivers. 7 

 The Veterans Health Administration (VHA) has been an innovator in supporting family caregivers. At the direction of Congress, VHA established the Caregiver Support Program (CSP) to provide resources and services to the 5.5 million family caregivers of injured and disabled Veterans. 8 Through a subsidiary program, the Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers (PCAFC), caregivers of eligible Veterans may receive education and training, a graduated stipend based on clinical evaluation, health insurance, mental health services, and respite care. 9 , 10 Within each VHA Medical Center, a Caregiver Support Coordinator (CSC), manages program applications, makes eligibility and re-eligibility determinations, and coordinates necessary services, benefits and home visits. 11 The CSC may also offer peer-support groups and other programming to caregivers who are not enrolled in or eligible for the PCAFC. In establishing PCAFC, Congress recognized that many Veterans returning home with debilitating injuries would require daily care from family members for decades to come, responsibilities that may be incompatible with full, or even partial, employment. 11 Absent a safety net, caregiving could have significant negative implications for the income, health insurance coverage, and long-term security of caregivers and their families. 1 – 6 , 8 

 Implementation and delivery of PCAFC within VHA proved challenging, and major legislative and programmatic changes have been undertaken in just a few years. Implementation was expedited at congressional urging, yet a national directive governing the program was not published until 2017. 12 , 13 Other challenges included program demand that far exceeded expectations, 14 , 15 inadequate staffing at some sites and inconsistent application of eligibility standards, which resulted in some caregivers experiencing delayed (or denied) application decisions and receipt of benefits. 16 , 17 In response, VHA took steps to standardize the program and define staff roles and program procedures. 12 Soon thereafter Congress voted to gradually expand the program to Veterans of all Service Eras as part of the VA MISSION Act of 2018. 18 

 As the VHA’s CSP expands, research has documented the high value caregivers place on the social support, clinical knowledge and monetary resources (the stipend) provided, but also identified knowledge and logistical barriers to accessing and utilizing services. 15 , 19 , 20 While this literature has begun to shed light on the significance of PCAFC for Veterans and caregivers, we know very little about how caregivers and Veterans experience the program, their perceptions and beliefs, and views regarding elements of the program that may require change. Understanding Veteran and caregiver experiences with this innovative program is essential to allow policymakers and program staff to refine this rapidly expanding federal program, as well as to inform broader efforts at the state and federal levels to support family caregiving.

 2.1. Sample description and recruitment

 Veterans were eligible to participate in the study if they served in Post-9/11 conflicts and had a clinically confirmed diagnosis of traumatic brain injury (TBI). We employed three approaches to identify participants: (1) direct referrals from VHA providers, (2) community outreach that invited Veterans and caregivers to self-refer, and (3) recruitment via letter. Interested Veterans were asked to identify a caregiver — defined as “a person in your life who supports or assists you in areas important to you [including health care]” — to participate with them. Research participants continued to be recruited to the study until study PIs determined that theoretical saturation had been reached and recruitment goals were met. 22 Participants completed written informed consent, and study methods were approved by both the VA Portland and Southeast Louisiana Veterans Health Care Systems.
 2.2. Data collection & analysis

 At in-person enrollment, Veterans and caregivers received cameras, training in photovoice methods, and guidance on how to take or select photographs for the study. 23 , 24 At the second visit, the study team interviewed caregivers and Veterans individually, using their photographs as visual prompts. At a third visit, dyadic interviews were conducted with Veteran/caregiver pairs (dyads). All interviews were conducted in-person by members of the study team, lasted between 60 and 90 min, and took place between 2016 and 2018. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.

 Transcripts were coded by experienced qualitative researchers using a modified grounded theory approach that involved developing a codebook through open coding and consensus methods. 25 All transcripts were coded by multiple team members to ensure comprehensiveness and accuracy. Recurrent themes and categories were identified through close examination of coded text and comparison of such within and across transcripts.
 2.3. Study results

 We conducted 52 individual interviews (with 26 Veterans and their 26 caregivers), and dyadic interviews with 18 caregiver-Veteran pairs. Eight Veteran/caregiver pairs who participated in the individual interviews did not participate in the dyadic interviews due to reasons including major illness, competing priorities, and redeployment of the Veteran.

 At baseline, 12 caregivers were enrolled in the VHA PCAFC; a majority of caregivers in the study had utilized benefits and resources available through the broader VHA Caregivers Support Program. While the larger photovoice study was not focused specifically on the VHA Caregiver Support Program, discussion of CSP benefits and services, including the PCAFC and the Caregiver Support Coordinator, came up spontaneously and often during the individual and dyadic interviews. Thus, the authors in consultation with the study Advisory Board (composed of Veterans, caregivers, operational partners and researchers), decided to examine, categorize, and summarize data to better understand Veteran and caregiver experiences with and views of these benefits. Study participants were split between the two research sites. See Tables 2 and 3 for sample description.

 2.4. Primary themes

 We identified three primary themes related to Veteran and caregivers’ experiences with and perceptions of the PCAFC program: 1) appreciation of the PCAFC for validating and compensating the work that caregivers do, 2) perception that some caregiving activities are less visible and go unrecognized and uncompensated, 3) concerns about loss (or potential loss) of PCAFC benefits. Illustrative quotes representing these key themes are presented below. Quotes are identified according to the name selected by research participants, as well as their preference for service connection history.
 2.5. Appreciation of PCAFC for validating and compensating caregiver work

 The PCAFC provides tangible benefits to caregivers to meet basic needs through a monthly stipend, health insurance, caregiving classes and support groups; resources one participant described as, “incredibly beneficial.” Johnique, caregiver to her Army Veteran husband described, “If I’m working full time, everybody is getting neglected—the kids are getting neglected, he’s getting neglected, and when I come home, I’m tired…” For Johnique, the stipend helps ameliorate the earning capacity lost to caregiving and allowed her to provide the care her husband required, without sacrificing her family’s financial well-being.

 For Sharon, caregiver to her Marine Corp Veteran husband Carlos, the self-care skills she gained from caregiver classes had been essential. “The tools that they teach you aren’t about picking up the phone to make sure that the insurance is covered for this appointment…What they are teaching you is that you can’t lose yourself in this process of taking care of this person.” The class had helped Sharon adjust to a new, challenging role and provided essential support through this process.

 The contributions of program staff were also emphasized in connecting families to needed resources and services. One caregiver, Megan, caregiver to her Army Veteran husband, credited the Caregiver Support Coordinator (CSC) as key in securing an expensive track chair that allowed her husband to remain mobile on his farm. Another caregiver, Sarah, said her CSC was “the only person I really trust in the VA.”

 Program resources were particularly valued by caregivers given the lack of supports for Veteran caregivers outside of VHA, as Sharon explained,

 I have been told to tap into resources for myself. Like ‘Call the Vet Center … They see spouses. They see dependents.’ Then I called the Vet Center and they say ‘Ma’am, we are so overwhelmed with just Veterans, we can’t take on dependents at this point in time.’ I don’t feel like there is a true resource for caregivers. If you call Military One Source, they will tell you the same thing. …

 While the benefits and services offered by the PCAFC met crucial needs, interview participants also described a need for social support that often went unmet. Laurie, caregiver to her Army Veteran husband described, “Somebody that I could connect with. Even to hang out with, and they understand what I am dealing with and going through… that would be great, because then I would not feel so alone.” For participants, space and opportunity to meet regularly and in person with other caregivers would help mitigate feelings of social isolation that they experienced.

 Participants also emphasized feelings of recognition for their work and validation of their role as intangible benefits of program participation. Caregivers such as Damariz felt their work often went unrecognized, “People expect that that’s your job, that you’ve got to do it, and they don’t have to say thank you sometimes…”, acceptance into the PCAFC was perceived as validation of the substantial efforts they put forth in caring for their partner. Marine Corps Veteran Carlos recalled a phone interview with program staff as part of his in-take process. “My wife’s walking around… and the lady [from the CSP] asked me, ‘can you tell me what your wife does for you?’… I went [on] forever and then, when I finally got off the phone, my wife is like ‘I really do all that?’” When ultimately accepted into the program, the Veteran described the subsequent conversation he had with his wife, “I told her, ‘Baby, that’s awesome that the VA is not forgetting about you. They see the support you’re giving me…you are being recognized!’

 Later, Sharon, Carlos’s wife and caregiver, described how program acceptance came with emotional challenges, however, she saw these challenges as outweighed by the tangible benefits of inclusion in the program,

 I had very mixed feelings, because finally I feel like somebody else does recognize the work that I do and the help I give him, but I was also sad…I am so young, and I don’t necessarily always want to caregive for him because our roles in our relationship have changed… But it will be good because…right now, I don’t have health insurance. And it will be good for me to have insurance.

 While she experienced validation and material support from the program, she also experienced ambivalence about formalizing her role as caregiver to her husband.
 2.6. Less visible caregiving activities go unrecognized, uncompensated

 Participants described a disconnect between the hours they spent caring for their Veteran and program staff’s estimates of their caregiving time. This had important implications for the benefit level and respite services to which families were eligible. Damariz, whose caregiving responsibilities were evaluated as taking just 2 hours daily, described the caregiving tasks she performs for her husband, who was in recovery from a substance use disorder in addition to other health issues,

 I drive him to his appointments, I cook for him……he doesn’t take care of his finances at all…If he has money, it’s too much temptation with the drugs and the alcohol……I’ve got to be there when he’s having his panic attacks, I’ve got to stay with him in the bathroom, or when he’s having his nightmares at night, or, when he stops breathing, I have to wake him up. But it’s like those don’t all count as me doing something.

 Damariz felt that her around-the-clock caregiving responsibilities were not adequately accounted for in program staff’s calculations. Notably, what can and cannot be included in the calculation is governed by legislation, but may not take into account all of the day-to-day responsibilities of family caregiving.

 Casey, an Army Veteran, described his perception of the mismatch between the staff’s accounting and the realities of the day-to-day work of caregiving.

 The VA looks at a caregiver as: you’re wiping their butt, you’re making their meals and you’re controlling their medication timing. But, that’s eight hours to twelve hours a day. That’s not the Veteran having a flashback and a nightmare and waking up and then what? You’re going to call 911 and wait five minutes or you’re going to try and get a hold of the help desk at the VA at one o’clock in the morning? No. That person [the caregiver] is going to keep them from doing something stupid.

 For this Veteran, caregiving was both more amorphous and time intensive than what was reflected in staff evaluations. The tasks that Casey required help with could occur day or night and required intimate knowledge of his emotional, as much as physical, challenges to successfully manage.
 2.7. Concern about loss of benefits

 While participants deeply valued program resources, many described a continuous struggle to remain enrolled. To maintain eligibility Veterans must be reevaluated annually and show evidence of treatment engagement, although these requirements may not be consistently applied nationally. 13 , 19 For some families, this evaluation process could feel threatening, even adversarial. Caregiver David described how the process of maintaining enrollment in the program could be re-traumatizing.

 … we go over this with [providers] constantly…like, today with… [staff name] for the Caregiver Support Program. We had to go back over it again. All we talk about with people from the VA seems to be the bad, the negative… the implications of his TBI and his other illnesses…right after the caregiver people left today… the first thing he said was ‘God dammit, I had to go through my whole life arc again.’ …her interview is kind of like a deposition… It was adversarial.

 For families reliant on the PCAFC stipend and benefits, the re-evaluation interview was crucial to ensuring the continuous receipt of benefits. Yet, maintaining eligibility and benefits at the same level required participants to emphasize their limitations and challenges, which could feel difficult, even demoralizing. In contrast, some families described experiences where any signs of improvement in the Veteran’s functioning—such as attending an event outside the home– could result in a drop in tier level (and corresponding stipend payment), or even revocation from the program.

 Proving on-going eligibility for services could be particularly challenging for Veterans who suffered from largely invisible injuries, as is the case for many Veterans with TBI and PTSD. 26 Johnique described the challenge of demonstrating her husband’s limitations to CSP staff who came for short home visits to assess his functioning,

 There’s a lot of stuff that they knocked him down a tier for, stuff that didn’t make any sense to me…. If we’re in tier 1, [it is assumed] he could do everything by himself, and he can’t… if I’m not here, he does not eat. He does not eat because he gets confused when he goes to the refrigerator and can’t remember where’s the bread, where’s the milk, or where’s the cheese…I know it may look like…, ‘Oh, he’s great. He’s fine. He can do that by himself,’ but that’s not the case.

 For many, proving that the Veteran was, and remained, eligible for the program could be experienced as an on-going stressor.
 3. Discussion

 Through interviews with Veterans diagnosed with traumatic brain injury and their caregivers, we identified three themes related to program experiences: validation and necessary compensation; invisibility of certain caregiving tasks; and fears of losing benefits. This research builds on a growing body of evidence addressing family caregiver support programs, by illuminating Veteran and caregiver experiences with the VHA’s PCAFC, a billion-dollar federal program that has rapidly expanded—given strong, bipartisan support—but also faced challenges, including unexpected demand, expanded eligibility requirements, and changes to program criteria. While some of these challenges were reflected in our interviews, participants also described strong connections with program staff, emotional validation linked with participation, and financial resources that allowed families the freedom to provide their loved one with the care that they felt was required.

 Results of this study should be viewed in light of limitations. This study was limited to Veteran participants with a clinical diagnosis of traumatic brain injury (TBI) sampled in two VHA health care systems. Other geographic settings and participants with other diagnoses may yield different findings (among Veterans enrolled in the PCAFC, roughly 25% are diagnosed with a TBI). 17 Finally, our study was not focused on the Caregiver Support Program and not all participants were enrolled in the PCAFC; a study focused on and drawing exclusively from PCAFC participants might have yielded different findings.
 4. Conclusion

 As policymakers design and roll-out programs and services to meet the needs of the rapidly growing population of informal family caregivers, it may prove useful to consider the experiences of family caregivers served by the VHA’s innovative Caregiver Support Program. Our results point to several ways the PCAFC program could be altered to better meet participants’ needs. First, program staff should continue to work towards consistency in the application of criteria nationally, which would build families’ confidence in, and perceptions of, the program as fair and equitable. Second, given the dynamics described as “adversarial” by some participants, the program should consider separating the annual eligibility evaluation from the support provided by the Caregiving Support Coordinator (CSC), to ensure that families do not feel compelled to continually “prove” their needs for program services to staff, which may have the unintended consequence of degrading the supportive CSC relationship. Third, our findings regarding the emphasis Veterans and caregivers place on the social support aspects of the program mirror prior research and suggest the need for additional resources, particularly support groups, for both caregivers and Veterans. 19 Increasing opportunities for Veterans and caregivers to meet regularly with peers could go a long way towards fostering social connection and enhancing social support among Veterans and their caregivers. For health care systems beginning to consider programs to support family caregivers, our results suggest that systems should develop clear and consistent criteria for program eligibility, separate eligibility determination from the purview of program staff, and provide a robust system of social support for participants from program initiation.

 Our results also have implications for the broader movement to support family caregivers and craft a national caregiver strategy as mandated in the RAISE Act. As the U.S. increasingly recognizes caregiving as labor , worthy of acknowledgement and monetary compensation, our findings highlight the challenges future programs are likely to encounter as they work to value and monetize this labor. For instance, our results reveal that family caregiving may encompass care tasks (e.g., supervision, feeding, bathing) that are discrete, monetized, and possible for paid staff to provide, alongside emotional labor that may be required around-the-clock, is difficult to quantify, and potentially impossible for formal caregivers to carry out. Another challenge relates to respite care: given that an important aspect of caregiving is providing love and support, how can respite care be crafted so that caregivers are willing to take it and patients willing to accept it? Finally, and even more broadly, what types of caregiving labor should government recognize and compensate, and which are simply an expectation of family roles and relationships? As the nation’s population ages, and efforts at the state and national level increasingly strive to recognize and compensate caregiving labor, this is a conversation that has only just begun.
