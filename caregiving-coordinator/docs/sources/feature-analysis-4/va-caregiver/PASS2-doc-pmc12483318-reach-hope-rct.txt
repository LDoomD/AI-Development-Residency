 Abstract

 Purpose/Objective:

 Caregivers of Veterans with co-morbid traumatic brain injury (TBI) and dementia face many challenges managing their loved ones’ neurobehavioral functioning and, importantly, their own well-being. This study developed and tested Resources for Enhancing All Caregivers’ Health (REACH) Hope, which provides caregivers with one-on-one telehealth education, support, skills building, and personalized digital information.
 Method:

 Caregivers (N=110) of Veterans with both TBI and dementia were randomly assigned to either REACH Hope (n=56) or a waitlist-control group (n=54) for 3 months; waitlist participants then received REACH Hope. Data collection occurred by telephone at baseline, 3 months (post intervention/waitlist switch), 6 months, and 9 months (for waitlist-control only). The Zarit Burden Interview-short version (ZBI-12) at 3 months (post-treatment) was the primary outcome. Secondary outcomes included caregiver depression, anxiety, and self-efficacy, and number of Veteran safety risks.
 Results:

 REACH Hope reduced burden and anxiety for caregivers with moderate to high levels of burden and anxiety compared to the waitlist group. Caregivers with ZBI-12 scores ≥21 had increasing treatment benefits with higher burden. Caregivers with Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 scores ≥13 showed the same pattern for higher anxiety levels. There were no significant treatment group effects for depression, self-efficacy, or Veteran safety.
 Conclusion:

 This is the first clinical trial to evaluate the efficacy of a telehealth intervention for caregivers of Veterans with both TBI and dementia. REACH Hope represents the first evidence-based intervention of its kind and one that warrants further study and implementation.
 Keywords: Caregiver, Veteran, traumatic brain injury, dementia, burden
 Introduction

 Research has shown an association between traumatic brain injury (TBI) and increased dementia risk ( Barnes et al., 2014 , 2018 ; Gu et al., 2022 ; Peterson et al., 2020 ; Stopa et al., 2021 ). People with TBI and dementia have different neurobehavioral, medical, and mental health profiles than those with TBI or dementia alone ( Selim et al., 2004 ; Dams-O’Connor et al., 2013 ; Gardner et al., 2017 ; Harnod et al., 2019 ; Peltz et al., 2017 ; Rao et al., 2010 ; Sayad et al., 2013). Compared to those with dementia alone, people with TBI and dementia have more depressive, disinhibited, agitated, and irritable symptoms ( Dams-O’Connor et al., 2013 ; Rao et al., 2010 , Sayad et al., 2013), and greater gait dysfunction, motor slowness, and falls ( Dams-O’Connor et al., 2013 ; Sayad et al., 2013). Common co-occurring medical conditions for people with TBI and dementia include cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease ( Dams-O’Connor et al., 2013 ; LoBue et al., 2017 ; Veitch et al., 2013 ). Co-occurring mental health conditions include depression ( Byers et al., 2012 ; Dams-O’Connor et al., 2013 ; LoBue et al., 2018 ; Perry et al., 2016 ; Veitch et al., 2013 ), posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD; Veitch et al., 2013 ; Yaffe et al., 2010 ), and mixed affective or bipolar disorders ( Perry et al., 2016 ). Consequently, people with TBI and dementia take more medications ( Dams-O’Connor et al., 2013 ) and have more hospital days and medical visits than those with TBI or dementia only ( Harnod et al., 2019 ).

 People with severe TBI and/or dementia often cannot self-manage their healthcare needs and are at risk for unintentional injury, rehospitalization, and early mortality (Byers et al., 2022; Carlson et al., 2012 ; Dams-O’Connor et al., 2017 ). Thus, family caregivers are the cornerstone of their care ( Griffin et al., 2012 ). More than 11 million family and other unpaid caregivers provided an estimated 18 billion hours of care to people with Alzheimer’s or other dementias in 2022; their care is valued at $339.5 billion ( 2023 Alzheimer’s Disease Facts and Figures, 2023 ). In the U.S, there are 4.3 million individuals caring for all Service Members or Veterans, including approximately 1.5 million caregivers for a post-9/11 Service Member or Veteran ( Ramchand et al., 2024 ). Economic value estimates of largely uncompensated, all-cause military/veteran caregiving ranges from $119 billion to $485 billion per year ( Ramchand et al., 2024 ).

 Neurobehavioral and healthcare management demands for people with TBI and/or dementia place caregivers at increased risk of poor psychological and physical health ( Carlozzi et al., 2015 ; Kreutzer et al., 2009 ). Persistent and common caregiver issues are burden, depression, and anxiety ( Brickell et al., 2018 , 2020 ; Malec et al., 2017 ; Nichols et al., 2016 ; Schulz & Sherwood, 2008 ; Villeneuve et al., 2023 ). Caregivers’ physical and mental health declines ( Brickell et al., 2018 , 2024 ; Schulz & Sherwood, 2008 ; Wolff et al., 2016) and healthcare service use increases (Martindale-Adams et al., 2016) as their burden increases. In the long-term, a chronic burden of care leads to emotional difficulty, activity restriction, and loss of work productivity for caregivers (Wolff et al., 2016). Despite the high needs of caregivers of Veterans with comorbid TBI and dementia, no evidence-based caregiver intervention has been developed or systematically tested for this group.

 A caregiving intervention’s ability to reduce stress can be linked to the stress/health process model ( Lazarus & Launier, 1978 ). Caregivers experience stress if they perceive that environmental, situational, and internal demands are greater than their resources and capacity to manage them. For example, many caregivers of individuals with TBI and/or dementia see keeping their loved one safe at home as a primary role so providing individualized support to reduce safety risks is essential to ease burden ( Oyesanya, Arulselvam et al., 2021 ; Ramchand et al., 2024 ). Caregiver self-efficacy is their belief in their own ability to perform tasks important to caregiving ( Gallagher et al., 2011 ). For dementia caregivers, higher self-efficacy is associated with lower burden ( Gallagher et al., 2011 ), fewer depressive symptoms ( Fortinsky et al., 2002 ), and better management of loved ones’ behavior ( Cheng et al., 2013 ). For TBI caregivers, higher self-efficacy is associated with financial, social, and physical well-being ( Degeneffe et al., 2011 ) and use of problem-focused coping ( Anderson et al., 2020 ).

 One-on-one caregiving intervention strategies have the advantage of personalizing support to the caregiver’s specific and diverse situations, preferences, and needs ( Ramchand et al., 2024 ). However, they are often time-limited and do not provide instruction when problems arise outside of sessions. Telehealth can reduce caregiver distance and time constraint barriers, and digital information provision can be pre-loaded via apps to provide caregivers anytime access to a range of strategies for addressing problems ( Oyesanya, Thompson et al, 2021 ; Ramchand et al 2024 ). Conversely, up to 25% of caregivers may not have stable internet access, and digitally presented topics and strategies are often generic and may not be sufficiently applicable to the caregiver-patient dyad. To date, there has been no successful melding of one-on-one, telehealth, and digital app support delivery approaches.

 Resources for Enhancing all Caregivers’ Health (REACH) Hope is developed for caregivers of Veterans with both TBI and dementia. REACH Hope combines the REACH VA behavioral, education, and support intervention ( Martindale-Adams et al., 2020 , 2021 ; Nichols et al., 2011 , 2016 ) and the Department of Defense Virtual Hope Box information and resources app ( Bush et al., 2017 ) to create a tailored, telehealth intervention with one-on-one coaching and personalized digital support. This study tested the effects of REACH Hope using a randomized waitlist-control trial design. Our primary hypothesis was that caregivers of Veterans with TBI and dementia who are provided REACH Hope would have reduced caregiver burden at 3 months post-baseline (end of treatment). Secondary outcomes at 3 months include caregiver depression, anxiety, and self-efficacy, and Veteran safety.
 Method

 Transparency and Openness

 This study was preregistered with ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT04969796 ). We report how we determined our sample size, all data exclusions, all manipulations, and all measures in the study. All data analysis syntax and outputs, as well as research materials are available via request to the corresponding author. All analyses were completed using R statistical software ( R Core Team, 2024 ). The study was conducted under oversight of Lt. Col Luke Weathers, Jr. VA Medical Center (VAMC) at Memphis (1544094) and Virginia Commonwealth University (VCU) (HM20019111) Institutional Review Boards, and US Army Medical Research and Development Command Office of Human Research Oversight, Office of Human and Animal Research Oversight (OHARO) ( E01560 .1a).
 Participants

 Participants were primary caregivers of Veterans with TBI and dementia who had at least one activity of daily living (ADL) limitation (bathing, dressing, grooming, toileting, transferring, eating) or two or more instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) limitations (shopping, cooking, managing medications, housework laundry, driving/transportation, finances, telephone use). In addition, caregivers had to have a mobile telephone or electronic device that could accept the Hope Box application, provide 4 or more hours of care per day for at least 6 months, and endorse a score of at least high burden (≥ 8) on the Zarit Burden Inventory-4 ( Stagg & Larner, 2015 ; Zarit, et al., 1980 ).

 Potential participants were identified through lists of Veterans from VA’s Corporate Data Warehouse, which includes medical record data for all VA Veterans, using ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnoses for TBI and dementia; households that met these diagnostic criteria were sent recruitment letters inviting any caregivers to participate. After return contact, caregivers were screened. A consent form, post-paid return envelope, and response cards were mailed to eligible caregivers. After a consent telephone call, caregivers returned the signed copy of the consent.

 A total of 160 caregivers were assessed for eligibility ( Figure 1 ), and 110 (69%) were randomized to either the REACH Hope intervention (n=56) or waitlist-control (n=54) treatment group. The REACH and waitlist-control groups were comparable across baseline demographic and clinical characteristics with no clinically meaningful differences between groups for caregivers or Veterans. The study had 4% missing data which led to limited impact on results.

 Procedure

 After consent and before data collection, caregivers were randomly assigned initially to either the REACH Hope (n=56) or waitlist-control group (n=54) for 3 months, after which the waitlist group received the intervention. The block randomization with random blocks was developed by one of the authors, with actual randomization assignment under the direction of another author, who had no participant contact. Data were collected by telephone at baseline, 3 months (post-intervention/waitlist switch), and 6 months post-randomization and 9 months post-randomization (for waitlist-control arm only) by telephone. There were two trained and certified research associates masked to treatment group assignment; each screened, consented, and performed all data collection interviews for their assigned participants.
 REACH Hope Intervention

 REACH Hope combines the REACH VA evidence-based, behavioral intervention for caregivers ( Martindale-Adams et al., 2020 , 2021 ; Nichols et al., 2011 , 2016 ) and the Department of Defense Virtual Hope Box app, which contains information and tools on behavioral health, adaptive coping, and emotion regulation ( Bush et al., 2017 ). REACH VA, which became a VA-wide program in 2011 and is described by Nichols and colleagues (2016) , is adapted from REACH II ( Belle et al., 2006 ), focuses on caregiving risk areas and is structured through use of intervention protocols, scripts, and talking points for each session ( Nichols et al., 2016 ). REACH VA is standardized, provides education and support, and builds problem solving, cognitive reframing, and stress management skills, and is targeted to the needs of each care dyad through a disease-specific Risk Assessment ( Czaja et al., 2009 ). The assessment identifies the needs of each care dyad and helps tailor individualized content and a Caregiver Notebook, both developed by research team and caregiver subject matter experts (SMEs). The REACH Hope Caregiver Notebook includes 28 chapters on practical strategies for managing persons with TBI and dementia symptoms/behaviors and 16 chapters on self-managing caregiver stress and coping.

 Virtual Hope Box app, which is personalized by caregiver and coach, includes seven components that complement one-on-one coaching in REACH Hope. Components include: Remind Me (supportive photos, videos, recorded messages, music); Distract Me (games); Inspire Me (preloaded or user loaded inspirational quotes); Relax Me (mindfulness, controlled breathing, muscle relaxation, guided meditation); Support Contacts (quick access to preselected contacts); Positive Activity Planner (positive messages, activity planning); and Coping Tools such as Coping Cards (problem, emotions and physical symptoms it causes, coping skills to manage).

 Each caregiver worked with a certified REACH Hope coach, a psychology doctoral student, for six 1-hour sessions by telephone. Each caregiver selected up to two problems to problem solve with their coach with commitments to try strategies in between sessions. The caregiver further personalized one-on-one coaching by defining and framing areas of concern and identifying desired solutions that accounted for their preferences, situations, and culture.

 As the caregiver and coach worked on strategies one-on-one, relevant digital materials were placed into Hope Box to trigger, support, and reinforce caregivers’ new skills and identified solutions between sessions. REACH’s problem-solving skills were reinforced with Hope Box videos and coping cards targeted to caregiver concerns. Personalized coping cards, inspirational quotes, and stress management strategies were available in Hope Box to reinforce REACH’s cognitive reframing skills and strategies to manage stress outside sessions.

 As part of each coach’s certification, the investigators listened in on all sessions for the first caregiver served to ensure that the protocol was being followed and debriefed the coach immediately after the session. For the duration of the project, each week coaches and investigators discussed that week’s sessions. Project documentation forms, which detailed components of the intervention required for each session, were also reviewed.
 Measures

 All data were from caregiver self-report. The Zarit Burden Interview 12-item version (ZBI-12), measuring caregiver burden, has excellent discriminative ability ( Higginson et al., 2010 ). Items are rated 0 (never) to 4 (nearly always), with higher scores indicating more burden ( Bédard et al., 2001 ; Zarit, et al., 1980 ). A summed score of > 17 ( Bédard et al., 2001 ; Stagg & Larner, 2015 ) indicates high (clinically relevant) burden. The ZBI-12 has very good validity (rho = 0.95–0.97) and internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88–0.93) ( Higginson et al., 2010 ).

 Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) ( Kroenke et al., 2001 ) items based on the DSM-IV depression diagnostic criteria are scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). Summed items characterize depression as minimal (0 to 4), mild (5 to 9), moderate (10 to 14), moderately severe (15 to 19), or high/severe (20 to 27). Cronbach’s alpha is 0.86 ( Kroenke et al., 2001 ).

 Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) items detect generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, and PTSD ( Kroenke et al., 2007 ). Items are rated 0 (not at all) to 3 (more than half the days) for an overall score range of 0 to 21. Clinical score characterizations of anxiety are 0–4 (none), 5–9 (mild), 10–14 (moderate), and 15–21 (severe). GAD-7 scores ≥10 are indicative of clinically relevant anxiety with 89% sensitivity and 82% specificity relative to mental health professional diagnoses of anxiety ( Spitzer et al., 2006 ).

 The Caregiving Self-Efficacy Scale-Revised ( Au et al., 2009 ; Steffen et al., 2002 ) contains 15 items and three self-efficacy subscales: for obtaining respite (SE-OR), for responding to disturbing behaviors (SE-RDB), and for controlling upsetting thoughts (SE-CUT). Participants rate their level of confidence to perform each item on a scale from 0 (cannot do at all) to 100 (certain can do) (10-point increments). Subscale scores are reported separately. Cronbach’s alphas for all three subscales are greater than 0.80 ( Steffen et al., 2002 ).

 Eight primary safety risks, identified by TBI/dementia SMEs, rated yes (1) or no (0), include access to dangerous objects or to a gun, driving, responding to emergency situations, leaving the home and wandering, smoking concerns, aggression or violence, and harming self. Higher scores (0–8) reflect greater risk. Cronbach’s alpha for safety risks from study data is 0.79.

 Project evaluation, collected at 3-month post-intervention follow up, assessed satisfaction, usefulness, and benefit, through quantitative and qualitative data collected by a non-study staff member. Quantitative questions are rated on scale from not at all (1) to extremely (5).
 Data Analyses

 Descriptive statistics were computed for the total sample and stratified by treatment group. No tests of baseline differences were conducted as such tests are not recommended in randomized control trials ( Altman et al., 2001 ). Intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was used for outcomes at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months, meaning all participants were analyzed in the group they had been randomized to regardless of treatment and/or study completion. Due to expected carryover effects for those assigned to REACH Hope in the first 3 months, a traditional carryover analysis was not used.

 Parallel analyses were completed for each outcome. An analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model was used to test for difference in means between treatment groups (REACH Hope vs. waitlist) at 3-month follow up with outcome baseline scores used as a covariate. Complete cases were used in this analysis as missing data was found to be less than 10%. To test homogeneity of regression assumption, models initially included the interaction between baseline score and group. If a significant interaction indicated a violation of this assumption, an ANCOVA with heterogeneity of regression (ANCOHET) model, that included group by baseline interaction, was used to test for severity dependent response differences in predicted values of group at each baseline score. To account for multiple testing, Potthoff’s (1964) extension of the Johnson-Neyman technique was used to control the type I error rate to 0.05. In addition, group means were compared at 6 months post-randomization. Given that both groups had received the intervention at this timepoint, no difference between groups was expected. Standardized mean differences , d , were calculated using the baseline standard deviation.

 To test REACH Hope maintenance of effects and effectiveness in the waitlist group, we employed a linear mixed model that analyzed outcome data for both groups at 3-months post-intervention (T2 for REACH Hope; T3 for waitlist) and 6-months post-intervention (T3 for Reach Hope; T4 for waitlist). The model included the effect of group and time, as well as group by time interaction and a random intercept to account for within-subjects nature of data. Missing data in these models was handled via maximum likelihood estimation which is appropriate for missing completely at random or missing at random data. Linear mixed models were fit using the lme4 package in the R statistical software ( Bates et al., 2015 ).

 Responses to open-ended questions were examined by two anthropologists experienced in qualitative data. Using scrutiny techniques of repetitions and similarities and differences ( Glaser & Strauss, 1967 ; Ryan & Bernard, 2003 ), descriptions, concepts, and central ideas were sorted into themes ( Bernard, 2006 ; Maxwell, 1996 ) and linked to quotes ( Bernard, 2006 ).
 Results

 Treatment Effects

 At baseline, the mean score for burden was above the severe cut point (≥ 17), while mean depression and anxiety scores were consistent with mild symptoms (< 10). The ANCOHET model revealed a baseline burden score by treatment group interaction ( p = 0.012) ( Table 3 ). Potthoff’s test found that REACH Hope reduced burden for caregivers who had ZBI-12 baseline scores ≥21 with increased treatment benefits observed as baseline burden scores increased. Figure 2 presents estimated means for the treatment and waitlist control groups by baseline burden scores. For example, for a person with a ZBI baseline score of 30 represented on the x-axis, the predicted 3-month score was approximately 21 for the treatment group and 27 for the waitlist control group. More specifically, the predicted reduction in burden at 3 months for caregivers receiving REACH Hope versus waitlist-control was 2.37 (95% CI: 0.37 – 4.37, d = 0.32) points for baseline scores of 21 (adjusted p = 0.024).  Intervention Maintenance

 Results from the linear mixed model on 3-month and 6-month intervention follow up timepoints found no statistically significant effects for group, time, or group by time interaction for caregiver anxiety or burden ( Table 3 ). These findings indicate no evidence of a difference between groups or time (3 vs. 6 months) suggesting the waitlist group performed similarly to the REACH-Hope group and intervention effects were maintained long-term.
 Caregiver Benefit and Satisfaction

 Ease of using telephone (4.8 ± 0.4) and support from coach (4.8 ± 0.6) received highest satisfaction ratings, while Hope Box app received lowest (4.0 ± 1.0). Some caregivers (8%) could not download or fully access all features of the Hope Box app, and an additional 7% did not like the app. Intervention sessions (4.6 ± 0.9) and stress and relaxation information (4.5 ± 0.9) had the highest helpfulness scores with the lowest score (3.8 ± 0.9) for Hope Box.

 In response to open-ended questions, caregivers in both arms identified five general benefits. Two benefits were directly related to the intervention format, including the sessions themselves and education about TBI and dementia and caregiving including the Caregiver Notebook. Two benefits, knowing someone cared and feeling not alone, focused more on caregivers’ emotional well-being. “ Thank you for putting this program together. Coming in with no knowledge of his dementia and TBI, I felt alone. It’s made such a difference with how I relate to my husband. It’s been such a great help .” Finally, caregivers took control of the care of their loved one and their own self-care, learning how to value themselves with new skills, such as communication, stress management, and mood management. One caregiver felt surer of herself: “ The reinforcement, sharing of ideas, posing of problems, etc. It was such a big sharing for me. I learned. I grew. I see things through a different set of eyes. Because I was given these ‘try this’ [from Program Coach]. And I didn’t have that before. I’m a better person. I went from ‘Woe is me’ to being sure of myself. Now I’m his rock. I didn’t have the tools [before this program ].” Others started caring for themselves, “ Because of my Coach, I also went to the doctor for the first time in like 20 years. I’m going to take care of me .” Another reported, “ This is a wonderful program. It’s been so helpful for me, especially the coping mechanisms and the relaxation. And I’m carrying over [those techniques] into other parts of my life .”
 Discussion

 This randomized controlled trial provides evidence that a 6-session, telehealth intervention—REACH Hope—reduces burden and anxiety in high-burden and high-anxiety caregivers of Veterans who have a TBI history and dementia. To our knowledge, this is the first clinical trial that has evaluated the efficacy of a telehealth intervention for caregivers of Veterans with both TBI and dementia who experience high caregiver burden levels. REACH Hope was effective specifically for caregivers experiencing high burden levels (ZBI ≥21); caregivers experiencing higher levels of burden (ZBI ≥30) showed exponential decreases in their burden. REACH Hope was also effective for caregivers experiencing moderate anxiety (GAD-7 ≥13) and provided increased improvement for caregivers with higher anxiety levels based on categorizations of the GAD-7. The current study’s findings generalize to educated, female, racially diverse caregivers aged 45–85 years old, who feel moderate-to-severe levels of burden and generally spend 5–16 hours per day providing hands-on care to Veterans with a TBI history and dementia. These Veterans tended to be aged 50–90 years old, with substantial functional impairments and 4–12 chronic health conditions.

 The finding that caregivers with higher levels of burden and anxiety benefited more from the intervention is consistent with REACH I studies, which also found greater improvements for caregivers with higher levels of baseline burden ( Gitlin et al., 2003 ). REACH Hope, as well as its predecessor REACH II and REACH VA interventions, was designed to tailor education, support, skills building, and stress management for caregivers with high levels of burden and risk ( Belle et al., 2006 ). The efficacy of REACH Hope and its predecessor interventions in providing exponentially greater benefit for caregivers with higher burden levels has substantial implications. The U.S. grapples with how to support family and other informal caregiving, with an estimated 53 million people providing care to persons due to natural aging, disability, and/or chronic health conditions. Replacing informal care with paid services would cost an estimated $470 billion each year. In addition, when family caregivers no longer can provide support, institutional care costs are typically borne by taxpayers with poorer results ( ACL, 2024 ).

 National attention has recently been focused on the issue of family caregiving, with the 2022 National Strategy to Support Family Caregivers, mandated by The Recognize, Assist, Include, Support, and Engage (RAISE) Act. As one of its goals and outcomes, the Strategy recommends that evidence-based, education services be available to family caregivers. It further suggests ( ACL, 2022 ) that programs be “flexible, available in-person or remote, and feature the lived experience of other caregivers.” Clearly, REACH Hope meets these criteria. However, it has also shown its utility for caregivers who are most in need, with high burden and anxiety. While all caregivers need and deserve support and assistance, there is wide variation in complexity, volume, and duration of support needed by each caregiver ( ACL, 2022 ). Tailoring and targeting to high-risk needs are a cornerstone of REACH ( Belle et al., 2006 ). This issue is particularly important for the Departments of Defense and VA and for Service Members and Veterans with TBI. According to the Defense and Veterans Brain Injury Center, nearly 414,000 Service Members worldwide sustained TBI between 2000 and late 2019. More than 185,000 Veterans receiving VA health care have received at least one diagnosis of TBI, mostly mild ( Massaad & Kiapour, 2024 ; VA, 2022). TBI is associated with increase in the risk of dementia ( Kennedy et al., 2022 ). Not all caregivers of those with TBI report high burden ( Marwitz et al., 2023 ); however, supporting family caregivers of those with TBI and dementia with what will best meet their needs and with the best use of resources will be a paramount concern.

 Limitations and Future Directions

 While this randomized controlled trial design eliminated many potential biases, we note several limitations that represent directions for future research. First, inclusion criteria only selected caregiver participants for high burden. Caregivers’ baseline depression and anxiety levels, which were not inclusion criteria, averaged in the mild range. This reduced study power to find intervention effects on these two mental health conditions. As a result, future REACH trials may benefit from additional screenings to target higher-risk caregivers. Second, the caregivers were overwhelmingly women and the Veterans with TBI and dementia were overwhelmingly men. Thus, we cannot generalize with certainty that these study results will apply to male caregivers and female Veterans with TBI and dementia. Future research should attempt to recruit men and women across both Veterans and caregivers. Third, there are well-documented challenges in using clinical diagnostic codes for dementia in Veterans health datasets. While we used best methods for identifying Veterans with dementia based on ICD codes, the most common Veterans dementia diagnoses were due to mental disorders or unspecified. Lastly, an intervention delivery limitation was the universal usability of the Hope Box app. Some caregivers had difficulty accessing and using the app, including occasions where the app did not work properly (e.g., videos would not download). Coaches and caregivers used workarounds, such as sending internet video links to the caregiver. A potential solution would be for VA to take over the app including routine updates which may alleviate usability concerns.
 Conclusion

 Despite these limitations, the current study was the first to develop and successfully test a telehealth intervention for caregivers of Veterans with a comorbid history of TBI and dementia. REACH Hope was found to reduce burden and anxiety among caregivers who had high levels of both of these variables at baseline. As a result, it represents the first evidence-based intervention of its kind and one that warrants further study and implementation.
 Impact.

 This study is the first clinical trial to evaluate the efficacy of a telehealth intervention for caregivers of Veterans with both TBI and dementia.

 Resources for Enhancing All Caregivers’ Health (REACH) Hope significantly reduced burden for caregivers with moderate-to-high levels of burden with a similar effect for caregivers with moderate-to-high anxiety compared to the waitlist group.

 Caregivers with higher baseline burden and anxiety levels increasingly benefited from the intervention, indicating REACH Hope’s effectiveness for those most in need.
