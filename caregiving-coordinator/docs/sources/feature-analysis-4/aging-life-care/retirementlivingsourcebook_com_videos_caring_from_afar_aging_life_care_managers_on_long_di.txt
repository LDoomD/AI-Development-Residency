 Caring From Afar: Aging Life Care Managers on Long-Distance Caregiving
 proaging aging life care caregiving 
 It's one of the most common calls the Positive Aging Community receives: "I live here in the DC area. My brother is in Memphis. My sister is overseas. Mom is in Michigan, and none of us agree on what to do." Caring for an aging parent is hard enough up close. From hundreds or thousands of miles away, it can feel impossible.
 That was the focus of the Positive Aging Community's latest roundtable, where founder Steve Gurney sat down with two aging life care managers who help families through exactly this: Susy Elder Murphy, who serves the Washington, DC metro area, and Nina Herndon, who serves the San Francisco Bay Area. The two are longtime colleagues through the Aging Life Care Association. Here's what they want every long-distance caregiver to know.
 First, what is an aging life care manager?
 Susy described aging life care managers as the only truly interdisciplinary profession in aging. They help with crisis intervention, finances, legal questions, family dynamics, housing and advocacy. Just as important, their code of ethics bars them from having a financial relationship with any resource they recommend. "You pay us and we give you the best advice for you in your situation with your budget and preferences," she said.
 The metaphors are plentiful. Susy calls them "wedding planners for aging well." Others say GPS for aging, or a surrogate daughter. Nina likes "quarterbacks of care," and describes the job as shepherding families through an experience they may never have faced before. Even if they have, she noted, the details are almost always different the second time around.
 Aging life care managers are expected to be conversant in eight knowledge areas and in how those areas intersect. That breadth is what sets them apart from a hospital or insurance case manager, who is typically focused on getting you discharged or managing a single illness. "They're not necessarily going to ask you, well, are you getting enough to eat?" Susy said. An aging life care manager puts all of those pieces together.
 Why the title matters
 The profession is a little over 40 years old. It grew up alongside assisted living, as the Greatest Generation lived longer and needed help weighing options beyond staying home or entering a nursing home. It began as the National Association of Professional Geriatric Care Managers. The association later did branding research, and few people in their 80s and 90s want to be called "geriatric." So it adopted the name Aging Life Care.
 Only members of the Aging Life Care Association (ALCA) may call themselves aging life care managers or professionals. Membership requires certification, education and adherence to the code of ethics. Others may use titles like senior care consultant or elder care consultant. Nina stressed that this is more than a regional naming difference. A "senior care consultant" may be paid by assisted living communities to place residents. That is a very different relationship than an independent professional you hire. Firms can also be mixed, with some staff who are ALCA members and some who are not.
 Challenge one: you can't see what's really happening
 "If you are not there, it is impossible to know what's really going on," Nina said. The first priority in any long-distance care plan is getting "eyes on." That might be a sibling, a neighbor, or the healthcare providers who see your parent.
 But observation alone isn't enough. You also need the "so what." If Mom seems more forgetful, how do you act on it? How do you raise it with her? How do you balance her safety with her autonomy? This is where adult children often feel most alone, and where a professional with feet on the street can fill the gap.
 Challenge two: siblings who don't agree
 When Gurney hears "my brother thinks this, my sister thinks that," he takes it as a sign the family should huddle with an aging life care manager. Nina starts by validating the local caregiver, who is often overwhelmed and frustrated. She cited figures showing that 11% of family caregivers experience harm to their mental health. She added that 29% of those caring for aging relatives are also raising young children.
 Nina uses a "Share the Care" approach so distant siblings can contribute in ways they feel capable of. Susy recommended Helping Yourself Help Others , a recently updated book by former First Lady Rosalynn Carter. It suggests roles like taking over bill paying or funding weekend caregiver coverage so the hands-on sibling gets a break.
 Susy also offered a reframe: each sibling was, in a sense, born into a different family. The firstborn and the youngest experienced different parents and may feel differently about the family home. In family meetings, her team gives each person five uninterrupted minutes to share what they see and what matters most. "Frequently when we go around the room, everybody really is on the same page in many ways," she said. "It's just maybe the details that they haven't been able to agree on."
 Often, Nina added, family members may not agree with each other, but they agree to follow the advice of the professional.
 Start local, then build a team
 A practical first step is to call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 and reach the Area Agency on Aging (AAA) in each relevant location. Ask their helplines to send you everything they have.
 Then, if you're the family member taking the lead, it makes sense to start with an aging life care manager where you live, even if you'll ultimately need one near your parent. Susy confirmed that care managers routinely work in pairs across regions. One organizes everything on the departure end, and another is ready on the arrival end so the transition goes smoothly. She and Nina have even coordinated cross-country moves together.
 Care managers also work wherever "home" is: a house, a condo, assisted living or a skilled nursing facility. When a parent is already in a care community, Nina explained, the care manager can "talk shop" with staff. They can help families tell a true regulatory gap from something that simply needs supplementing. They can also serve as an objective third party making sure the community delivers what the family is paying for.
 When Mom won't consider moving
 One audience member asked how to convince an 89-year-old mother to move closer to her adult children. Both guests have heard this countless times. "We can't make anybody do anything," Susy said. But parents often open up to a professional in a way they won't with their own kids.
 In a private in-home assessment, a parent might admit, "I probably should stop driving. I don't go out in the rain or at night, but how else would I get to the doctor?" The care manager can then connect them with local transportation and other resources. As Susy put it, it's hard to take advice from children you once diapered and taught to walk. A professional is more like a trusted real estate agent: someone who listens and guides. Some adult children even pay for that initial assessment themselves to remove any barrier.
 Technology helps, but it isn't a caregiver
 Both guests see real promise in technology, with caveats. Nina pointed to simple tools like a smart speaker that announces daily reminders for medications, exercises or waking up. "But Alexa, for instance, doesn't have eyes," she said. Video calls such as FaceTime can help distant family see more of the picture. Cameras raise important privacy issues and should always be disclosed. The goal is technology that supports a person's agency, not technology that makes them feel watched.
 Susy sees many healthy older adults building smart homes with sensors that alert someone if they haven't gotten out of bed by a certain time. She expects technology to keep people at home longer and more affordably, and home care companies to adopt more of it to reduce costs. Her caution is social isolation. She reminds families of the four things that help an aging brain: good nutrition, good hydration, physical exercise and social engagement. A smart speaker can prompt a snack or a glass of water, but it probably doesn't count as social engagement.
 One attendee shared that when she was caring for her father, she installed smart speakers, doorbell cameras and more, and he loved to unplug them. Every care plan ends up customized to the person's preferences.
 That's why Gurney keeps coming back to the quarterback. You can replicate much of the assisted living lifestyle at home: services, technology, support and transportation. But someone has to manage the vendors and devices coming and going. If you're healthy and active, aging in place works just fine. When one leg of the stool gets wobbly, you need someone in charge, whether that's a family member or an aging life care manager.
 What it costs and how families pay
 Aging life care managers bill much like attorneys or accountants: their expertise is their time. Rates vary by region, just as the cost of care does. Susy shared her firm's DC-area rates as an example. Ongoing care management is $210 an hour. A one-time consultation with a senior leader is $275 an hour. Both are billed in six-minute increments. There's no minimum commitment. Some well clients get one visit a year, some quarterly, and some in crisis are seen weekly or even daily. A few practices also offer packages, such as help transitioning from hospital to home.
 Most practices, including both guests', offer a free initial consultation of about 30 minutes. Nina said it should be "meaningful and tangible," not a sales pitch. If fees are out of reach, a good care manager will point you to the local AAA, a village, or a nonprofit with a sliding scale. Many families find that a single one- to two-hour paid consult gives them enough of a roadmap to take it from there. That consult might settle which memory care community fits Mom, or which nursing facilities to target for long-term care Medicaid.
 A few payment points worth knowing:
 Long-term care insurance may include a care coordination benefit that covers a care manager. Care managers are used to reviewing these policies and can coach families through activating them, a step that often catches people off guard.
 Health insurance generally does not cover care management, so it's otherwise private pay. It may, however, qualify as a tax-deductible medical expense.
 Medicare covers acute hospital care and short-term rehab, but not custodial care like bathing, dressing and medication help. For Medicare enrollment questions, Susy refers clients to a health insurance broker.
 Medicaid planning can shape the whole search. If a parent may spend down, look for communities with a path from independent or assisted living to skilled nursing on the same campus. That skilled nursing should accept long-term care Medicaid.
 Get the documents in place, then have the conversation
 Susy called legal paperwork a "black hole" families fall into. When she asks callers whether they hold power of attorney, many answer, "Yes, I'm the executor." But an executor acts after death. Powers of attorney are used while the person is alive. The advance directive states what kind of care someone wants. A healthcare power of attorney names who decides if they can't. A financial power of attorney lets someone sign contracts, hire help or sell a house.
 Her practical advice:
 Don't just add your name to a parent's bank account. If you're ever sued, the law may treat those funds as yours. Be on the account as power of attorney instead.
 Make the POA effective immediately and keep an original. Susy shared the story of her company's founder, who held her mother's POA and died suddenly. When her brother took it over, the bank wanted an original, and no one could find one.
 Consider a trust. Both guests are seeing more families name trustees, since financial institutions tend to prefer trusts.
 Nina added the step that paperwork can't replace. If you're named as someone's healthcare agent, your job is to do what they would have wanted, not what you think is best. Too many adult children are unprepared because they never asked. What levels of medical intervention would they accept? What gives their life meaning? Would they truly want to leave home only "feet first," knowing the real cost of 24-hour care for years? "Get the documents in place," she said, "and then please have a conversation."
 Dementia directives and POLST/MOLST
 Both guests are seeing more dementia-specific advance directives. In them, people spell out the care they would or would not want after a dementia diagnosis. Susy described clients kept alive for years, unable to speak or recognize family. As Nina noted, three meals a day could feel like force-feeding to someone who never would have wanted to live that way. Being clear ahead of time helps loved ones honor those wishes, even with something as simple as "no antibiotics after a certain point."
 A DNR-type order is a separate medical document: a POLST (Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment), called a MOLST (Medical Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment) in Maryland. Without one, emergency responders will attempt resuscitation by default. Susy's tip: keep a photo of your family member's POLST or MOLST on your phone. The ER will generally accept it as a copy.
 Choosing a care manager
 Practices range from solo practitioners to larger interdisciplinary teams. Susy's firm has 15 aging life care managers and 12 concierge companions. Nina's has a team plus a dedicated on-call person every night. Susy estimated ALCA membership is roughly half solo and dual practices and half multi-member firms. Solo practitioners often have 24-hour coverage too, typically by partnering with colleagues who cover calls and vacations.
 Practice size is worth weighing. Gurney's own aunt recently interviewed aging life care managers and chose a larger practice so she'd know someone was always available. Nina recommends the list of questions to ask when hiring a care manager on the ALCA website. She sends it along with every referral she makes.
 When a loved one with dementia refuses help
 Near the end, an attendee asked what to do when a loved one with dementia denies it and refuses help, especially with family spread out. No two situations are alike, but Gurney's advice is to find two or three aging life care managers at aginglifecare.org and take advantage of their free consultations. Even if you can't afford ongoing services, you'll come away knowing the local resources. Ask them about local dementia caregiver support groups, too.
 Susy added a key insight. When dementia affects the brain, the person can't adjust their behavior, so we have to adjust our approach. Care managers spend much of their time coaching families on how to engage, whether that means accepting help at home or making a move. Language matters. One attendee found that shifting to "we're all doing this together" worked better than "let's do this." Teepa Snow's work, the Montessori approach to dementia care, and even an improv class can help. In caregiving, "yes, and" is a way of life.
 You don't have to knit the sweater alone
 Some families want to handle caregiving themselves, and Nina offered them encouragement: every community has resources, and tapping into them is a great place to start. She recalled an adult child on a flight who told her, "This is a full-time job." Not everyone can take that on. Sometimes the answer is moving a parent closer. Sometimes that isn't what the parent wants, and the question becomes how to make it work from a distance. Either way, as Nina put it, it's about knitting the sweater together so your parent gets what they need.
 Families often ask Susy where she would put her own mom. Her reply: "Have you met my mom?" Every family is different, and that's exactly why objective, personalized guidance matters.
 Resources mentioned
 Aging Life Care Association (aginglifecare.org): use the "Find an Aging Life Care Expert" search to find members near your loved one. The site also lists questions to ask when hiring.
 Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116): connects you to the Area Agency on Aging anywhere in the U.S.
 AARP Network of Age-Friendly States and Communities : more than a thousand member communities committed to supporting older adults.
 Helping Yourself Help Others by Rosalynn Carter.
 Teepa Snow's resources on communicating with people living with dementia.
 The Village to Village Network, for finding a local village.
 Chat summary
 Early reactions. One attendee said hiring an aging life care manager two months earlier was "saving my sanity." Another noted that in some regions, such as Memphis, similar professionals go by titles like senior care or elder care consultant. Not all of them belong to the Aging Life Care Association.
 Questions for the panel. Attendees asked:
 whether care managers can help someone already in a long-term care facility
 how billing works (hourly vs. retainer)
 how care managers get paid
 how to tell whether an agency or service is trustworthy, given how intimidating some contracts are
 whether dementia has different stages
 whether a photo of a POLST on your phone is accepted (the panel answered yes, and noted it's a medical document, not a legal one)
 whether chat comments would be collected and shared
 Moving a parent. One attendee asked how to persuade an 89-year-old mother who lives alone, and is mentally sharp but developing physical issues, to move closer to family. Another attendee replied that her family moved their father, who had dementia, to memory care three hours away without discussion. She acknowledged that approach doesn't fit a parent who is still cognitively sharp.
 Financial and legal tips from attendees: 
 Put planning documents in place early, especially a power of attorney.
 Look for facilities that offer every level of care: assisted living, memory care and skilled nursing.
 Confirm the facility accepts Medicaid for when private funds run out.
 Keep your own copy of DNR forms, not just the one on file at the hospital.
 One attendee mentioned the Five Wishes advance directive. Another said living through this with a mother-in-law showed how important it is to have documents signed while the person is of sound mind.
 Technology. 
 One attendee recommended Project Lifesaver, a free wandering-response program run through local law enforcement.
 Another advised setting up any technology before a parent loses the ability to learn it.
 ElliQ, a generative-AI tabletop companion for older adults, was mentioned.
 One attendee argued that the greater need is technology for caregivers, not for monitoring elders. They cited a remote nursing service and AI assistants that help families divide care duties. The pricing they mentioned is their own claim and wasn't verified.
 One caregiver shared that her father unplugged the smart speakers and cameras she installed.
 Dementia communication. A caregiver shared phrasing that worked for her family: framing tasks as "let's do this together," and opening with "I'm going through…" before asking how the parent felt. Attendees praised Teepa Snow's work. One said their organization partners with her.
