If you are pricing in-home care for a parent or spouse right now, you want one number you can trust and a way to turn it into a monthly budget. If you are still deciding which needs require paid help, this care-needs guide for elderly parents includes an ADL and IADL worksheet to map the gaps first.
Here it is: the national median for a non-medical in-home caregiver is $35 an hour, measured across 2025 by CareScout’s Cost of Care Survey. Most families land in the $30 to $45 per hour range depending on the state and the kind of help needed.
This guide gives you that snapshot up front, a plug-in estimator to convert any hourly quote into a monthly figure, real state numbers, and a plain walk-through of what Medicare, Medicaid, and VA benefits will and will not cover.
No fear, and no fine print you have to decode alone.

National median: $35 an hour. That is the 2025 median for a non-medical caregiver, up 3% from $34 in 2024 (CareScout / Genworth, March 2026).
A near-full-time week runs about $6,670 a month. At 44 hours a week that is roughly $80,080 a year at the national median (CareScout 2025 median cost tables).
Most families budget $30 to $45 an hour once state and shift factors are added in. Your local quote is what matters most.
Skilled home health is billed differently. When a doctor orders skilled nursing or therapy and your loved one qualifies, Medicare can cover it at $0, a separate track from the hourly help below (Medicare.gov).
One useful change in the data: CareScout now reports homemaker and home health aide together as a single non-medical caregiver category, because the two prices have converged (CareScout).
So the old habit of hunting for a cheaper homemaker rate mostly no longer saves money.
Reviewed July 2026 against the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey and its published median cost tables.
The Home Care Cost Estimator
Quotes come in by the hour. Budgets are monthly. This is the fastest way to bridge the two, no calculator gymnastics required.
The formulaHourly rate × hours per week × 4.33 = your monthly estimate
The 4.33 is just the average number of weeks in a month (52 weeks divided by 12), the same conversion A Place for Mom uses.
Use your own agency quote for the hourly rate. If you do not have one yet, start with the $35 national median and adjust once you make a few calls.

Treat the around-the-clock figure as a pure-math ceiling. Agencies price 24/7 and live-in care differently, so this is where quotes vary the most.
What’s Actually Included in the Rate
Before you compare two quotes, make sure they cover the same things. Ask each agency, in writing:
Nights, weekends, and holidays. These often carry a higher hourly rate.
Minimum shift length. Many agencies require a 2 to 4 hour minimum per visit.
Two-person assist. If your loved one needs two aides for safe transfers, that can double the rate for those hours.
Mileage and errands. Some rates include transportation, some bill it separately.
Backup coverage. What happens when the regular aide is sick?
Supervision and care notes. Is a nurse overseeing the plan, and will you get updates? Ask whether the quoted service includes family access to the agency’s scheduling and care-note software, or whether that access costs extra. Long-distance caregivers should also confirm who posts the shift summary and how remote relatives receive urgent changes.
Private hire (hiring an aide directly) can look cheaper per hour, but you take on payroll taxes, workers’ comp, backup coverage, and scheduling yourself. Whether you hire through an agency or directly, define how family and professional caregivers share the work before the first shift.
Once you know the hours and the number, a written caregiving plan keeps everyone on the same page about who does what and when.
What Drives the Price Up or Down
Two families in the same town can get very different quotes. These are the levers that move the number.
Type of care. Companionship and light housekeeping sit at the low end. Physical help with bathing, transfers, and toileting costs more. Review these common caregiver responsibilities before comparing quotes so each agency is pricing the same scope.
Dementia and behavioral support. Wandering, sundowning, and agitation call for trained aides and often raise the rate.
Time of day. Evenings, overnights, weekends, and holidays typically add a premium.
Two-person assist. Safe transfers for a heavier or less mobile adult can require two aides.
Minimum shifts. A 2 to 4 hour minimum means a one-hour need can still cost you several hours.
Where you live. State and metro differences are large, which is the next section.
Live-In vs 24/7 Care and How the Math Works
When one person needs help at all hours, families weigh two models, and they are not priced the same.
Live-in care means a caregiver stays in the home and is generally paid for a set daily block with built-in sleep and rest periods. It suits a loved one who is mostly stable overnight but needs someone present.
24/7 awake care means someone is alert and on duty every hour, usually two or three caregivers rotating across the day. This is the model for a loved one who wakes often, wanders, or needs repositioning through the night.
To estimate 24/7 from an hourly rate, run the full 168-hour week through the formula: 168 × $35 × 4.33 lands near $25,500 a month at the national median.
That is a math estimate, not a quote. Live-in day rates and agency 24/7 pricing frequently come in lower, so get real numbers before you panic.
When around-the-clock home care starts rivaling the cost of assisted living or memory care, that is the moment many families compare options side by side. If the goal is to age in place safely, include home modifications, transportation, and assistive technology in the same budget.

What Medicare and Medicaid Actually Cover
This is where the most expensive misunderstandings happen, so here is the plain version. The hourly help this guide has been pricing is one thing; skilled care ordered by a doctor is another, and they are covered very differently.
Home careNon-Medical Home Care
Bathing, meals, companionship, and supervision. The day-to-day help most families are searching for.
Who paysMostly private pay. Custodial care on its own is generally not covered by Medicare.
Home healthSkilled Home Health
Nursing or therapy ordered by a doctor for a homebound patient who qualifies.
Who paysMedicare pays $0 for covered home health, and an aide is covered only alongside that skilled care.
What Medicare covers. For eligible patients, Medicare pays $0 for covered home health services when a doctor certifies the need and the person is homebound (Medicare.gov).
A home health aide is covered only if your loved one is also receiving skilled care like nursing or physical therapy at the same time. Custodial or personal care by itself, the day-to-day help most families are searching for, is generally not covered by Medicare.
What Medicaid can cover. Medicaid is the largest payer of long-term care, and its Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers can fund personal care at home for those who qualify. Programs, benefits, and waitlists vary a lot by state (Medicaid.gov).
So for most non-medical, ongoing home care, plan on private pay or one of the pathways below, not Medicare.

Ways to Pay Without Draining Retirement
A $35 hourly rate is easier to face when you know the funding sources. Budgeting a recurring relief shift can also help prevent caregiver burnout before exhaustion turns into a crisis. A few worth checking:
VA benefits for veterans. The Aid & Attendance pension can add funds for a veteran or surviving spouse who needs help with daily activities (VA.gov), and the VA’s Homemaker and Home Health Aide program is part of its long-term care services.
Long-term care insurance. If your loved one holds a policy, read it closely. Many cover in-home care but require a waiting period and proof of need for help with daily activities.
Tax relief. Families may be able to use the Child and Dependent Care Credit for a qualifying adult dependent or itemize qualifying medical expenses. The IRS spells out the rules in Publication 502 and the Form 2441 instructions. Confirm your eligibility with a tax professional.
Free planning tools. Caring Village’s caregiver checklists help you gather the benefit letters, policies, and documents these programs ask for.
Getting Quotes and Building the Schedule
You do not need a perfect plan to start. You need three quotes and a schedule you can actually run. Call three agencies and ask each the same short list:
Hourly rate, plus night, weekend, and holiday rates.
Minimum shift length.
Two-person assist policy and cost.
Backup coverage when an aide is out.
Who supervises the care and how you get updates.
Whether they carry insurance and run background checks.
