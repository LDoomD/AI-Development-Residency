=== COMMENT 17 (2026-01-22T15:33:56Z, likes 26) ===
Driving: we let the Dr. be the bad guy (and we make sure we are available to take her where she wants to go). When she complains about not driving, we just remind her that the doctor said no. (He actually looked at her test results and used that to make a decision.) Looking out for when they are tired: Yes, we have learned to schedule for this! We try not to have her do too many things in one day or do things too late in the day. It was definitely a learning curve, but now that we have the rhythm of it, she is doing so much better because we try to get her home before she is too worn out, and once she gets home she relaxes. We can tell when her brain starts to "shut down" when we are out. It's not like we are out all the time...she goes to the doctor and shopping mostly. Unless she is feeling poorly, she prefers to do her own shopping. I have also learned that the shopping is partly an excuse to socialize, because she almost always runs into someone she knows to chat with!

  --- CREATOR (2026-01-11T23:14:02Z) ---
Bee, thank you for sharing, this is such a common challenge for many of my carepartners.
For many people with dementia, holding onto items becomes a way to feel safe and reduce anxiety, even when the clutter feels overwhelming to you.

A few gentle strategies that often help:

• Use forced choices:
“Hey, we need to clear a few things—would you like to keep this or that?”
This preserves a sense of control without overwhelming them.

• Do major decluttering when they’re out of the house, if possible.
If they later ask about an item, you can say something like:
“Oh, I cleaned up earlier. I can’t remember where I put it right now, but I’ll help you look after I finish dinner,”
and then calmly redirect.

The key is how and when you redirect...too fast can increase anxiety and feel dismissive, too slow can escalate distress. There really is an art to this so I’ll add this to my topic list and make a more in-depth video walking through it step by step. Hope that helps!

Ahh, Jojo, without realizing it, you're asking quite the controversial question. 😅 Neuroplasticity allows the brain to reorganize itself whereby the healthier parts of the brain compensate for the damaged areas. 

However, in dementia, this compensation has limits. Early in the illness (eg mild) we can see some compensatory strategies assist. Most dementias are progressive though, meaning that over time the very brain regions doing the compensating (the healthy parts) are also affected by the disease. As a result, the brain cannot fully reorganize or permanently take over ALL the functions that are being lost.
