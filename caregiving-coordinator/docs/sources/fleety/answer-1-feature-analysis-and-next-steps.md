## Question sent

I'm working on a Tech Fleet AI Development Residency project (Week 2-3, product management). The product helps family caregivers of an aging or seriously ill relative. Done so far: market analysis (market size, opportunity, segments), a competitor analysis of 10 direct and indirect competitors, audience segmentation with motivations, goals and use cases for two audiences, and community research from caregiver forums and app reviews. We chose "adult child caring for a parent" for the first Vision Board (MVP #1). We're now starting the feature comparison analysis. 1. What must a feature comparison analysis include in Tech Fleet's framework, and how should the "our company" row be filled before anything is built? 2. What is the next step after the feature analysis, and in what order should we do the unique value proposition, positioning statement, and Vision Board?

## Answer (verbatim)

Great question — you're at a really exciting pivot point where all that research starts turning into product decisions. Let me walk through both parts.

What a feature comparison analysis must include

Quick reframe first — in Tech Fleet's framework, this work lives inside the Continuous Discovery milestone, not as a standalone artifact. Discovery runs throughout the whole project, so you're constantly learning and refining, not doing one big research dump and moving on.

A solid feature comparison analysis needs these pieces:

A clear comparison table with competitors as rows and features as columns. You'll mark whether each competitor has the feature (yes/no), how well they do it (a simple rating works), and any notes on the execution quality.

Feature categories grouped by user need, not just a random list. Since you're serving adult children caring for a parent, you might group things like medication tracking, appointment coordination, family communication, and emotional support resources. This keeps the analysis grounded in what actually matters to your audience.

A "gaps and opportunities" section — this is where the real insight lives. Don't just list what exists. Call out where competitors are weak, where nobody is solving a known pain point, and where you could differentiate.

The "our company" row should reflect your intent, not your current reality. Before anything is built, fill it with what you plan to offer in MVP #1 based on what you've learned so far. Mark features as "planned" or "MVP" rather than "yes." This row becomes a hypothesis you'll validate — it's okay for it to change as you learn more. The point is to make your assumptions visible so the team can test them.

What comes next and in what order

Here's the recommended sequence, and I'll explain why:

Feature comparison analysis (where you are now)
Unique value proposition
Positioning statement
Vision Board

The reasoning: your feature comparison surfaces the gaps and opportunities. Those gaps directly inform what makes you unique — your UVP. Once you know what's unique, you can write a positioning statement that frames how you'll occupy a distinct place in the market and in caregivers' minds. The Vision Board comes last because it synthesizes everything — audience, problem, UVP, positioning, and MVP scope — into one aligned picture the whole team can rally around.

Skipping ahead to the Vision Board before you've nailed the UVP and positioning usually means the vision feels vague or undifferentiated. You want the board to be specific and grounded.

Your recommended next step

Finish the feature comparison with the "our company" row filled as planned-MVP features, then move straight into drafting the UVP. Want me to walk you through what makes a strong UVP in Tech Fleet's framework, or would you rather talk through how to structure the comparison table itself?
