Excellent advice already given! But before all that, something else that may buy you additional time... here is what I have found after 8 yrs of full-time caring for my own mother, who has advanced dementia now. Her dementia began at about age 92. She will be 107 in December. And as mentioned in another response, think VERY CAREFULLY whether you are up to caring for her yourself...whether you will be in 8 yrs time, as it does NOT get easier! But...back to "before all that"… Hospitals are good at some things and bad at others. Many, and probably all, suffer from inadequate and overworked staff to properly care for patient load. This leads to (usually unintentional) neglect of their patients, incomplete patient summaries in medical records and missed diagnoses. You might even be surprised and find her medical history might not even mention dementia, which is important for other staff to know! I have found that hospital staff, including doctors, often do not act as if they understand much about dementia. Family members must truly be advocates for their hospitalized loved ones, which means being a "nag" even when you sympathize with the plight of overworked staff! If you think something is "off", be kind but demand answers until you get one that makes sense. The squeaking wheel truly is the one that gets the grease in this case. While dementia manifests differently in different patients, it is typically slow-moving. You describe her as rather suddenly having become "a different person" after being relatively functional even with dementia. If stroke has already been ruled out (and they should be able to determine this), the two red flags would then be Urinary Tract Infection and /or Dehydration. Hospitals can and DO miss checking for these, especially UTI, and both of these cause remarkable mental confusion. Once treated, you may find she is able to return to life before this happened, at least for a time. Demand that she be checked. Then, if she does improve and is able to return home, invest in some indoor cams to place strategically about her house so that you can keep close tabs on her from afar (whether you live with her or not) … thus enabling her to still live somewhat independently. Let her know the cams are there and explain that the purpose is to keep her safe but to enable her to live alone as long as possible. The longer she can maintain independence and stay ambulatory, the more slowly the dementia is likely to advance. Think, "Use it or lose it." Even in their dementia, patients tend to rather adeptly hide their behaviors from others in a fight to remain independent. The cams will give you a view into what's going on when you are not there and you will gain a much clearer idea of her actual needs as dementia advances and a better sense of when it is truly time to place her elsewhere. I have been right where you are; praying for your clarity and strength.

Blondie, 2 different crisis zones (1. Rehab &2. What to do with a house) & you are blurring these as the same problem. They are NOT. The house you can put on the back burner. Focus imo needs to be about getting her into rehab and having her in rehab as long as possible 

If she is currently hospitalized AND she is discharged with orders for rehabilitation, then the discharge planner @ the hospital should be the one that looks for placement for her. Rehab usually done at a SNF aka Skilled Nursing facility with a rehab unit; although there are places that are strictly rehab only and imo you DO NOT want that. The discharge planner may ask you for the name of a place or give you a list of possibilities but really it’s on them to find a spot and submit the paperwork for this as all - !ALL! - her health chart, doctors orders, medication lists, etc are all under the control of the hospital. If she exits hospital having rehabilitation orders this will mean the billing for rehab will be a health insurance benefit. Both her hospitalization and her rehab will be a health insurance policy benefit. 

If she is 65+ then she should have Medicare. Maybe she chose to go from Original Medicare to a Medicare Advantage Plan, but she should have either Medicare are her primary health insurance. Original Medicare will cover the first 20/21 days @ 100% then up to 100 days if their health cart shows this to be what’s needed @ 50%*. The other 50% if on Original is a copay & usually done/paid by supplemental or gap or secondary policy, which covers the copay. If she does not have a gap policy then she’s responsible for the daily copay. 

If she switched from Original Medicare to an Advtange Plan, well they determine what the benefit will be. And the AP tend to be a tight and minimal as possible. Just sayin….

You want her to go into rehab and stay there as long as it is truly feasible. Each day on rehab in some way it’s being paid for by her health insurance. And it gives you another day to figure out just what the next step is best to be…… it also gives you the ability to actually get some rest. Most elderly do not ever get to the 100 days of rehab benefit. If the rehab place tell you this, they are gaslighting you. It tend to be 3-5 weeks tips for elderly rehab. But it’s still gives you time to deal with the financial aspect of IF SHE CANNOT RETURN HOME then WTF to do to deal with her home, all the stuff in it, paying the bills on it, yada yada. But right now that’s all a down the road thing.

Again the discharge planner job is to find a spot for her. Medicare absolutely will pay for rehab.

Also once you get her into rehab, do another post on her house, ok. And get some sleep!

Medicare only pays for rehab up to 100 days and they aren't guarenteed. It does mot pay for longterm care. 

To get into Longterm care you either pay privately or you apply for Medicaid. If Mom has assets other than the house, it will be hard to get Medicaid. Those assets have to be spent down. If all she has is her Social Security and maybe a pension, she may be able to get Medicaid. The house would be an exempt asset. You need to talk to a Medicaid caseworker to find out how to go about things. They are usually found at Social Service Offices.

I wish I could answer your question, "Am I fooling myself?"
I suppose it depends on what kind of help your mother needs, and for how long, and whether you are up to it.
Medicare is limited in paying for short term rehab (phys therapy) in a nursing home. Once the 20 days are up, she can pay to continue to stay, if you feel that she is benefitting and will benefit from longer physical therapy. This is the time to tap in to her savings, or sell her home, if needed for long term nursing care.

Long term care is a different category than the nursing homes which offer physical therapy, with the goal of preparing the patient to return home to live an independent life (or with family, with manageable care needs).

Yes! You can hire a "nurse for a day" - or something like that. You probably won't have much luck finding someone for one day with short-notice. But, there are home care providers which can offer a variety of services, depending on your mother's needs. It would be best to hire someone to come to the home several days a week, even if for a few hours at a time, just to establish a relationship with a care provider, so when you need to schedule an appointment for yourself, you already have your backup scheduled.

Take a tour of some long term care nursing facilities, or assisted living, depending on how independent you mother is. You may be able to find physical therapists who will come to the home, or she can go to physical therapy as an outpatient.

With her dementia, and sudden weakness, she is not likely to get much better. She will only continue to decline in ability and cognitive function. It is probably time to sell her home to pay for a care facility which can meet her needs. 

IF you feel up to it, you can of course move her in with you, or move in with her, but be prepared - it is Hard work and only gets harder!

This is going to be a lot of work but focus on one single step at a time. You’ll lose your mind if you try to plan the next years before you even have proper diagnoses.

First off you make it clear to the hospital social worker, the discharge planner, the doctor, and anyone else you can find that you are NOT taking her home. Tell them it’s UNSAFE. Ignore everything they try to tell you about how they’ll set you up with this or that it’s unsafe and you will not do it.

The hospital social worker, discharge planner, whoever will find appropriate placement. It sounds like they haven’t even figured out her needs fully yet. Often people get discharged to a skilled nursing rehab facility as a bridge between total discharge and the hospital. The person will get lots of PT and OT evaluations there. Once the evaluations are done and your loved one has made as much progress as they are likely to it’s time for discharge. Repeat the step above telling everyone she can’t come to your place because it’s unsafe. 

The rehab facility will find a placement for her and if it’s to a poorly rated and inconvenient location that’s fine. You’ll work on moving her somewhere better and that will be a hundred times easier than moving her out of your house.
