=== COMMENT 1 (2026-10-09T15:15:52.920-0400) ===
1. Sis needs to formally resign POA so your husband can make decisions. I believe this can be as easy as her writing a letter saying she resigns and having it notarized.

2. Find a proper place for dad that will take Medicaid and use his cash and the money from the sale of his house to pay for it starting now. 

3. Start the Medicaid paperwork asap once the house is sold and you can estimate how long his money will last.

4. Trust us that placing dad is not “throwing him away” or “letting someone else do all the work”. There will be a ton of work between shopping, advocating, planning, and visiting. Place him so your husband has the time and energy this work that will not end until months or years after he eventually loses his dad.

Your husband's sister can resign on the POA. Either the POA document itself will say how she does this, or the law in their original state will. Once that is done, then your husband can proceed to make the decisions on his own (with the guidance of the attorney if you choose, which is a good idea; your FIL's money can pay for the attorney since it's on his behalf). 

Your FIL won't qualify for Medicaid LTC until his assets, including his house, are spent first for his care.

=== COMMENT 5 (2026-10-08T22:31:19.080-0400) ===
PJCO, Your Father in law did not need to move in with you. When the hospital is ready to discharge, if his home (or your Sister In Law's home) is not safe for him, then the hospital must find another option. He should have been discharged to a nursing home for short-term care, or rehabilitation. Only when he is able to return home safely should he be sent home. If he is not able to return home safely, and there is no one to adequately take care of him, then it is time for long term care in a nursing home. 
Is there a secondary POA named if your SIL is unable to perform her duty as POA?
Can you or your husband contact the County Medicaid office and get that application process started? You may need to help him to complete the application. 
Direct the attorney to wherever your SIL is, whether the hospital or at home. They may just want to determine her capacity to continue as POA. Have them go to her, if she can't or won't go to them.

Attorneys are trained to assess capacity. 

If SIL doesn't want to deal with dad, she can resign POA. Then your husband remains sole POA. She should resign both mPOA and dPOA so that he has full control. The person who is in charge of the medical care must have control of the money; if not, there are hundreds of issues that can affect this, such as being in charge of medical and having to go to sister (who is mentally ill and may not have capacity to be POA) when he needs money to pay for dad's care. She may not agree that a certain level of care is necessary. She may think husband is squandering their inheritance, or she may be so unwell that she cannot function normally.

DO NOT EVER AGREE THAT YOU WILL KEEP FIL IN YOUR HOME AND BE HIS CAREGIVER. Make sure husband understands this. Being DIL of a dementia patient and having to provide the highly specialized and exhausting care he will need is a sure highway to hell. I am not making this up. (Think poopy pants and showering him when he's batting his fists at you and screaming obscenities.)

Good luck, and just say no. No, no, no, and no again!

=== COMMENT 7 (2026-10-08T20:07:57.247-0400) ===
Has your elder law attorney assessed your FIL for legal capacity (which is pretty low bar)? Or does he have a medical diagnosis yet? You will need to sort out the PoA issue first and foremost. If FIL still has the capacity to create a new PoA, this would be a required start. If he lives with you but you never get legal authority for making decisions on his behalf, it's going to be a rough ride. You will definitely need the money from the house sale. I'm assuming he is the owner or part owner of the home. 

You do not have to keep him in your home or be his caregiver but like I mentioned above you'll need some things in place before transitioning him into a facility or figuring out some other sustainable solution that doesn't include you and your husband financing his care. If he cannot create a new PoA then you may need to pursue guardianship -- and this can be expensive depending on what state you're living in.
