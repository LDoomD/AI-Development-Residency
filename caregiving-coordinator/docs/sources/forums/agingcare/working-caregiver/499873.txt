=== COMMENT 3 (2026-10-29T11:21:23.580-0400) ===
Who are you to this person? Are you a family member, a hired caregiver?

This is so common. The thought process of dementia patients rarely makes sense to the rest of us. They are often resistant, or even combative to cares, or in denial that they need to be bathed or change clothes.
Lealonnie is right, the dementia patient no longer gets to call the shots. You must change their clothes and help with bathing because you know it needs to be done.
If you are the caregiver, and you can not handle this, you may need a more experienced professional to manage their care. 
If you are looking for tips on how you can manage this, I've been doing this for 10+ years for a combative husband who is in diapers. Here's what helps; 
Maintain your own calm. If you get agitated or excited, it will prompt them to respond in a similar manner.
Calmly, but firmly, give instruction, Step-by-Step and in a matter of fact tone, not asking or pleading, and help them with each step. It may seem obvious to you, but they may not remember how to remove their pants and what to do with them. 
So, you start by helping remove their pants. If they fight you, you insist; "No, this needs to be done, and I'm here to help". "We're going to get you cleaned up, and get some new, clean pants on". You may even need to instruct them to sit down so you can pull the pants off. I'm serious. Every little move they make, you have to give instruction and help. Lead them to the bathroom, and into the shower if needed, and help to clean their skin. Don't expect the person will know how to clean themselves or that they will do so on their own. Be calm and reassuring the whole time, even when they become combative. They may feel overwhelmed and under attack, so compare it to rescuing a scared wild animal. You want to convey that you are not going to hurt them, and that you will help them. They may fight back, but only because they are scared. 

My husband has TBI and after all these years, still fights me off and is non-cooperative. I have a young, male caregiver come 3 days a week to help with diaper changes, bathing, and to get him out of bed. My husband hits him the entire time, not hard, he's just trying to prevent access. We put a thick, winter mitten on his only good hand; the kind with a strap to tighten it so it doesn't fall off. The caregiver aide calls it his "boxing glove". This keeps him from inadvertently scratching anyone, and the hits don't hurt. My husband hates being cleaned up, and yells "help me!" the entire time. We keep reminding him that after he's clean and dressed, he can go out in the living room.

This is truly serious. Will you, won't you, will you, won't you, it's the dementia dance. The bottom (!) line is that they MUST be kept clean. There is no other option. Yet often they struggle, or they hit, or they scream. And you, the caregiver, are stuck. It is your job. If you don't do it, they get infected sores. If you DO do it, you're at risk of getting hurt, or contaminated with body waste (which is hazardous and can cause disease). And your loved one hates you, when the whole point of keeping them home under your care is because you love them. You are indeed in a mess.

Think of it as a turning point. It could be the reason you find a fine facility where they are taken care of by professional caregivers. Unfortunately, it seems to be a no-no to use one's own common sense and place a loved one where they can get the very best care available. I've taken care of both parents at home while they were dying. I placed my husband. I've looked at poop from both sides now, and I can verify that professionals know what they are doing so much better than loving home caregivers. They've likely changed hundreds if not thousands of soiled pants by the time they're caring for your loved one. They can cajole, insist, order, work as an efficient team, and have their patient back in their chair in minutes. You, meanwhile, would be hiding in the shower and crying because LO screamed bloody murder when you tried to slip down his elastic waist sweatpants in preparation to clean him up, and he's still sitting in his waste and giving you the malevolent side-eye when you walk through the room. Holding your nose. The trails of your tears still wet on your face.

I often advise family caregivers to place their loved ones when they get to the point where you are now. Sometimes they leave you no other choice.
