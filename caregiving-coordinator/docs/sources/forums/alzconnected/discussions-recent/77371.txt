@[member] 

Hi and welcome. I am sorry for your reason to be here but pleased you found this place.

You've gotten a lot of good advice here already.

Is this neurologist a specialist in dementia or at a university memory center? Was any bloodwork done to specifically rule out a vitamin or hormone deficiency? Did he have any tests (Pet scan, blood test or spinal tap) to confirm Alzheimer's? Did the neuropsych point to any patterns consistent with Alzheimer's or any other type of dementia? What did "fairly heavy" drinking look like for him in terms of weekly amounts and years? 

It's pretty typical of neurologists to use MCI as a tentative diagnosis and revisit that in 6-12 months. This would especially be true in a situation where there was an addressable condition that mimics dementia. I had this scenario with both my parents. With dad, it was alcohol use. His Thiamine was borderline and he was treated with IV supplementation and told to be abstinent (he was not 100%). He did improve for a time when he couldn't access alcohol, but his new baseline was considerably lower than before. With mom, it turned out she had untreated Lyme Disease— a course of antibiotics and she improved to the point where her MoCA was normal for her age and education. 

Circling back, the alcohol piece could be in play here. My dad did have Wernicke-Korsakoff's Syndrome (WKS) which was caused by alcohol use disorder. There seems to be a genetic piece to this, like AUD it can "run in families". Dad's nephew had WKS from AUD, my younger sister had it in the months before she died from complications of HIV (she wasn't a drinker) and dad had a bunch of elderly aunts and uncles who "were senile" back in the day. One of the tests dad had to confirm his WKS was a specialized PET scan that showed glucose uptake in the brain. Two areas that showed changes for dad were the hippocampus (memory and learning) and the hypothalamus (temp and BP regulation)— these are common in WKS. The twist to dad's story is that he also had Alzheimer's and the symptoms associated with that. Your DH could have both and teasing that out can take time.  

Some things you might see if your DH does have WKS would be— an unsteady gait, eye movements, feeling cold, confabulations (false narratives based on real events) and confusion.

Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome - Wikipedia

Wernicke-Korsakoff Syndrome: Symptoms, Causes, Treatment

I can appreciate that 6 months seems like forever. That said, being sober in the timeframe and revisiting his cognition and testing in 6-12 months will give a clearer idea of what exactly your DH has going on. 

IME, the best "treatment" for whatever your DH has is to remain sober. He may regain some cognition over time. If it's Alzheimer's, he may be a candidate for infusion therapy that might give him more time (about 8 months on average) in the earlier stages.

IME, "bringing up the tough stuff" can lead to disharmony. 

Many PWD are also impacted by anosognosia which is the inability to fully recognize their deficits. They may cop to a lousy memory, but they may not be able to comprehend just how bad it is nor the other cognitive changes around empathy, apathy, executive function, judgement that also part of the disease. Keep in mind that despite all these losses, emotional intelligence persists will into the latest stages of the disease. The result? A PWD will accurately parse your mood and upset but may not be able to fully understand and process it. 

Given this, dwelling on a PWD's deficits with them and/or the changes in your lives can trigger anxiety and agitation. Most caregivers, whether spouses or adult children, find not engaging their LO in the details or heavy discussions best. I can appreciate wanting to give them a say in decisions that impact them, but sometimes that's not the kindest or safest approach.
