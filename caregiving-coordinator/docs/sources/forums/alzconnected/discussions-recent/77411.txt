=== COMMENT 1 (2026-10-24T07:00:23+00:00) ===
Hi and welcome to the forum. You need an appt with an elder care lawyer. You can search naela.org or nelf.org for one in your area and some will do consultations by video or even phone. They should be able to tell you what you can and cannot do with your current documents. It might be helpful if you took a good look at you documents beforehand. Although a will, medical and financial POAs are often drawn up at the same time, they are separate documents usually. Your financial POA might be valid and able to be used the moment it is signed and notarized or it could be what is called a "springing" POA and cannot be used unless one or two doctors agree that your husband is incompetent to handle affairs. In any case your husband gave permission for you to handle financial matters when he signed that document and you do not need his permission again. He does not need to accept that he is ill for you to do what is necessary. You will have to learn to "work around". Trying to reason is like trying to go thru a wall. There is a good you tube channel called dementia careblazers. Try searching that for "accepting help".

@[member] 

Hi and welcome. I am sorry for your reason to be here but pleased you found this place. 

Anosognosia is a common condition that rides with dementia. It's when a PWD is unable to appreciate that they have had a cognitive shift nor how it incapacitates them. They might cop to a poor short-term memory, but they generally don't see how dementia impacts higher order thinking. There is no point in trying to convince him— in his mind he's the bright and competent guy he always was. To suggest otherwise will not bring harmony to your home. 

One question. Normally Wills are a separate document from POAs and Medical Proxies/POA/Directives. The former is valid with death, and the latter expires with death. It would make sense to consult— solo— the lawyer who drew up the documents. You may have a springing POA which requires the signature of one or more physicians to attest to his incapacity to activate the POA. This can be hard for some folks to ge or not. It's more expensive, but guardianship/conservatorship is another way to legally take over the finances and decision making. Both my aunt and a friend had to do this. Both PWD had court-ordered neuro-cognitive testing and both aunt and friend prevailed. Friend's dad was a PhD scientist how had enough cognitive reserve to even find his own lawyer, but his testing convinced the judge. 

Your story is really familiar. TBH, your DH sounds a bit like dad. Dad also had some mental health issues (his psychiatrist would say bipolar but I suspect some psychopathy) which informed how dementia impacted him. His pre-dementia personality was very forceful, and he liked to be the one in control. My mom is passive by nature making for a dangerous situation. Dad made some very questionable financial moves in the early and middle stages of dementia before he was diagnosed. Dad day-traded away $360K before he finally forgot how to use his laptop. There was a period of time when he was hazy on passwords and struggling, so he would just buy a new one because, of course, it wasn't him it was the computer. A few months after he died, I discovered 5 internet security suites on auto-renew for about $300 each. Seven years after he died, I found a recurring charge on a VISA for a $10 to a company to insure a flip phone bought in 2005. Dad also went out when mom was in the hospital and bought a new car because he was felling fretful. A Ford Taurus at full MSRP. (The day he did this, he was supposed to stay in a hotel across the street. He called me from the hospital in FL to ask me in PA how to get to the hotel. It was directly across the street per google maps. Mom could see it from her room. He never did find it and slept in the car) You'll need money for his care at some point, so you need to lock things down now. 

To protect yourself and your son's future, you need to take control of finances and decisions for your household. You need to bring your POA (once active)/court papers to any banks and investment houses and have him taken off as a decision maker. Be sure to change the passwords as well. You need to lock your credit with the 3 major bureaus so he can't get a loan or credit card. Another safety issue is the internet and phone. At his stage, he's prey for scammers. At the very least, you may need to supervise internet access (changing the network password) or take away computers and smart phones. 

Getting through the middle stages with dad was the hardest part. He was unhappy and hellbent on ensuring the rest of us were as well. He even articulated how he planned to make mom miserable. He'll likely need to be on psychoactive medication if he isn't already. If he becomes threatening, he may need to be transferred to a geripsych hospital for medication management.

Hello, @[member]   Welcome to the club no one wants to join.  So sorry you find yourself here, but glad you found us.

Your DH has ‘anosognosia’ where his brain disease does not allow him to recognize his cognitive decline and limitations.  Trying to reason with him or explain things is going to backfire.  Learn what ‘work-arounds’ will help in your situation, and also start to take over all the complex tasks - finances, bills, wills, etc.  See if you can hide his wallet with important information like insurance cards, credit cards, etc.  Get a ‘dummy’ wallet for him with copies of some of that along with a little bit of cash and a gift card or two with a limited amount of money - he is open to scams if things are lost, stolen, he is online, etc.

=== COMMENT 6 (2026-10-24T14:32:29+00:00) ===
When DH nearly lost us $30,000 to a scammer, I realized that he wasn't always thinking clearly. He was still in MCI stage at that time. I locked down all the credit bureaus, got online and changed the primary account person on every financial account to me, changed passwords and ID and then talked to our broker about DH's diagnosis and that he should not be permitted to make financial decisions without talking to me. Fortunately, our broker's mother and father in law both had had ALZ so he understood what I was/am dealing with. Also, luckily for me, DH didn't shop online or even use credit cards much. If your DH is using credit cards, take them away and replace them with one that has a spending limit on it.
