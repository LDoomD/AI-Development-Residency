=== COMMENT 1 (2026-10-29T20:18:29+00:00) ===
My parents’ hospice evaluation was done by a hospice intake nurse in their assisted living apartment.  Basically it’s a check of all their vitals - blood pressure, oxygen, heart rate, weight etc.  They basically check their balance, mobility, physical condition, medical history, medications, etc.  They will ask a series of questions about how well they can/can’t take care of themselves, their activities of daily living, what their diagnoses are.  They will contact their doctor, and then send all the information to the hospice doctor - who will decide if they are eligible for hospice. 

My step-dad was accepted on the first try.  He had been losing weight, had thyroid cancer nodules in his lungs, etc.  I did not attend that evaluation because he would have said no if I had said yes to any question.  The assisted living nurse attended along with my mom( although she had dementia). 

When mom was evaluated a few months later, she was denied the first time because she wasn’t losing height and was still able to do some of her ADLs even with dementia. The assisted living resident assistant that attended did not answer the questions about needing help correctly.  Two months later, the hospice agency called to schedule a re-evaluation.  I asked a different resident assistant to attend… and she answered the questions correctly.  Mom needed help showering, picking out clothes and dressing.  I knew she was incontinent.   She was also on oxygen for COPD. 

Something to keep in mind, dementia  patients have different eligibility criteria than those people with physical illnesses.  Mom was not expected to die soon when she was accepted.

Here's what wikipedia says about the difference between palliative care and hospice care in the US: 
Palliative care versus hospice care
See also: Hospice and palliative medicine and Hospice care in the United States

The distinction between palliative care and hospice differs depending on the global context. In the United States, the term hospice refers specifically to a benefit provided by the federal government since 1982. Hospice care services and palliative care programs share similar goals of mitigating unpleasant symptoms, controlling pain, optimizing comfort, and addressing psychological distress. Hospice care focuses on comfort and psychological support, and curative therapies are not pursued.[15] Under the Medicare Hospice Benefit, individuals certified by two physicians to have less than six months to live (assuming a typical course) have access to specialized hospice services through various insurance programs (Medicare, Medicaid, and most health maintenance organizations and private insurers). An individual's hospice benefits are not revoked if that individual lives beyond six months. In the United States, to be eligible for hospice, patients usually forego treatments aimed at a cure, unless they are minors. This is to avoid concurrent care, where two different clinicians are billing for the same service. In 2016, a movement began to extend the reach of concurrent care to adults who were eligible for hospice but not yet emotionally prepared to forego curative treatments.[16]

=== COMMENT 3 (2026-10-30T02:52:30+00:00) ===
A few other notes. My experience with palliative care was that a nurse practitioner would visit monthly to monitor. They also had a social worker available to tell you about any programs you might be eligible for and to offer visits if needed. This all they provided. Palliative allows curative treatment. Hospice does not. Hopsice sends a nurse weekly to monitor and provide certain medications, supplies and equipment that Medicare pays for. There is usually a social worker and pastor available too. They provide an aide for a set number of hours which varies patient to patient.
