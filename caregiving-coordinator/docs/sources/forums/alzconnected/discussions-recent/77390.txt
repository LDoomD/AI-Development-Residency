=== COMMENT 1 (2026-10-20T23:34:23+00:00) ===
Welcome. At some point the shop will not be safe for him. He probably won’t recognize that it’s not safe and will believe he is perfectly capable. Having his shop rights there and not allowing him to use it is going to be very very difficult for both of you. How would you even stop him if running the chainsaw when it becomes too dangerous and he won’t listen. A clean break from all those reminders of things he used to be able to do but can’t, may be kinder. My mom wanted to pull weeds in the garden, reupholster the couch, paint the garage. It was a constant battle. She was very angry when we moved her to Al, but she soon stop ruminating over all these things she couldn’t do. I know this is different (and so much more difficult) being it’s your husband. Your husband, even if he seems like he is getting along ok, is probably not able to process all that needs to be considered in this situation. My husband and I live in a very rural location and I’ve often thought there is no way I could maintain everything and be so far away from everything. In my opinion, you have a rough road ahead, you should be in a setting where you feel comfortable. If you have lived there a while a move is going to be a LOT of work (I’m picturing all that my husband and I have accumulated over the years). You don’t want to have to do it in a rush while also dealing with a husband that needs your attention 24/7. I will add a few resources that might be useful if you haven’t seen them yet.

=== COMMENT 9 (2026-10-23T12:27:20+00:00) ===
@[member] 

IMO, there are 2 pieces to this. The first is you figuring out where you want to be in "stage 8" after he's passed. If, like [member], you can't imagine living out your life elsewhere, then you might look to do what's needed to safely age-in-place. Another option might be to rent the property with the intention of returning later and using the proceeds to rent or buy elsewhere. 

The second is whether it is possible for you maintain the property on your own. At some point soon (possibly already), you will be 100% responsible for every aspect of running the household in terms of maintaining the home, handling the financial pieces, and taking care of those in the house. If you have the means, you might be able to hire out things like home repair, lawn care, etc. assuming your DH isn't one of those MWD (men with dementia) who thinks he can still do it all and interferes with you doing this. 

IME, having moved my own parents (dad had dementia), a PWD can't really participate fully in the process of moving. By the time an individual is diagnosed, their empathy and executive function are impaired and reasoning for moving may be beyond them. He might not have the reasoning skills to help you decide on a new place or physically assist in the process. 

I would not be concerned about being close to his doctors unless he is doing infusions and follow-ups. If you want to move, this could be a way to sell the idea to your DH. 

I would be concerned about medical care for you. Caregiving is stressful and often has an impact on the health of the caregiver. I would be concerned about DH having access to a shop with power tools, firearms, cars and lawn equipment.

I would not be overly concerned about friends. IME, almost all friends fade into the background by the middle stages of this disease. PWD can be hard to connect with, some lose their social filters and friends typically find it hard to maintain the friendship.

=== COMMENT 12 (2026-10-25T13:49:41+00:00) ===
@[member] 

Therapeutic lies, aka "fiblets", are how most caregivers manage to make the changes they need to make to keep their LO safe. You are being gifted a couple of great options you can build on. If you want to move, using the "I want you to get the best care possible and be near the doctors" is one way to position the move so that it's not based on him being unable to maintain the home. And the TKR could be used going forward as an excuse to keep him off ladders and driving if you can get a male PT to prescribe this as a "not for a while" activity. 

Sometimes a PWD will agree to something like a move or giving up driving at some future point and then insist they didn't when the time comes. Sometimes the forget because of short term memory deficits. Sometimes, anosognosia creeps in and they are unable to perceive their impairments and deny needing to move forward with the original plan. In a year, he might not make it easy to move ahead with the plan.

I know you didn't ask, but because you brought it up. 

TKR is not a minor procedure. Two concerns which might not apply here but are worth asking about— what sort of anesthesia will be used and is this an inpatient procedure. There still seems to be quite a bit of variation around this particular procedure. Mom had both her knees done in the hospital as an inpatient with a 2-day stay because of other health concerns. I had my first knee done at a free-standing orthopedic clinic (like a hospital but no ED)— it was "ambulatory" but there was an overnight stay months after mom's second knee with the same surgeon. The second knee was truly same-day with surgery at 1pm and home on my own sofa by 4:30pm. Hospital stays can induce delirium in PWD; even mom (no dementia) had considerable agitation with her second and she's normally the model-patient. If he does stay overnight, you might plan to stay with him.

Most docs use a regional anesthesia with sedation which isn't too huge a burden on the brain. Ask if this can be the case. General anesthesia can cause progression in some people that sometimes results in a lower functioning baseline than prior. Make sure the surgeon and anesthesiologist are aware of the diagnosis. 

The standard for PT is currently 2-3 months post-surgery. Most folks I know are doing 3x weekly the first 4-6 weeks and 2x weekly for another 4-6 weeks. He'll have exercises to do at home during this time and beyond. Assuming he's cooperative, this could be something you segue into a new hobby for both of you. 

Depression is pretty common in PWD. Emotional intelligence (the ability to intuit how others feel about them) and feelings remain well into the end stages of the disease and processing or rationalizing those feelings iisn't really possible anymore which leads to depression as their world becomes smaller. In some people, men especially, depression looks like irritability. Medication can be really helpful with this. 

IME, the rate of progression seems to be different for each PWD. I don't know that depression impacts the rate. That's a good question for his doctors.

=== COMMENT 21 (2026-11-02T00:07:28+00:00) ===
My recommendation is to move as quickly as possible to independent living where meals, housekeeping, and activities are provided. My DW was diagnosed with MCI 4 years ago and AD 3 years ago. Within 6 months of the AD diagnosis, we sold our two homes and moved to an independent living community within a retirement community with nearby shopping and medical facilities. As I look back over the past 2-1/2 years, that was the perfect decision because it took the stress out of the activities of daily living. Further, as the disease has progressed, I find that I have my hands full just taking care of her and making up for the continuing brain losses. There is no respite from this disease which progresses far too quickly. Consider taking drastic action to prepare yourself! Best of luck to you.
