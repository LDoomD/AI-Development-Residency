I completely understand. I found that dementia amplified some of moms less flattering character traits. This makes things really hard. Is it mom just being mom or is it dementia? Sometimes I think it’s bit of both. Remember she can only control your life if you allow it. I made a lot of decisions that really made mom mad. I did what I needed to do and tried to accept that she wasn’t going to like it and there was nothing I could do to change that or make her understand. Indecision, anger, depression, resentment, confusion, lack of empathy and annoyance are not uncommon with dementia (off and on depending on the stage and medication). Responding, reacting and letting her emotions leach into your life isn’t good. A person in the early stages of dementia still needs a lot of supervision and limits to decision making. If she has too much control you’re  going to be an absolute wreck running around behind her fixing things. My advice is to limit her control of things. I know it’s easier said than done.

=== COMMENT 4 (2026-10-29T13:31:52+00:00) ===
Pretend like you are a professional caregiver when you are with her. Doing so may allow you to separate that you are her daughter. Doing so also makes it easier to meet every conflict with total kindness.

Identify resources for her caregiving beyond yourself, .e.g, solicit help from her friends/cousins, check with your area on aging office to see if they offer visitors free of charge, use grocery delivery to cut back on shopping together.

Identify areas of your life that are off limits. My mom was very critical, all her life, about peoples' homes. I could not take her further criticism of mine so we just didn't visit there—ever.

Learn to say no in a new way. My mom had to go to Target—no Walmart, no CVS, etc. I often just did not have the time to add a new store to our day. Folks who shop with pwd know that the trip often takes 5X as long—up and down every aisle many times. Rather than say no to Target, I would highlight that my doctor appointment would not allow for the trip. I would then be absolutely sure to find a happy trip the following day to ensure my mom saw sunshine and conversion, e.g., "Mom, I'm going to the resale shop today—want to go with?". The small resale shop meant we both could relax a bit and it would be easy to keep an eye on her.

Tell yourself over and over again that none of this is your mom's fault. There may have been trauma in the past; however, this is an entirely new ball game. Your mom is being negative because of the disease. Do seek professional counsel if issues remain from the past. I am sad to say it is too late to work this out with your mom. She needs her loved ones now and is scared.

Your husband and children 100% need your time and attention, and any care for your mom should be built around those priorities. You may need to look into placement options that will accept the funds/medicaid that she has. Nothing you do will change your mom's trajectory, but your kids need the foundation to be able to build their own lives. One of the sayings here is that we can't keep them happy; we can only keep them safe.

I recognize that supporting our elders is one of the values we want to model for our families, but not at the expense of those we do have primary responsibility for. Don't forget that you are also one of those priorities, and that you owe yourself and your family a functional, resilient mom and wife.
