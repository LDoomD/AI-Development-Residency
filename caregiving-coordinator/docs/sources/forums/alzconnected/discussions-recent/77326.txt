My partner is having increasing problems completing tasks.  For example, she is very intent on making our bed, but has now put the top sheet over the blanket rather than beneath it.  It doesn't matter to me, and I am wondering whether this is something not to bother pointing out, as this tends to provoke a range of emotions, from irritation to anger. I could also just change it, which tends to be ignored.  While this is a specific instance, these sorts of missteps are happening more frequently with previously easily completed tasks.  How are others dealing with this?  Thank you.

Hi and welcome. I am sorry for your reason to be here but pleased you found this place.

There is no "reteach" for PWD. Learning requires both working and short-term memory which are impacted fairly early in the disease.

Emotional intelligence remains intact well into the later progression of the disease which means even "gentle correction" will come with feelings she will feel acutely. 

Many PWD have anosognosia and are unable to perceive their own areas of impairment. In your wife's mind, she the high functioning and capable woman she's always been even if she does admit to problems with her memory. 

The best practice around this sort of thing is to accept that you can't change her or her disabilities— only how you react to them. I found this short read helpful in reframing these kinds of issues.

=== COMMENT 9 (2026-10-13T12:22:53+00:00) ===
I agree with all those who said not to point out the mistake. This is going to happen more and more as the disease progresses. Just thank her and let her know you appreciate the help.

The only thing I disagree with in the comments is that someone with Alzheimer's is incapable of learning anything new. I am not saying to reteach her - I agree that's not a good idea. But it's not that black and white. Every person is different. My mother certainly did or adjusted to new things throughout her progression. You do not know until you try. In my opinion, it's always worth a try. I wouldn't create situations that caused frustration on her part, but I would always try new things with her.

We had participated in a clinical trial using Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation a few years ago, which resulted in a significant improvement in expressive and receptive language, the areas most initially impacted by primary progressive aphasia.
