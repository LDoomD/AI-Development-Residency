                                   2          A.     Personal Jurisdiction over SkinSpirit Holdings
                                   3          Personal jurisdiction may be either general or specific. Goodyear Dunlop Tires
                                   4   Operations, S.A. v. Brown, 564 U.S. 915, 919 (2011) (citation omitted). General personal
                                   5   jurisdiction allows a court to hear “any and all claims against [defendants] when their
                                   6   affiliations with the State are so ‘continuous and systematic’ as to render them essentially
                                   7   at home in the forum State.” Id. (quoting Int’l Shoe, 326 U.S. at 317). Specific
                                   8   jurisdiction, on the other hand, “depends on an affiliation between the forum and the
                                   9   underlying controversy, principally, activity or an occurrence that takes place in the forum
                                  10   State and is therefore subject to the State’s regulation.” Id. (citation modified). A court
                                  11   must have either general or specific jurisdiction over a defendant.
                                  12          When a defendant challenges a court’s personal jurisdiction, the “plaintiff bears the



 United States District Court
                                  13   burden of establishing that jurisdiction is proper.” Herbal Brands, Inc. v. Photoplaza, Inc.,
                                  14   72 F.4th 1085, 1090 (9th Cir. 2023) (citation modified). On a motion to dismiss, “the
                                  15   plaintiff need only make a prima facie showing of jurisdictional facts.” Id. (citation



Northern District of California
                                  16   omitted). Plaintiffs need only present facts that, if true, would be sufficient for
                                  17   jurisdiction—any conflicts between the parties’ facts must be resolved in Plaintiffs’ favor
                                  18   at this stage. See WebGroup, 93 F.4th at 447 (citation omitted).
                                  19                 1.     General Jurisdiction
                                  20          The paradigmatic forum for general jurisdiction over a corporation is its place of
                                  21   incorporation and its principal place of business, with rare exceptions. BNSF Ry. Co. v.
                                  22   Tyrrell, 581 U.S. 402, 413 (2017) (citing Daimler AG v. Bauman, 571 U.S. 117, 137
                                  23   (2014)). Plaintiffs do not assert that SkinSpirit Holdings is from California, and rightly
                                  24   so—it is incorporated in Delaware and has its principal place of business in Washington.
                                  25   See Heublein Decl. ¶ 6. Plaintiffs only allege that SkinSpirit Holdings has “systematically
                                  26   and continually conducted business in California” as a basis for general jurisdiction. Am.
                                  27   Compl. ¶ 15. But SkinSpirit Holdings refutes this conclusory allegation by declaration,
                                  28   see Heublein Decl. ¶¶ 7–11, and in any case, “a corporation’s continuous activity of some
                                                                                      7
                                       Case 3:24-cv-09317-CRB          Document 44       Filed 10/15/25     Page 8 of 27




                                   1   sorts within a state is generally not enough to support” an assertion of general jurisdiction.
                                   2   See Williams v. Yamaha Motor Co., 851 F.3d 1015, 1020 (9th Cir. 2017) (citation
                                   3   modified). There is accordingly no general jurisdiction over SkinSpirit Holdings.
                                   4                 2.      Specific Jurisdiction
                                   5          To establish specific jurisdiction over a defendant, a plaintiff must meet three
                                   6   requirements: “(1) the defendant either purposefully directs its activities or purposefully
                                   7   avails itself of the benefits afforded by the forum’s laws; (2) the claim arises out of or
                                   8   relates to the defendant’s forum-related activities; and (3) the exercise of jurisdiction
                                   9   comports with fair play and substantial justice, i.e., it is reasonable.” Id. at 1023 (citation
                                  10   modified).
                                  11          SkinSpirit Holding’s parent-subsidiary relationship with SkinSpirit Essential (over
                                  12   whom there is certainly personal jurisdiction) cannot alone form the basis for specific

                                  18   name, phone number, “treating physician names, medical treatment appointment
                                  19   information, and medical treatment discharge dates and times” are not substantive
                                  20   “medical information” under CMIA, see Wilson v. Rater8, LLC, No. 3:20-cv-01515-
                                  21   DMS-LL, 2021 WL 4865930, at *5 (S.D. Cal. Oct. 18, 2021), Rusow alleged that the third
                                  22   parties intercepted more than that. Specifically, Rusow alleged interception of “the
                                  23   specific procedure [patients] want completed” and “the reason for the procedure.” Am.
                                  24   Compl. ¶ 83.

                                  15                 2.     Reasonable Expectation of Privacy



Northern District of California
                                  16          Next, Rusow must allege that she had a “reasonable expectation of privacy in the
                                  17   circumstances.” Hill, 7 Cal. 4th at 40. SkinSpirit again relies on Rusow’s acceptance of
                                  18   LinkedIn’s Privacy Policy to argue she did not have a reasonable expectation of privacy
                                  19   over the intercepted information. Mot. at 19.
                                  20          “Whether plaintiff has a reasonable expectation of privacy in the circumstances is
                                  21   [a] mixed question of law and fact.” Hill, 7 Cal. 4th at 40 (citation modified). As a matter
                                  22   of law, there is generally no reasonable expectation of privacy over the information
                                  23   communicated on a website. See Thomas v. Papa Johns Int’l, Inc., No. 3:22-cv-02012-
                                  24   DMS-MSB, 2024 WL 2060140, at *2 (S.D. Cal. May 8, 2024) (collecting cases). But
                                  25   there are exceptions to this rule. See, e.g., Facebook Internet Tracking, 956 F.3d at 601–03
                                  26   (concluding that a reasonable expectation of privacy existed where Facebook’s Privacy
                                  27   Policy indicated that tracking would conclude when users logged out); Brown v. Google
                                  28   LLC, 685 F. Supp. 3d 909, 941 (N.D. Cal. 2023) (concluding that users in private or
                                                                                    22
                                       Case 3:24-cv-09317-CRB         Document 44      Filed 10/15/25     Page 23 of 27




                                   1   incognito mode had a reasonable expectation of privacy); Calhoun v. Google LLC, 526 F.
                                   2   Supp. 3d 605, 630 (N.D. Cal. 2021) (recognizing that a defendant’s representations can
                                   3   give rise to a reasonable expectation of privacy).
                                   4          Here, the question is whether a reasonable user would expect privacy against
                                   5   unwanted disclosure of her administrative and health information on SkinSpirit’s website.
                                   6   Such a reasonable expectation can exist where the third parties “gained ‘unwanted access
                                   7   to data by electronic or other covert means, in violation of the law or social norms.’ ”
                                   8   Facebook Internet Tracking, 956 F.3d at 601–02 (quoting Hernandez, 47 Cal. 4th at 286).
                                   9   Courts frequently hold that patients have a reasonable expectation of privacy over their
                                  10   medical information. See, e.g., Los Angeles, 65 Cal. App. 5th at 643–45 (recognizing such
                                  11   a reasonable expectation); Lewis v. Superior Ct., 172 Cal. Rptr. 3d 491, 504 (Ct. App.
                                  12   2014) (same), aff’d and superseded, 3 Cal. 5th 561 (2017); L.A. Gay & Lesbian Ctr. v.



 United States District Court
                                  13   Superior Ct., 194 Cal. App. 4th 288, 308 (2011) (same). This Court also recognizes the
                                  14   same reasonable expectation between a prospective or current patient and her medical
                                  15   provider, notwithstanding the general rule about websites.



Northern District of California
                                  16          SkinSpirit does not contest this point. It only argues that Rusow gave up this
                                  17   reasonable expectation by assenting to LinkedIn’s Privacy Policy, and it makes no
                                  18   argument as to Google. See Mot. at 19. But just as LinkedIn’s broadly phrased third-party
                                  19   policy does not constitute consent under CIPA, it also does not mitigate Rusow’s
                                  20   otherwise reasonable expectation of privacy over the information she input on SkinSpirit’s
                                  21   website. See Meta Pixel, 647 F. Supp. 3d at 800 (holding that Meta’s general privacy
                                  22   policy, which did not disclose Meta’s collection of health data, did not affect the plaintiffs’
                                  23   reasonable expectation of privacy); Microsoft, 2023 WL 8780879, at *9 (rejecting a
                                  24   nonparty’s broadly phrased privacy policy as evidence against a reasonable expectation).
                                  25          Accordingly, the Court rejects SkinSpirit’s argument and concludes that Rusow has
                                  26   alleged a reasonable expectation of privacy.
                                  27                 3.     Highly Offensive Invasion of Privacy
                                  28          Finally, Rusow must allege that SkinSpirit’s intrusion was “highly offensive.”
                                                                                      23
                                       Case 3:24-cv-09317-CRB          Document 44      Filed 10/15/25      Page 24 of 27




                                   1   Facebook Internet Tracking, 956 F.3d at 601. Because this question is fact-intensive,
                                   2   “courts must be reluctant to reach a conclusion at the pleading stage about how offensive
                                   3   or serious [a] privacy intrusion is.” Facebook Consumer Priv., 402 F. Supp. 3d at 797
                                   4   (citation omitted). In determining the “offensiveness” of an invasion of a privacy interest,
                                   5   courts may consider “the degree of the intrusion, the context, conduct and circumstances
                                   6   surrounding the intrusion as well as the intruder’s motives and objectives, the setting into
                                   7   which he intrudes, and the expectations of those whose privacy is invaded.” Hill, 7 Cal.
                                   8   4th at 26 (citation omitted).
                                   9          It is true that “courts in this district have consistently refused to characterize the
                                  10   disclosure of common, basic digital information to third parties as serious or egregious
                                  11   violations of social norms.” Meta Pixel, 647 F. Supp. 3d at 801 (citation modified). But
                                  12   Rusow alleges that SkinSpirit disclosed more than mere “basic digital information”—she



 United States District Court
                                  13   alleges that third parties intercepted her medical information. Am. Compl. ¶¶ 83, 89.
                                  14          In response, SkinSpirit cites no cases where the disclosure of medical information
                                  15   was not a serious invasion of privacy—and the authorities it does cite do not support its



Northern District of California
                                  16   position. Hammerling, Low v. LinkedIn Corp., 900 F. Supp. 2d 1010 (N.D. Cal 2012), In
                                  17   re Google, Inc. Privacy Policy Litigation, 58 F. Supp. 3d 968 (N.D. Cal. 2014), In re
                                  18   iPhone Litigation, 844 F. Supp. 2d 1040 (N.D. Cal. 2012), and Williams v. DDR Media,
                                  19   LLC, No. 3:22-cv-03789-SI, 2023 WL 5352896 (N.D. Cal. Aug. 18, 2023), did not
                                  20   involve medical information. All involved browsing history or similar information.
                                  21          In re Ambry Genetics Data Breach Litigation, 567 F. Supp. 3d 1130 (C.D. Cal
                                  22   2021), is more on point. There, the plaintiffs alleged that defendant Ambry Genetics
                                  23   disclosed “names, dates of birth, Ambry account numbers, health insurance information,
                                  24   confidential medical information, medical diagnoses, billing information including
                                  25   addresses, email addresses, and telephone numbers, and Social Security numbers.” Id.
                                  26   at 1138. The court held that the allegations sufficiently stated a “highly offensive” breach
                                  27   of social norms because of the sensitive nature of the data involved. Id. at 1143 (citation
                                  28   omitted); see also Stasi v. Inmediata Health Grp. Corp., 501 F. Supp. 3d 898, 926 (S.D.
                                                                                      24

                                  10          E.     Electronic Communications and Privacy Act (Wiretap Act)
                                  11          Rusow and T.S. allege that SkinSpirit violated the ECPA. Am. Compl. ¶¶ 126–48.
                                  12   The ECPA creates a cause of action against any defendant who intercepts communications



 United States District Court
                                  13   without consent. 18 U.S.C. §§ 2511(1), 2520(a). Unlike CIPA, a party to a
                                  14   communication (like SkinSpirit) cannot be liable under § 2511 as an accomplice.
                                  15   § 2511(2)(d). A party can only be liable if he intercepts the communication “for the



Northern District of California
                                  16   purpose of committing any criminal or tortious act” (the crime-tort exception). Id. So, the
                                  17   principal question here is whether Plaintiffs have alleged “sufficient facts to support an
                                  18   inference that the offender intercepted the communication for the purpose of a tortious or
                                  19   criminal act that is independent of the intentional act of the recording or interception
                                  20   itself.” B.K. v. Eisenhower Med. Ctr., 721 F. Supp. 3d 1056, 1065 (C.D. Cal. 2024) (first
                                  21   emphasis added) (citation omitted), reconsideration granted in part on other grounds, No.
                                  22   5:23-cv-02092-JGB-DTB, 2024 WL 2037404 (C.D. Cal. Apr. 11, 2024).
                                  23          The crime-tort exception’s “focus is upon whether the purpose for the
                                  24   interception—its intended use—was criminal or tortious.” Sussman v. Am. Broad. Cos.,
                                  25   186 F.3d 1200, 1202 (citation modified). SkinSpirit points to Plaintiffs’ allegations that
                                  26   undermine any assertion that its purpose in intercepting data was criminal or tortious.
                                  27   Reply at 13–14 (citing Am. Compl. ¶¶ 82, 139). These paragraphs allege that SkinSpirit’s
                                  28   purpose was “to monetize that data for targeted advertising.” Am. Compl. ¶ 82; accord id.
                                                                                     25
                                       Case 3:24-cv-09317-CRB        Document 44       Filed 10/15/25     Page 26 of 27




                                   1   ¶ 139. Even if SkinSpirit in fact committed an invasion of privacy tort, as Rusow alleges,
                                   2   Plaintiffs do not allege that it did so for the express purpose of committing that tort. See
                                   3   Sussman, 186 F.3d at 1202–03 (“Where the purpose is not illegal or tortious, but the means
                                   4   are, the victims must seek redress elsewhere.”).
                                   5          Allen v. Quicken Loans Inc., No. 2:17-cv-12352-ES-MAH, 2018 WL 5874088
                                   6   (D.N.J. Nov. 9, 2018), is on point and persuasive here. There, defendant Quicken Loans
                                   7   installed third-party tracking software on its website to obtain useful summaries of user
                                   8   activity. Id. at *1–2. The court rejected application of the crime-tort exception because
                                   9   Quicken Loans did not have the “purpose of facilitating some further impropriety, such as
                                  10   blackmail.” Id. at *5 (quoting Sussman, 186 F.3d at 1203). Unlike CIPA’s aiding
                                  11   liability, which does not require an accomplice to know his acts are illegal, the ECPA
                                  12   requires exactly that knowledge and purpose. Plaintiffs’ own allegations acknowledge that



 United States District Court
                                  13   such knowledge is lacking here.
                                  14          Furthermore, Plaintiffs’ arguments “plainly fail because the alleged tortious or
                                  15   criminal activities are not independent from the intentional act of intercepting; they are the



Northern District of California
                                  16   interception itself.” See id. at *6 (citing In re Google Inc. Cookie Placement Consumer
                                  17   Priv. Litig., 806 F.3d 125, 145 (3d Cir. 2015)). For the crime-tort exception to apply,
                                  18   Plaintiffs must allege that SkinSpirit had an “independent criminal or tortious purpose at
                                  19   the time” the interceptions were made. See Planned Parenthood Fed’n of Am., Inc. v.
                                  20   Newman, 51 F.4th 1125, 1135–36 (9th Cir. 2022). Here, Plaintiffs allege no independent
                                  21   criminal or tortious intent; they accordingly fail to state a valid claim under the ECPA.
                                  22          The Court accordingly GRANTS Defendants’ motion to dismiss the ECPA claim,
                                  23   with prejudice.
