13                                        SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

14           Plaintiff’s SAC re-asserts her claim under Section 631(a) of CIPA and asserts a new claim

15   against SkinSpirit under Section 632 of CIPA. These claims fail as a matter of law and should be

16   dismissed with prejudice.

17           Both claims are time-barred because Plaintiff has again failed to plead facts showing she

18 discovered the alleged violations within the applicable one-year statute of limitations, disregarding
19   this Court’s prior order to do so.

20           Plaintiff’s newly asserted claim under CIPA Section 632 independently fails because the

21   statute does not expressly recognize derivative aiding and abetting liability, the software at issue

22   does not constitute a “device,” and Plaintiff’s communications were not made under circumstances

23   that satisfy Section 632’s “confidentiality” requirement, as interpreted by courts.

24           Finally, Plaintiff’s re-asserted Section 631(a) claim also fails because Plaintiff has not

25 alleged that any third party attempted to learn the contents of her communications “while in transit,”

26 a prerequisite for finding that SkinSpirit aided a violation of that section.

27           For these reasons, the Court should dismiss all of Plaintiff’s CIPA claims with prejudice.

 3          This lawsuit arises from Defendant SkinSpirit Essential LLC’s (“SkinSpirit”) alleged

 4   integration on www.skinspirit.com (the “Website”) of widely used analytical and advertising tools

 5 provided by LinkedIn and Google. According to Plaintiff, SkinSpirit’s use of these tools enabled

 6   LinkedIn and Google to collect and use “click event” data related to her use of the Website’s online

 7   appointment booking feature to serve her targeted advertisements related to cosmetic procedures.

 8          Plaintiff initially sued SkinSpirit for violations of the Electronic Communications Privacy

 9   Act (“ECPA”), the California Confidentiality of Medical Information Act (“CMIA”), the California

10   Invasion of Privacy Act (“CIPA”), and the California Constitution. See Compl. (ECF No. 1). Shortly

11   after SkinSpirit moved for dismissal, however, Rusow filed an amended complaint adding a new

12   plaintiff, T.S., a new claim under Washington’s My Health My Data Act (“MHMDA”), and two

14   (the “Vistnes Entity”). See First Am. Compl. (“FAC”), ECF No. 14.

18   Id. at 7–11. The Court also dismissed Plaintiff’s CMIA, ECPA, CIPA, and MHDMA claims, while

25   Website “within one year of her complaint[.]” Id. at 13 n.7.

16                                               ARGUMENT

17              To survive a motion to dismiss, a plaintiff must plead facts from which a court can

18   reasonably infer that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged. Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S.

19   662, 678 (2009). A pleading is insufficient if it offers mere “labels and conclusions” or “a

20 formulaic recitation of the elements of a cause of action.” Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550

21 U.S. 544, 555 (2007); Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678; see also Ass’n of Gen. Contractors of Cal., Inc. v.

22 Cal. State Council of Carpenters, 459 U.S. 519, 526 (1983) (holding it is improper to assume

23 plaintiff “can prove facts that it has not alleged” or that laws were violated in ways not alleged).

24         I.     Plaintiff Has Failed to Satisfy Her Burden of Pleading That She Saw Targeted
                  Advertisements Within One Year of Filing Suit and, Thus, Her CIPA Claims Are
25                Time-Barred.

26              Plaintiff asserts claims under Sections 631(a) and 632 of CIPA. Both are subject to a one-

27 year statute of limitations. See Order at 12 (citing Javier v. Assurance IQ, LLC, 649 F. Supp. 3d 891,

28 901 (N.D. Cal. 2023)); see also Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 340.

 1           Plaintiff alleges she visited the Website on May 12, 2023. SAC ¶ 7. She initiated this lawsuit

 2 on December 20, 2024—more than one year and seven months after visiting the Website. See

 5           Plaintiff seeks to invoke California’s delayed discovery doctrine to revive her untimely CIPA

 6 claims. “For the delayed discovery rule to save her otherwise untimely claim[s], [Plaintiff] must

 7 plead facts showing “(1) the time and manner of discovery and (2) the inability to have made earlier

 8 discovery despite reasonable diligence.” Order at 12 (quoting Fox v. Ethicon Endo-Surgery, Inc., 35

 9 Cal. 4th 797, 808 (2005)). “Under this rule, [Plaintiff]’s accrual of her CIPA claim is delayed until

10 she had inquiry notice of the cause of action.” Order at 12 (citing Fox, 35 Cal. 4th at 807). Plaintiff

11 “surely had inquiry notice when she began seeing ads[.]” Order at 13 (quoting Saedi v. SPD Swiss

10      II.     Plaintiff’s Newly Asserted Claim Under Section 632 of CIPA Independently Fails.

11            Section 632 makes it unlawful to “intentionally and without the consent of all parties to a

12   confidential communication, use[ ] an electronic amplifying or recording device to eavesdrop upon

13   or record the confidential communication.” Cal. Penal Code § 632(a).

14            Here, Plaintiff concedes—as she must—that SkinSpirit did not directly violate Section 632.

15   See SAC ¶ 155 (alleging that “the Third Parties [Google and LinkedIn] eavesdropped and/or

16   recorded confidential communications through an electronic amplifying or recording device in

17   violation of § 632 of CIPA”); see also Turner v. Nuance Commc’ns, Inc., 735 F. Supp. 3d 1169,

18   1181–82 (N.D. Cal. 2024) (“[A] party cannot ‘eavesdrop’ on their own conversation under the first

19   prong of section 632. Only a third party can do that.” (citing Thomasson v. GC Servs. Ltd. P’ship,

20   321 F. App’x 557, 559 (9th Cir. 2008)). Rather, Plaintiff alleges that SkinSpirit should be “liable for

21   aiding violations of Section 632 by LinkedIn and Google.” SAC ¶ 158 (emphasis added).

22            Plaintiff’s claim fails because: (A) neither Google nor LinkedIn used an “electronic

23   amplifying or recording device” to eavesdrop on or record her “confidential” communications, as

24   courts have interpreted those terms; and (B) Section 632 does not expressly recognize a derivative

25   claim for aiding and abetting and, even if it did, Plaintiff does not allege that SkinSpirit acted with

26   the requisite knowledge or intent to be held liable under such a theory.

20           B.      Section 632 Does Not Expressly Recognize a Derivative Aiding and Abetting Claim.

21           Even if Plaintiff could allege facts sufficient to establish that Google or LinkedIn violated

22   Section 632, her claim against SkinSpirit would still fail, because “[u]nlike the fourth clause of

23   California Penal Code § 631(a), the text of Penal Code § 632 does not expressly provide for

24   derivative liability.” Smith v. YETI Coolers, LLC, 754 F. Supp. 3d 933, 943–44 (N.D. Cal. 2024).

25   Rather, to state a plausible aiding and abetting claim, Plaintiff would need to bring a claim under

26   California Penal Code § 31, which states that “[a]ll persons concerned in the commission of a crime

27   ... or [who] aid and abet in its commission ... are principals in any crime so committed.”

12 Section 632 by LinkedIn and Google.” SAC ¶ 159. This conclusory allegation falls short of what is

21 clauses apply “only to eavesdropping by a third party and not to recording by a participant to a

22 conversation.” Warden v. Kahn, 99 Cal. App. 3d 805, 811 (1979). Because it is undisputed, here,
