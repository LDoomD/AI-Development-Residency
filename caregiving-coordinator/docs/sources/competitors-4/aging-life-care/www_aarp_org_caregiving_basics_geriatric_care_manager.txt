They go by different titles﻿ ﻿— most commonly geriatric care managers, eldercare consultants and﻿﻿, for those who are members of the Aging Life Care Association﻿, aging life care managers. ﻿Usually licensed nurses﻿, social workers or therapists trained in caring for older adults, they generally serve clients and families whose incomes are too high to qualify for publicly financed services.﻿ These clients often reach out in times of crisis, “when a diagnosis or complex medical situation has turned their world upside down,” says Nina Herndon, president of the Aging Life Care Association in Tucson, Arizona. “Our purpose is to bring calm to the chaos.” Most Popular ARTICLE CONTINUES AFTER ADVERTISEMENT Establishing a human connection with both the family caregiver and their loved one is one of the most important parts of a care manager’s job. An in-person assessment gives them the opportunity to observe the home environment, understand family dynamics and identify potential problems that might not come up during a phone call or video visit. They may notice loose rugs that increase the risk of falls , unopened mail piling up, an empty refrigerator or subtle signs that memory or mobility has declined. Those observations help ensure families hire the right type of help rather than paying for services they don’t need. "A lot of families will say: ‘We need to hire a nurse for mom.’ I always stop and ask them why they think that,” says Colleen Duewel, founder of LionHeart Eldercare & Consulting in Falls Church, Virginia. “Maybe they don’t need a nurse, which will be costly over time. Instead, maybe the individual needs a personal companion to spend time with them and ensure they are safe.” Get More From AARP MEMBERS ONLY View MEMBERS ONLY View MEMBERS ONLY View Along with assessments, other care management services may include: Evaluating, arranging for and monitoring hired caregivers, such as home health aides. Coordinating medical appointments and arranging transportation. Identifying social services and programs that could help the care recipient. Making referrals to financial, legal or medical professionals and suggesting ways to avert problems. ﻿ Conducting home safety inspections to help identify potential hazards and suggest modifications. Explaining complex or difficult topics to care recipients and their families. Creating short- and long-term care plans that could include assisted living or nursing homes.﻿ Coordinating hospital discharge plans to return home or move to a rehab facility. Acting as a liaison to families who may be hundreds of miles away. Answering questions and addressing emotional concerns of caregivers and their loved ones. Arranging for relief or respite care for stressed-out caregivers.﻿ “We’re the project managers for care. We start by asking what’s important to the caregiver, and then we bring together all the right professionals and make sure everything works together,” says Duewel. “Our specialized experience and local connections can speed families through the roadblocks they often encounter.” Count on paying out of pocket Professionals charge by the hour, ranging from $150 to $300, depending on the geographic region. Many begin with an initial assessment of 4 to 6 hours, which may require a one-time charge. Some also charge for mileage and travel time. Employee assistance programs may cover some care management fees because the services can help family caregivers stay focused on their paying jobs and miss less work time. ﻿﻿9 questions to ask before you hire a care manager Be clear about your expectations when hiring a geriatric care manager.
