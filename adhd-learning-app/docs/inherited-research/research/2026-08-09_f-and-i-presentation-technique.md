## Executive summary

Seven things worth knowing before you build the sessions.

| # | Finding | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The menu is **three pages, not one**. Terms page → product page → accept/decline page. That structure is near-universal in trade press. | High |
| 2 | **Column count is genuinely contested.** Three-column and four-column both have named advocates. There is no industry consensus. Teach the logic, not a number. | High (that the conflict exists) |
| 3 | The strongest "don't sound like a salesman" idea in the trade press is **tell, don't sell**: present every product with the same flat energy, disclose, then stop talking. | Moderate |
| 4 | **Needs discovery happens before the menu, not on it.** Multiple named trainers agree the menu should *close* a conversation, not open one. | Moderate |
| 5 | **Adaptive selling has real meta-analytic support. "Customer orientation" mostly does not.** Adjusting your approach per customer predicts objective performance. Merely *caring* about the customer predicts only how you rate yourself. | High |
| 6 | **The "but you are free to decline" technique has collapsed under replication testing.** A 2013 meta-analysis said it works. A 2023 pre-registered re-analysis found no effect in the low-bias studies. Do not teach it as science. | High |
| 7 | **Role-play has real evidence — but only certain kinds.** Behaviour-modelling training works best with *bad* examples shown alongside good ones, and with scenarios the learner writes himself. | High |

The single biggest gap: **there are no controlled studies of F&I presentation technique.** None. Everything in Sections 1, 2, 4 and the practitioner half of 5 is practitioner opinion published in trade press, mostly by people who sell training or menu software. Treat it as craft knowledge. Treat Sections 3 and the science half of 5 as the only actual evidence.

## Section 1 — Menu presentation mechanics

**Plain language first.**

| Jargon | What it means |
|---|---|
| **The menu** | A printed or on-screen sheet showing the protection products a customer can add to the deal, usually grouped into packages. |
| **Column / tier** | One package option on the menu. Four columns = four package choices. |
| **Base payment** | The monthly payment with **no** products added. The payment the customer already agreed to in sales. |
| **Accept/decline page (A/D page)** | The last page. Two lists: what the customer took, what they turned down. The customer signs it. |
| **Penetration** | The share of customers who buy a given product. |
| **PVR** | Per vehicle retail. Average profit per car. |
| **VSC** | Vehicle service contract. The extended warranty. |
| **300% rule** | Offer 100% of products, to 100% of customers, 100% of the time. |

### 1a. The three-page structure

This is the most consistent finding in the whole brief. Multiple independent trade sources describe the same skeleton.

| Page | What is on it | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Terms page** | Vehicle price, down payment, trade, **base payment**, term, APR. Customer **initials** it. | Proves the customer knew they could drive away without buying anything. Makes every product visibly optional. |
| **2. Product page** | Every product offered, with features and benefits. Final terms with products selected. | This is where the presentation happens. |
| **3. Accept/decline page** | Two columns: accepted vs declined. Base payment **and** final payment shown. Individual product prices. Statement that products are optional and do not affect the rate. Customer signs. | Documents the choice. This is the page that exists because of the law. |

Sources:

- Penelope Bell, Associate, Automotive Compliance Education — "Read This Menu," *F&I and Showroom*.
  `https://www.fi-magazine.com/375813/read-this-menu`
  Gives the three pages by name and lists the required contents of each. Sample word track quoted: *"These are the terms you agreed upon in sales, and you can take delivery with approved credit."*
  **Grade: High** for the structure.
- Gil Van Over, Executive Director, Automotive Compliance Education; founder, gvo3 & Associates — "Menu Best Practices," *F&I and Showroom*.
  `https://www.fi-magazine.com/370558/ace-space`
  Describes it as a **two-step** process: close the sale and cover features/benefits on the first page, then the accept/decline page with two columns. *"The base payment on the presentation page is initialed by the customer."* Product prices must match across every document. Base **and** final payment both appear on the A/D page.
  **Grade: High.**
- Dave Gibbs, Training Manager, EFG Companies — "A New Approach to Menu Presentations."
  `https://efgintelligence.com/dealershipcorridor/a-new-approach-to-menu-presentations/`
  Same requirement: customers review, initial and sign; every presentation covers base payment, term, rate, and full cost detail per product.
  **Grade: Moderate** — vendor-published, but consistent with the two ACE sources.

**Note the two-step vs three-page wording.** Van Over says two steps. Bell says three pages. They are describing the same thing with different granularity — Van Over folds the terms line into "the presentation page." Not a real conflict.

### 1b. Why the menu exists at all (this is the "why it's ordered that way" answer)

Thomas B. Hudson, partner at Hudson Cook LLP — "What's on the Menu?", *F&I and Showroom*.
`https://www.fi-magazine.com/articles/whats-on-the-menu`

> "Menu presentations have been developed, in part, to provide a way for the dealer to prove that the purchase of ancillary products was voluntary."

He also notes products are "often grouped into marketing packages" with names like Platinum, Gold, Silver, descending to lesser tiers.

**This is the load-bearing sentence for Session 1.** The menu's shape is not a sales invention. It is a proof-of-voluntariness document that a sales process was later built on top of. That explains the initialed base payment, the two-column A/D page, and why every product must be individually priced.

Gil Van Over frames the same point as a dual purpose — the menu is *both* sales tool and compliance record, and the industry argues about which comes first: "The Big Menu Debate."
`https://www.fi-magazine.com/351192/the-big-menu-debate`
> "If I can lay this type of paper or digital trail out in front of a Dark Side antagonist, it is difficult to dispute that the consumer reached and agreed to an informed decision."

**Grade: High.**

### 1c. The column count debate — sources genuinely conflict

There is **no industry consensus**. Here is who says what.

| Source | Position | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Rudi Hynek, *Agent Entrepreneur*, 2016 `https://www.agent-entrepreneur.com/342276/structuring-a-better-menu` | **Four columns** | 4–6 products total. Every product in column 1. Each column to the right drops at least one product. **VSC in all four columns** because it is the most profitable. **VSC alone in the final column**, to protect it. **GAP only in column 1**, so the easy sale doesn't cannibalise the rest. Price descends left to right. |
| Mark Virag, management consultant, Virag Consulting, 2018 `https://viragconsulting.blog/2018/06/26/best-practices-for-menu-selling/` | **Four columns** | Column 1 shows every product appropriate to the deal (cap 6–8; use bundles beyond that). Column 1 drives the A/D waiver layout. Column 4 payment ≈ **$30 above base payment**. Payments step up gradually right-to-left. Show individual product prices, not just package prices. Show prices as a **daily** amount. |
| Product Prep (Gerry Gould's platform) `https://prodprep.com/blog/post/the-best-way-to-present-the-fi-menu/` | **Three columns** | Worry-Free (products listed individually) / Practical (bundled, for most customers) / Core Basic Coverages. |
| Paul Brown, VP, Ascent Dealer Services, in *CBT News* `https://www.cbtnews.com/fi-menu-strategies-that-drive-trust-and-profit/` | **No mandated format** | Avoid more than four columns. Pick a layout you can present comfortably. Cap at **4–5 bundles** to reduce decision fatigue. |
| Anonymous practitioner critique surfaced in the same search corpus | **Against four** | The complaint: with four columns "the last three columns have no meaning," producing pre-programmed objections; and a rigid four-column paper menu can't handle a customer who wants only one product out of a two-product column. **Grade: Extrapolated** — I could not attribute this to a named author, only to search-summary text. Treat as a real-sounding objection, not a citation. |

**What to teach Orlando:** the *rule* is not "three" or "four." The rules that all sources actually share are:

1. Column 1 contains everything. That is what the A/D page is built from.
2. Each column to the right removes something.
3. Price descends across the columns.
4. Do not exceed four or five choices — decision fatigue.
5. Customers pick a **plan**, not a product. (Hynek: *"Be sure your finance managers are asking the customer to select a 'plan' rather than a 'product.'"*)

**Grade: Moderate** for rules 1–4 (multiple independent named practitioners). **Moderate** for rule 5 (single named source).

### 1d. Best option first, descending — status

Hynek's descending-price structure is consistent with it, and anchoring is separately well-replicated (Many Labs — already in the prior file, graded High). But no F&I-specific evidence exists.

### 1e. Payment vs cash presentation

| Claim | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Present the same way regardless of finance, lease or cash. Paul Brown calls this the **"400% rule"** — 100% of products, to 100% of customers, 100% of the time, in 100% the same way. | Brown, *CBT News* | Moderate |
| Show **total cost first**, monthly impact only after the baseline is confirmed. Rationale given: when customers hear only monthly payments, many assume prices are being hidden. | Brown, *CBT News* | Moderate — plausible mechanism, no data |
| Show prices as a **daily** amount for consumer-friendly perspective | Virag | Moderate |
| Deal terms shown should include cash selling price, trade allowance, payoff, down payment, and — if quoted — payment, term and rate | ACE / *F&I and Showroom* | High |
| Cash customers: emphasise not having to dip back into savings for an unexpected repair | Product Prep blog | Extrapolated — vendor blog, no data |

**Direct conflict worth flagging:** Brown says total-cost-first. Virag says daily-payment framing. Those pull in opposite directions. Both are named practitioners in trade press. Neither has evidence. This is exactly the kind of thing Orlando should decide by trying both and watching what happens.

### 1f. Timing — sources conflict badly

| Source | Recommended length |
|---|---|
| Finance Manager Training `https://financemanagertraining.com/the-best-fi-menu-presentation/` | **~2 minutes** total, opening numbers through packages |
| Paul Brown, *CBT News* | **3–4 minutes** |
| Product Prep blog (cash-customer article) | **3–5 minutes** |

All three are selling training. Range is 2–5 minutes. **Grade: Moderate** that it should be short. **Not covered** on the specific number.

Brown's caveat is the useful part: *"When we shortcut our presentation process and information given to customers, we also shortcut their ability to comfortably say yes."* So: short, but not rushed.

### 1g. The 300% rule — what it is and why it's contested

| | |
|---|---|
| **Definition** | Offer 100% of products, to 100% of customers, 100% of the time. |
| **Origin** | Per Thomas Hudson: a case where an elderly couple was never offered credit life insurance. The husband died shortly after. The widow sued, claiming the dealership had an obligation to offer it. Dealers then started using express accept/decline forms. `https://www.fi-magazine.com/310174/300-percent-rule-examined` |
| **Why practitioners like it** | Consistency evidence. Everyone got the same offer. |
| **Hudson's objection** | Offering a product the customer **cannot use** creates its own exposure. His example: offering a service contract on a 120,000-mile car when the coverage excludes vehicles over 100,000 miles. |

**Grade: High** on the definition, origin story and Hudson's objection (all from a named attorney with 39 years in the field, in trade press).

There is also an *Automotive News* piece titled "A case for breaking the 300% rule" — **I could not fetch it (403).** Its existence confirms the rule is debated. I am not reporting its argument.

**Teaching point:** the 300% rule and needs-based selling are in tension. Present everything (300%) vs present what fits (needs-based). Hudson's mileage example is the clean illustration of where 300% breaks.

## Section 2 — The "don't sound like a salesman" problem

This is the core question. Here is what named practitioners actually do differently.

### 2a. The techniques, by name

| Technique | What it is | Who teaches it | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tell, don't sell** | Present each product as *disclosure*, not as a pitch. Same energy for every product. Brief. Then stop. | Gerry Gould, then Director of Training, United Development Systems — "Mastering the Menu," *F&I and Showroom* `https://www.fi-magazine.com/310788/mastering-the-menu` | Moderate |
| **"Talk to them, not at them"** | Deliberate informality to lower resistance. Avoid appearing "overly aggressive." | Gould, same article | Moderate |
| **Don't sell until they say no** | The presentation phase contains no persuasion at all. Persuasion only starts if there is an objection. | Gould — "Becoming the Ideal F&I Manager," *F&I and Showroom* `https://www.fi-magazine.com/309911/becoming-the-ideal-fi-manager`: *"They never sell — at least, until the customer says 'No.'"* | Moderate |
| **Benefits held in reserve** | Present products concisely. Deploy benefits **only when an objection arrives**. | Paul Brown, Ascent Dealer Services, *CBT News* | Moderate |
| **Permission language / softening** | Ask before redirecting. Gould's example: *"That may not be the best choice, as there are other choices I would like to share with you. May I?"* | Gould, "Mastering the Menu" | Moderate |
| **The pattern interrupt opener** | Name the customer's expectation out loud and refuse it, before presenting. Brown's line: *"I'm not going to sell you a bunch of things you don't need. What we find is that there's a handful of products that our customers love."* | Brown, *CBT News* | Moderate |
| **The "free no"** | Open the objection phase with a disarming question — *"Do you have any questions about the options I just shared with you?"* — so the customer's reflex "no" is spent on a harmless question instead of on the products. | Gerry Gould / Product Prep `https://prodprep.com/blog/post/how-to-overcome-any-objection-in-fandi/` | Extrapolated — vendor blog, no data. The associated "30% will still choose a product after saying no" figure is **unverified** and should not be repeated. |
| **Telling information, not selling information** | Put product detail on the dealership website so the F&I office is not the customer's first exposure. | John Tabar, VP of Training, United Development Systems / Brown & Brown Dealer Services — "Trainers in the Spotlight," *F&I and Showroom* `https://www.fi-magazine.com/363961/trainers-in-the-spotlight-learning-from-2020-to-succeed-in-2021`: *"Put more information about the F&I products on their website — not selling information, just telling information."* | Moderate |
| **Reframe the role from seller to explainer** | Shift the goal from "selling" to "helping customers clearly understand why F&I products matter." | Rick McCormick, National Director of Training, Reahard & Associates — "F&I Training Fundamentals," *F&I and Showroom* `https://www.fi-magazine.com/digital-cover-features/fi-training-fundamentals` | Moderate |
| **Shopping-cart close** | Let the customer physically select products themselves, then produce the payment from their selection. | Product Prep blog | Extrapolated |
| **The "if money weren't a concern" question** | For a hesitant customer, ask which options they *would* choose if cost were removed. Separates product objection from price objection. | Product Prep blog | Extrapolated |

### 2b. Needs discovery — the strongest structural idea

Three independent named sources agree on the same thing: **the menu should be the end of a conversation, not the start of one.**

- **Dave Gibbs, EFG Companies:** treat the menu as *"a natural conclusion to a benefits presentation and discussion, tailored to the needs of your customer."* And: *"Think of your menu more as a logical end to the process and a closing tool"* rather than an opening position. His stated reason — customers reflexively reject a menu-first presentation before they have heard any benefit.
  `https://efgintelligence.com/dealershipcorridor/a-new-approach-to-menu-presentations/`
- **Gerry Gould:** uses an interview-based needs discovery to customise the presentation. Sequence he describes: meet them in their comfort zone → congratulate them on the purchase → review driving habits → transition.
  `https://www.fi-magazine.com/309911/becoming-the-ideal-fi-manager`
- **Paul Brown:** questions build credibility and identify which products actually align with usage patterns.

**Framing products around actual usage** — commute length, lease vs buy, how long they keep cars — appears in the trade press as a principle but **I found no named source giving a specific discovery script.** The clearest statement of the idea traced only to search-summary text, not to a fetchable named article.

**Grade: Moderate** that needs-discovery-before-menu is real practitioner consensus. **Not covered** for a specific, sourced discovery question list. Orlando will have to build his own from the products his store sells.

### 2c. The unresolved tension in this whole section

Every source above is a trainer, a training company, or an insurance/product vendor. They all have a commercial interest in the claim "there is a better way and we teach it."

None of them present outcome data. The only outcome figures anywhere near this material are single-dealer testimonials from a menu-software vendor (already covered and graded Moderate in the prior file).

**So: this is craft consensus, not evidence.** Which is fine. But Session 2 should say so out loud.

## Section 3 — What the persuasion science actually supports (new findings only)

Cross-checked against Section C of `2026-08-08_f-and-i-resources.md`. Cialdini's seven principles, anchoring, framing, defaults and social priming are already covered there and are **not repeated**. Three genuinely new items.

### 3a. Adaptive selling vs customer orientation — the most useful new finding

**Franke, G. R., & Park, J.-E. (2006). Salesperson Adaptive Selling Behavior and Customer Orientation: A Meta-Analysis. *Journal of Marketing Research*, 43(4), 693–702.**
`https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkr.43.4.693`

Scale: **155 samples, more than 31,000 salespeople.**

| Behaviour | Predicts what |
|---|---|
| **Adaptive selling** — changing your approach based on the customer in front of you | Self-rated performance, **manager-rated** performance, and **objective** performance |
| **Customer orientation** — genuinely prioritising customer needs over the close | **Only self-rated** performance |

Both increase job satisfaction. Reciprocal tests indicate adaptive selling *causes* customer orientation, not the reverse. Selling experience increases performance but not satisfaction. Reported correlations across 28 relationships ranged from −.16 to .35; 19 were significant.

**Why this matters for Orlando's exact question.** "Don't sound like a salesman" is usually taught as *be more customer-oriented*. This meta-analysis says the customer-orientation part mainly changes how you feel about yourself. The part that shows up in the numbers is **adaptation** — reading this specific customer and changing what you do. That is an argument for needs discovery on performance grounds, not just ethical ones.

**Grade: High** for the finding as stated. **Caveats to state honestly:** correlational, cross-sectional, mostly B2B sales, 2006. The effect sizes are modest (max r = .35). It does not prove that training adaptiveness produces performance.

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of adaptive selling exists in *Industrial Marketing Management* (`https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0019850125001324`) but **I was blocked (403) and could not read it. Not reported.**

### 3b. "But you are free to decline" — this one failed

This is important because a softened, pressure-free close is exactly what Orlando wants, and there is a well-known technique that claims to deliver it.

| Study | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Carpenter, C. J. (2013). A Meta-Analysis of the Effectiveness of the "But You Are Free" Compliance-Gaining Technique. *Communication Studies*, 64(1), 6–17.** `https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10510974.2012.727941` | 42 studies. Concluded the technique effectively increases compliance in most contexts. |
| **Fillon, A., Souchet, L., Pascual, A., & Girandola, F. (2023). The Effectiveness of the "But-you-are-free" technique: Meta-analysis and re-examination of the technique. *Meta-Psychology*, 7.** `https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/2640` | Pre-registered. 52 experiments, N = 19,528, 74 effect sizes. Headline effect **g = 0.44 [0.36, 0.51]**. **But** only **seven** studies were low risk of bias — and among those, **g = 0.11 [−0.18, 0.40]. No effect.** Reproducibility indices were "critically low": R-index 9.77%, Z-curve expected discovery rate 6%. Most recent studies are too underpowered to detect Carpenter's effect. |

One moderator did survive in the full (biased) set: the effect was stronger for **face-to-face** interaction than other formats. That is the F&I context. But it is a moderator inside a literature whose baseline effect does not survive bias correction, so it cannot carry weight.

**Grade: High** confidence that this technique is **not** evidence-backed.

**What to do with it.** Saying "this is completely optional, and you can decline any of it" is still the right thing to do — it is honest, and on the accept/decline page it is *required* anyway. Just do not teach it as a persuasion lever that increases yes rates. It is a transparency practice, not a technique.

**Flag for the whole project:** this is the same failure pattern as power posing and social priming, already flagged in the prior file. Any sales-training source that cites BYAF, "the freedom-to-choose technique," or reactance-reduction scripts as proven should be downgraded on sight.

### 3c. The blemishing effect — relevant to disclosure-style presentation, but fragile

**Ein-Gar, D., Shiv, B., & Tormala, Z. L. (2012). When Blemishing Leads to Blossoming: The Positive Effect of Negative Information. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 38(5), 846–859.**
`https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/38/5/846/1796852`

Finding: adding a **small dose of negative information** to an otherwise positive product description can make people *more* favourable toward it. Four studies, lab and field.

Two strict boundary conditions, both stated in the paper:

1. It only works when the information is processed **effortlessly**, not effortfully.
2. The negative info must come **after** the positive info, not before.

**Why it's tempting here:** it looks like scientific backing for "tell them what the product doesn't cover." Naming an exclusion is exactly the disclosure-style move Orlando is drawn to.

**Why I am grading it down:** an F&I customer signing a five-figure contract is the definition of **effortful** processing — which is the condition under which the paper says the effect *disappears*. It is also a single-lab 2011/2012 consumer-psychology finding from the era whose replication record is poor, and I found no independent replication.

**Grade: Extrapolated.** Disclose exclusions because it is honest and because it survives the customer finding out later — **not** because science says it sells more. Do not present the blemishing effect as support in Session 2.

### 3d. Nothing else new

I looked for evidence on: needs-discovery questioning frameworks, "consultative selling" as a trainable construct, and sales-pressure effects on satisfaction. Nothing peer-reviewed and specific enough to grade. **Not covered.**

## Section 5 — Practice and rehearsal

Split this into two very different halves.

### 5a. The evidence half — this is genuinely solid

**Taylor, P. J., Russ-Eft, D. F., & Chan, D. W. L. (2005). A meta-analytic review of behavior modeling training. *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 90(4), 692–709.**
`https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ936609` (full abstract fetched and verified)

117 studies. Behaviour modelling training = watch a model perform the skill, then practise it, then get feedback. That is what F&I role-play is.

Findings that matter for Orlando:

| Finding | What it means for practice design |
|---|---|
| Effects were **largest for learning, smaller for job behaviour, smaller still for results** | Role-play reliably teaches the skill. It moves the numbers less. Expect that. |
| Effects on **declarative knowledge decayed** over time, but effects on **skills and job behaviour stayed stable or increased** | Memorised product facts fade. Practised delivery does not. Prioritise reps over reading. |
| Skill development was greatest when **learning points were used and presented as rule codes**, and when **training time was longest** | Name the moves. "Tell don't sell." "Same energy each product." "Hold benefits until objection." Short labels, then drill. |
| Transfer was greatest when **mixed (negative and positive) models** were presented | **Watch bad presentations on purpose, not just good ones.** This is the most counterintuitive and most actionable finding here. |
| Transfer was greatest when practice included **trainee-generated scenarios** | Orlando should write his own customer scenarios rather than only using canned ones. |
| Transfer was greatest when trainees were **instructed to set goals** | Set an explicit goal before each rep. |
| Transfer was also greatest when **superiors were trained** and when **rewards/sanctions existed at work** | Outside his control. Note as a known limit, not a to-do. |

**Grade: High.** Large, peer-reviewed, directly about the training method in question. **Caveat:** it is about behaviour-modelling training generally, not about F&I. The transfer to a menu presentation is an inference — a reasonable one, but an inference.

## What to build into Session 1 and Session 2

### Session 1 — "The menu: what's on it and why it's ordered that way"

**The one sentence the session hangs on:**
> The menu was invented to prove the customer chose freely. Everything about its shape follows from that.

**Teach in this order.**

| Beat | Content | Source strength |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Why it exists.** Hudson's line: menus exist "to provide a way for the dealer to prove that the purchase of ancillary products was voluntary." Tell the credit-life-insurance widow story — it explains the accept/decline page in thirty seconds and it sticks. | High |
| 2 | **The three pages.** Terms (base payment, initialed) → products → accept/decline (two columns, base and final payment, individual prices, "optional, doesn't affect your rate"). Draw it. Don't describe it in prose. | High |
| 3 | **What's on it.** VSC, GAP, prepaid maintenance, tire & wheel, dent — grouped into named tiers. Column 1 holds everything; each column right drops something; price descends. Customers pick a **plan**, not a product. | Moderate |
| 4 | **The column-count fight.** Show the three-column and four-column camps side by side as a table. Say plainly: no consensus exists, both have named advocates, use what your store gives you. Teach the five shared rules instead of a number. | High (that it's contested) |
| 5 | **The 300% rule and its limit.** Definition, origin, why it protects you — then Hudson's 120,000-mile service-contract example showing where it breaks. | High |
| 6 | **Cash and lease.** Same presentation, same order, same energy. Brown's 400% framing. Flag the total-cost-first vs daily-payment conflict as an open question for him to test. | Moderate |

**Do not teach in Session 1:** premium-first-anchoring as fact (Extrapolated), a specific presentation length (2–5 min, sources conflict), any penetration or PVR benchmark (the prior file already discarded most of those).

### Session 2 — "Delivering it without sounding like a salesman"

**The one sentence:**
> Presentation and persuasion are two different phases. Most people sound like a salesman because they fuse them.

**Teach in this order.**

| Beat | Content | Source strength |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Name the split.** Phase 1 = disclosure. Phase 2 = objection handling. Gould: *"They never sell — at least, until the customer says 'No.'"* Brown: benefits are held back until an objection arrives. This single structural idea does most of the work. | Moderate |
| 2 | **Discovery goes before the menu.** Gibbs: the menu is the *logical end* of the conversation, not the opening position. Gould's transition sequence: comfort zone → congratulate → review driving habits → transition. Orlando builds his own question list from his store's products. | Moderate |
| 3 | **The four delivery rules.** Tell, don't sell. Same energy for every product. Brief and to the point. Talk to them, not at them. Present these as *rule codes* — Taylor et al. found short named rules produce the best skill gain. | Moderate technique / High on the format |
| 4 | **Language moves.** Permission-asking ("May I?"). The pattern-interrupt opener. The free no. Give each a name so it's drillable. | Moderate / Extrapolated — label the weak ones as weak |
| 5 | **Why "adapt" beats "care."** Franke & Park: adaptive selling predicts objective performance; customer orientation predicts only your own self-rating. Feeling like a nice guy is not the skill. Reading *this* customer and changing what you do is the skill. | High |
| 6 | **A myth to kill on purpose.** "But you are free to decline" is taught in sales training as a proven lever. Fillon et al. 2023: no effect once you look only at low-bias studies. Say it out loud anyway — because it's true and it's on the A/D page — but never expect it to raise a yes rate. Use this as the session's calibration lesson: sales training cites failed science constantly. | High |
| 7 | **Practice protocol** (this is the deliverable, not homework): <br>• Set an explicit goal before each rep. <br>• Write your own customer scenarios — long commute, lease-return, cash buyer, 120k-mile trade. <br>• Watch **bad** presentations deliberately, alongside good ones. Mixed models produced the best transfer. <br>• Record yourself. Review it. <br>• Drill the rule codes, vary the sentences. Structure fixed, wording free. <br>• Expect skill to hold and product facts to fade — so re-read products, but re-*run* the delivery. | High (Taylor et al.) |

**Say out loud in Session 2:** every trainer quoted sells training, none of them publish outcome data, and the only real evidence in the session is the two meta-analyses. Orlando asked for sourced material specifically because the last version had none. Showing him where the sourcing runs out is part of the product.
