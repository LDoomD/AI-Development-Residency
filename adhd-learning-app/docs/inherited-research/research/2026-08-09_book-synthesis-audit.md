# Research Brief: Popular-Science Books vs. Peer-Reviewed Literature

**Prepared for:** ADHD/Dyslexia Learning-System build
**Date:** 2026-08-09
**Status:** RESEARCH TIER — reviewed via two independent adversarial fact-checks, corrections applied
**Scope:** Audits Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Duhigg's *The Power of Habit*, Clear's *Atomic Habits*, Brown/Roediger/McDaniel's *Make It Stick*, Ericsson's *Peak*, and Newport's *Deep Work* against literature this project has already verified and cited.

---

## Executive Summary

None of these six books should be cited as primary evidence in the build plan — they are secondary/popular sources, and in three cases (Kahneman, Duhigg, Ericsson) the flagship anecdotes have been publicly walked back or substantially revised by the original researchers or by subsequent meta-analysis. That said, they are not equally valuable:

- **Make It Stick** is the closest thing to a "safe to cite" popularization — it is authored by the same lineage as Karpicke, Bjork, and Roediger already in this project's corpus, and its main net-new contribution is **framing and vocabulary** ("desirable difficulties," "generation effect," "interleaving" made accessible) rather than new evidence. Nothing in it should be treated as an independent evidentiary source; it should be treated as a plain-language index into work already cited.
- **Atomic Habits** contributes one genuinely underrepresented, actionable mechanism not yet in this project's cited base: **habit stacking / implementation intentions as an if-then cue**, which is empirically grounded in Gollwitzer's work (not yet cited here) rather than in Clear's own claims. That's worth pulling in as a *primary* citation (Gollwitzer & Sheeran meta-analyses), not as "Atomic Habits says."
- **The Power of Habit**'s cue-routine-reward loop is real (Graybiel's striatum research) but Duhigg's "keystone habits" and corporate anecdotes (Starbucks, Alcoa/O'Neill, Pantene) are narrative journalism, not tested constructs — no dose-response or generalizability data exists for "keystone habits" as a category. Its main use here is as a **design lens for the STUDY habit itself** (cue design, friction reduction), not as evidence.
- **Thinking, Fast and Slow** is scientifically important for System 1/2 as a framing device for self-assessment bias, but its own author has publicly walked back confidence in the social-priming chapter (Ch. 4) — a useful cautionary tale for how *this project's own AI examiner agent* should treat confidently-stated-but-unreplicated claims. Overconfidence/availability-bias framing is solid; specific study citations from the book should not be reused without checking replication status.
- **Peak** overclaims relative to Ericsson's own 1993 paper (already cited) — the "10,000 hour rule" popularized in Gladwell's *Outliers* (drawing on that same 1993 study) is explicitly disowned by Ericsson himself, and Macnamara/Hambrick/Oswald 2014 (already cited in this project) is the correct counterweight, showing deliberate practice explains a domain-dependent share of skill variance that is largest in games/music/sports and much smaller in education and professional skill — the domains closest to this build (see Findings, below).
- **Deep Work** is the weakest evidentially of the six — it is a *synthesis/opinion* book with almost no original citations of controlled studies, and its core claims (attention residue, "deliberate practice for the mind") lean on Sophie Leroy's attention-residue work (real, but a narrow lab paradigm) and on Ericsson by reference (see above caveats). For an ADHD build specifically, Newport's model of long undistracted blocks is in tension with ADHD attention-regulation literature already in this project (e.g., Sibley et al. 2026, Alderson et al. 2013) which favors externally-structured shorter/scaffolded sessions over willpower-based deep-focus blocks — flag as a **design conflict**, not free confirmation.

**Bottom line for the build:** these books are useful as UX/motivation language and as pointers to primary literature, several of which (Gollwitzer implementation intentions, Lally habit-automaticity timeline, Wood's habit-cue taxonomy) are genuinely new primary sources this project should formally cite instead of the popularizations. None should be cited as evidentiary support in place of the peer-reviewed base already assembled.

## Genuinely New and Actionable (candidates to formally add to the project's cited base)

These are primary sources surfaced *through* the popular books but not yet in this project's verified corpus. Recommend citing the primary source directly, not the popular book, if adopted:

1. **Gollwitzer (1999); Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006) meta-analysis (d≈0.65), and — pending independent verification — Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer's 2024 follow-up meta-analysis of 642 tests.** The 2006 finding is the strongest fully-confirmed actionable result in this audit. Directly informs: designing the STUDY-session trigger as an explicit if-then cue ("After I [existing anchor habit], I open the study app and do one retrieval block") rather than relying on motivation or willpower. This is the primary mechanism underneath Clear's "habit stacking." **Note:** this cue should be implemented as a behavioral/environmental trigger tied to an existing routine step (e.g., "after I sit down with coffee"), not as an app-generated push notification or reminder — the latter would conflict with this project's existing no-notification/reminder-based-adherence decision.

2. **Lally et al. 2010 (habit automaticity timeline).** Median 66 days to automaticity among those who succeeded, actual range 18–254 days, missed single days don't meaningfully derail progress. Actionable: design the habit-formation UX to (a) not treat "66 days" as a hard target, (b) explicitly reassure users after a missed day that automaticity isn't reset — as in-app/dashboard state, not a push notification, to stay consistent with the no-reminder decision — and (c) expect and plan for a multi-month runway, especially relevant given ADHD's documented higher variance in routine-establishment.

3. **Wood & Neal (2007, *Psychological Review*) and Neal, Wood & Quinn (2006) — habit-cue research (context-stability over reward-salience; five cue categories: time, location, emotional state, preceding action, other people).** Actionable: when designing the STUDY cue, prioritize a stable time/place/preceding-action anchor over reward/gamification mechanics — this also dovetails with existing FSRS/spaced-repetition scheduling already in the project, since the cue design is what gets the learner to the point of opening the review queue at all.

4. **Kahneman's own 2017 comment walking back the priming chapter (Schimmack, Heene & Kesavan 2017 R-Index analysis), treated as a case study — not a citation, a design lesson.** Actionable for the AI examiner/grading agent specifically: build explicit anti-overconfidence guardrails into the grading rubric (require the agent to cite the specific rubric criterion it's scoring against, not just an intuitive "feels right" judgment), since LLM graders are structurally prone to the same fast-pattern-match overconfidence Kahneman flagged in System 1 — and prone to it in a way that is not self-correcting the way a human expert's "planning fallacy" awareness might be.

5. **Explicit design conflict to resolve, not adopt uncritically: Newport's long-block deep work vs. the project's already-cited ADHD attention-regulation literature (Sibley et al. 2026; Alderson et al. 2013).** Action item: the build plan's session-length/focus-block architecture should be derived from the ADHD-specific sources already cited, using Deep Work only as a vocabulary source ("attention residue," "shallow work") for user-facing copy, not as a scheduling model.

## What This Means for the Build Plan

- **Formally add three primary sources to the project's cited corpus**: Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006) implementation-intentions meta-analysis, Lally et al. (2010) habit-automaticity timeline, and Wood & Neal (2007)/Neal, Wood & Quinn (2006) habit-cue taxonomy. Use these — not the popular books — as the citations behind the STUDY-session trigger design.
- **Design the STUDY-session trigger as an if-then habit stack anchored to an existing routine step** (time/place/preceding-action), per Gollwitzer and Wood. Implement the cue and any "you haven't lost progress" reassurance as in-app/dashboard state, never as a push notification — this keeps the recommendation compatible with the project's existing no-reminder/no-notification-based-adherence decision.
- **Do not target "66 days" as a habit-formation milestone.** Build the onboarding/streak-adjacent UX (to the extent any exists, given the project's gamification stance) around an 18–254 day expected range, and explicitly do not penalize or flag a single missed day.
- **Do not use deliberate practice (Peak) as a rationale for expecting large gains from practice volume alone in this build.** Macnamara et al.'s full domain breakdown shows deliberate practice explains the least variance in education and professional-skill domains — the closest analogs to this project — so the build should keep leaning on the already-cited retrieval-practice/spacing/desirable-difficulties literature rather than importing a "practice harder, longer" framing from Peak.
- **Reject Deep Work's long-undistracted-block model as the default session architecture.** It is written for neurotypical knowledge workers and doesn't address ADHD attention regulation. Keep using the already-cited ADHD-specific literature (Sibley et al. 2026; Alderson et al. 2013) to justify shorter, externally-structured, scaffolded sessions instead.
- **Do not reuse any specific study or anecdote from Ch. 4 of Thinking, Fast and Slow, "keystone habits" from The Power of Habit, or the "10,000 hour rule" from Peak/Outliers anywhere in the project, including motivational copy** — all three are either non-replicated, anecdotal, or explicitly disavowed by their own originating researchers.
- **Treat all six books as UX/vocabulary sources at most** ("desirable difficulties," "cue-routine-reward," "attention residue," "if-then plans"), never as evidentiary support for a design decision. Where a book's claim is actionable, cite the primary literature behind it instead of the book.

## Adversarial Review Notes

This brief was checked by two independent adversarial fact-check passes before finalizing. Both passes agreed the analytical judgment (downgrading all six books to non-evidentiary status, correctly identifying genuinely new primary sources, and not re-citing the already-locked corpus) was sound, but found citation-integrity problems that required correction:

- **Fabricated direct quotation, fixed.** The draft presented an invented composite sentence in quotation marks as Kahneman's verbatim 2017 statement. It has been replaced with his actual wording ("I placed too much faith in underpowered studies... they cannot be as large and as robust as my chapter suggested"), sourced to his comment on Schimmack's "Reconstruction of a Train Wreck" post. The underlying claim — that Kahneman publicly walked back Ch. 4 — was true and is retained.
- **Cherry-picked statistic, fixed.** The draft cited only the three highest-variance domains from Macnamara/Hambrick/Oswald 2014 (26% games, 21% music, 18% sports) while silently omitting education (~4%) and professional skill (<1%) — the two domains most analogous to this project's academic-study use case. The full breakdown is now reported, and the "What This Means for the Build" section explicitly notes that deliberate practice explains the *least* variance in the domains closest to this build, which strengthens rather than weakens the brief's original conclusion to prefer Macnamara's tempered estimate over *Peak*'s framing.
- **Unattributed statistic, fixed.** The "11 of 12 priming studies scored below the reliability threshold" claim is now attributed to its source, Schimmack, Heene & Kesavan (2017), rather than presented as a bare, unnamed "independent analysis."
- **Anonymous-looking citation, addressed with a caveat rather than dropped.** The two fact-check passes disagreed about the "2024 meta-analysis of 642 tests" figure: one pass verified it as real (Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer, *European Review of Social Psychology*, "The When and How of Planning") and confirmed the effect-size range; the other flagged it as unverifiable and recommended dropping it because it lacked a named author, matching a common hallucination pattern. Given the disagreement, this brief keeps the claim but now attaches full author names and journal, and explicitly flags it in two places as pending independent DOI verification before the build plan treats it as a confirmed citation. The well-established Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006) d≈0.65 finding, which both passes independently confirmed, is presented as the primary, fully-confirmed support for implementation intentions regardless of how the 2024 figure resolves.
- **Missing pinpoint citations, added.** Wendy Wood's habit-cue research was cited only as "Wood's peer-reviewed program" with no paper named; it is now attributed to Wood & Neal (2007, *Psychological Review*) and Neal, Wood & Quinn (2006), per one reviewer's recommendation.
- **Minor mischaracterization, clarified.** The draft implied the "10,000 hour rule" was popularized "around" *Peak* itself; it is now clarified that Gladwell's *Outliers* (2008) — not *Peak* (2016) — primarily popularized the rule, and that Ericsson wrote *Peak* substantially to correct that reading.
- **Notification-policy ambiguity, addressed.** Both reviewers flagged that the habit-cue and "reassure after a missed day" recommendations sit close to this project's locked no-notification/no-reminder-based-adherence decision. The brief now explicitly states these should be implemented as behavioral/environmental cues and in-app/dashboard state, not push notifications.
- **No changes made to:** the duplication check against the already-cited corpus (both reviewers found it clean), the contradiction check against locked project architecture/UX decisions (both found no direct contradiction), or the overall per-book grading and bottom-line recommendation, all of which both fact-checks endorsed as-is.
