## Executive Summary

The project's dyslexia research so far answers "what CSS should I ship" and stops there. That's a narrow slice of the problem. Adult dyslexia is not primarily a font problem — it's a **word-level decoding and orthographic-processing deficit that persists into adulthood** even in compensated, high-functioning readers (including college-educated adults), layered with (for this user specifically) **ADHD-driven working-memory and attention deficits that compound rather than merely coexist with the reading deficit**, and a documented risk of secondary anxiety/depression from a long history of reading-related failure.

Five things are missing that matter more than typography:

1. **What actually breaks, mechanistically, in adult dyslexic reading** (phonological + orthographic, not "one or the other," and it doesn't go away with age — it gets masked by compensation).
2. **Structured/multisensory literacy instruction (Orton-Gillingham and descendants) is a *children-learning-to-decode* evidence base**, and the best-verified evidence for it (a 2021 meta-analysis) found **non-significant** effects on foundational reading skills. There is essentially **no RCT evidence for OG-style methods applied to adults who already read** and are trying to learn *new domain content* (e.g., F&I sales), as opposed to adults being taught to decode for the first time. This is an evidence gap the build should not paper over by borrowing children's-literacy methodology wholesale.
3. **Content structure/authoring implications are a distinct lever from CSS** — chunking by meaning unit, morphology-first vocabulary introduction, sentence-complexity ceilings, and glossing are things you write into the Markdown, not the stylesheet. This is the single biggest concrete gap and the most actionable one.
4. **Text-to-speech has real but modest evidence** (a meta-analysis effect size around d=.35 — not the "fixes reading" claim vendors imply), and it hasn't been studied specifically for *adults learning new technical/professional material*, which is exactly this system's use case. That's a real evidence gap to name honestly, not paper over.
5. **Dyslexia+ADHD comorbidity has its own literature** showing additive/compounding executive-function deficits (not just "two separate issues") — this is directly relevant to a user with both, and argues for smaller work units and offloading working-memory load onto the interface, not just accommodating reading. (Note: one of the two studies behind the "three EF subprofiles" framing is a small, exploratory case series — see §5 confidence note.)

Overall skepticism flag: **popular dyslexia-remediation and accommodation industries oversell weak evidence across the board** — coaching-for-dyslexia-at-work has "good practice resting almost entirely on professional judgment," not evaluation studies; the best-verified OG meta-analysis finds *non-significant* effects; TTS's effect size is real but small. The build should adopt the *mechanisms* the evidence actually supports (reduce decoding load per unit, reduce working-memory load, use morphology/chunking, offer read-aloud as a modest aid not a cure) rather than buying into any branded "dyslexia intervention" product claim.

## Findings by Subtopic

### 1. Orthographic processing and phonological decoding deficits in adult dyslexia

The dominant explanatory model (phonological-deficit hypothesis) is real but incomplete on its own — phonological difficulties (phonological awareness, phonological processing speed, phonological memory) are the most consistently observed deficit in dyslexia, but they **do not fully account for the variability observed**, including in adults (Carroll et al., 2025, Delphi consensus study, *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry*). A convergent multiple-deficit view is now the field's consensus position, not a single-cause phonological story.

Orthographic processing — the ability to form and retrieve letters, letter sequences, and spelling patterns — is separately and commonly impaired, and recent adult-specific work using lexical-decision tasks found evidence that some adult dyslexic readers show an **orthographic deficit distinguishable from a purely phonological one** (2024 study contrasting phonological vs. visuo-attentional accounts in adults, PMC11178288). A 2025 sentence-reading study using behavioral and neural (ERP) measures in adults with dyslexia likewise separated phonological, orthographic, and semantic processing streams and found dissociable patterns (ScienceDirect, 2025).

Critically for this build: **this is not a childhood-only phenomenon that resolves with skill.** A meta-analysis of adults with dyslexia across multiple orthographic systems (**Reis, Araújo, Morais et al., 2020**, *Annals of Dyslexia*) found reading-related skill deficits persisting into adulthood relative to matched controls, even in populations who have reached functional literacy and post-secondary education. The 2025 IDA-endorsed revised definition of dyslexia (published in *Annals of Dyslexia*, "Defining Dyslexia: 2025 Revision") reinforces that dyslexia is understood as a persistent, lifelong difference in word-level reading efficiency and spelling, not something "grown out of."

**Skeptic's note:** don't build around a single mechanism. The literature has moved past "it's all phonological" — a build that only addresses phonological decoding (e.g., only sounding-out aids) is solving half the adult problem. Orthographic pattern recognition (word shape, spelling regularities, morpheme recognition) needs separate support.

**Confidence: high.** This section's most load-bearing citations were independently verified by both fact-checks with no discrepancies.

### 2. Structured/multisensory literacy instruction (Orton-Gillingham and successors) — does it apply to ADULT learners of NEW content?

This is where the popular narrative gets shakiest, and where the project should be most careful about scope creep. Orton-Gillingham (OG) is a direct, explicit, multisensory, structured, sequential, diagnostic, prescriptive approach — **designed for children who have not yet learned to decode**.

The best-verified evidence on OG's effectiveness even for that intended population is **Stevens, Austin, Moore, Scammacca, Boucher & Vaughn (2021)**, "Current State of the Evidence: Examining the Effects of Orton-Gillingham Reading Interventions for Students With or at Risk for Word-Level Reading Disabilities," published in *Exceptional Children*. This meta-analysis found the improvement in foundational reading skills (phonological awareness, phonics, fluency, spelling) was **not statistically significant** (small positive effect size, reported around 0.22–0.32 depending on outcome measure), and vocabulary/comprehension outcomes were likewise non-significant (effect size ≈0.14, p=.59). A Reading League Journal piece, authored by researchers including Solari, discusses and contextualizes this same 2021 finding for practitioners — it is a commentary on Stevens et al., not an independent second meta-analysis reaching a different conclusion.

**This is a genuinely under-supported literature, not a settled "OG works" claim** — treat any product or curriculum that cites "the Orton-Gillingham research base" as authoritative with real skepticism. The bottom line is actually more skeptical than "two meta-analyses disagree": the best single verified study on OG-based foundational-skill instruction did not find a statistically significant effect.

**More importantly for this build: none of the OG evidence base is about adults learning new professional/technical domain content.** OG and its structured-literacy descendants are almost entirely studied in K-12 populations learning to read English words for the first time (phoneme-grapheme correspondence, syllable types, etc.). This user already reads. The task isn't "teach someone to decode" — it's "help an adult who decodes slowly/effortfully learn unfamiliar domain vocabulary (F&I sales terms, compliance language, finance jargon) efficiently." That is a different problem, and this search turned up **no meta-analysis or RCT applying OG-style multisensory structured methodology to adults learning new subject-matter content** — the closest adjacent literature is workplace/employment support literature (see §5's workplace section below), which is explicitly candid that it rests on professional judgment rather than evaluation studies.

What *does* generalize from structured-literacy principles to adult new-content learning, even without a direct RCT, are the underlying mechanisms rather than the branded method: explicit teaching of morphological structure (see §3), reduced simultaneous processing demand, and multiple representational modalities (see §4) — these have their own adult-relevant evidence independent of "OG" as a brand.

**Skeptic's note:** Do not adopt "Orton-Gillingham" as a design principle or cite it as if it validates the build. Its best-verified evidence found non-significant effects even for its intended population, and it has not been validated at all for this population (adult, already-literate, learning professional content). If OG-derived techniques (e.g., explicit morpheme teaching, sequential complexity) are used, cite the underlying adult-relevant literature (morphology, cognitive load), not OG itself.

**Confidence: the "OG is not validated for this use case" conclusion is high-confidence and, if anything, understates the weakness of the OG evidence base.** See Adversarial Review Notes for what was wrong in the earlier draft of this section.

### 3. Content structure and authoring for dyslexic readers (distinct from CSS/typography)

This is the most under-covered and most actionable subtopic for a text-authoring system. Findings, organized by lever:

**Chunking by meaning unit reduces working-memory load during decoding.** Rather than decoding grapheme-by-grapheme or processing a full sentence as one unit, breaking content into meaningful chunks (morphemes, phrases, short sentences) lets the reader recognize units rather than assemble them serially, freeing cognitive capacity for comprehension of the whole (95 Percent Group / morphological-awareness literature synthesis, 2025; ASHA *Language, Speech, and Hearing Services in Schools*, 2025).

**Morphology-based presentation has evidence for reading/spelling gains in dyslexic readers**, not just typical readers, though the direct evidence is a small study. A 2024 controlled intervention study (**Mendes & Kirby**, *Learning Disability Quarterly*, SAGE) found a morphological-awareness intervention improved reading and spelling ability in children with dyslexia — but the study was small (N=16: 8 experimental, 8 control), so treat the effect-size confidence as low even though the direction of the finding is consistent with the broader morphology literature. Morphological awareness also appears to **mediate/support phonological processing** rather than being a separate unrelated skill — i.e., teaching word-part structure (prefixes, roots, suffixes) may bootstrap the very phonological skill that's otherwise deficient (ASHA 2025 synthesis). Practically: introducing new technical vocabulary (e.g., "amortization," "subvented," "reinsurance") by breaking the word into its meaningful parts and glossing each part, rather than presenting it as an opaque whole-word unit to memorize, is evidence-aligned, even if the direct adult-professional-content evidence doesn't exist yet.

**Sentence complexity is a lever independent of vocabulary difficulty.** Long, syntactically nested sentences increase working-memory load during reading regardless of word-level decoding difficulty, and this stacks with — rather than substitutes for — the dyslexic reader's baseline decoding cost. (This point is inferable from the general cognitive-load literature already in the project's existing coverage — Sweller, Miller/Cowan — combined with the dyslexia-specific finding that dyslexic readers show a *larger* comprehension penalty than skilled readers as sentence complexity rises, per the university-student dyslexia comprehension literature below.) The practical implication: cap subordinate-clause depth and prefer short, front-loaded main clauses in instructional prose, especially in first-pass explanations of new F&I concepts.

**Glossing (inline or marginal definitions) reduces the "detour cost" of stopping to look up unfamiliar terms**, which is disproportionately costly for a reader whose baseline decoding speed is already slow — every detour is a working-memory interruption on top of an already-strained system. There isn't a dyslexia-specific RCT of inline glossing found in this search, but it follows directly and uncontroversially from the general cognitive-load/complexity literature plus the specific finding below (§5) that university dyslexic readers rely on distinct comprehension strategies from skilled readers and are more sensitive to text-level friction.

**Skeptic's note:** the chunking/morphology/sentence-complexity claims are well-supported in the general dyslexia-intervention literature but the *specific application* to adult professional-content authoring (as opposed to children's literacy curricula or academic text) is inference, not directly tested. Label these as "evidence-informed design heuristics," not "proven for this use case." The citation list below deliberately mixes primary peer-reviewed literature (Mendes & Kirby, ASHA) with secondary practitioner summaries (95 Percent Group, IDA.org) — treat the practitioner sources as readable synthesis, not independent evidence.

**Confidence: moderate.** Chunking/morphology mechanisms are well-evidenced in general; the direct study (Mendes & Kirby) is real but small (N=16) and should not be treated as strong standalone proof.

- ASHA (2025). Morphological Awareness: Connecting Language Foundations and Academic Literacy Success for Students With Language and Literacy Deficits. *Language, Speech, and Hearing Services in Schools*. https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2025_LSHSS-24-00119

### 4. Text-to-speech, read-aloud, and multimodal presentation for dyslexic adults learning technical/professional material

The best available evidence is a peer-reviewed meta-analysis, not vendor claims:

**Wood, Moxley, Tighe & Wagner (2018), *Journal of Learning Disabilities*, 51(1), 73–84** — meta-analysis of 22 studies (1993–2013), n=2,942, on whether TTS and related read-aloud tools improve reading comprehension for students with reading disabilities. Result: **average weighted effect size d=0.35 (95% CI 0.14–0.56, p<.01)** — a real but small-to-moderate positive effect, not a cure and not close to eliminating the comprehension gap. The paper's own framing: TTS "addresses" specific symptom mechanisms (word misidentification, slow word reading, poor connected-text processing) that cascade into comprehension failure — it's a load-reduction tool, not a remediation of the underlying deficit. Both independent fact-checks verified this citation's numbers exactly — it is the most solidly-sourced finding in this brief.

**No RCT or meta-analysis specific to dyslexic adults learning new technical/professional material was found.** The Wood et al. base is overwhelmingly K-12 and general reading-disability populations, mostly on academic/narrative text, not adult professional upskilling content. This is a genuine, nameable evidence gap — the build should use TTS/read-aloud as a plausible, modestly-evidenced accommodation, not claim it's validated for this exact use case.

**Adjacent evidence on assistive technology more broadly**: a five-year longitudinal follow-up of dyslexic students' experiences using assistive technology (TTS, speech-to-text, audiobooks) to support written-language skills found sustained but uneven use and benefit, with the authors explicitly noting that most existing research is short-term and under-studied for long-term/adult use (Tandfonline, *Disability and Rehabilitation: Assistive Technology*, 2022 follow-up).

**University-level comprehension research is directly relevant** to this system's adult-professional-learner context: a study of university students with and without dyslexia found that **text reading fluency and text reading comprehension do not rely on the same underlying abilities** — i.e., a dyslexic adult reader who has developed adequate fluency (reads at reasonable speed) can still have a distinct, separate comprehension deficit, and vice versa (PMC9125151, 2022). This matters for the build: don't assume that if a reader can get through text at normal speed, comprehension is fine — the two need to be checked/scaffolded somewhat independently (which is consistent with this project's existing testing/explain-back methodology, e.g., the F&I "explain it back" step, but worth naming explicitly as dyslexia-relevant, not just ADHD-retrieval-practice-relevant).

**Skeptic's note:** d=0.35 is a real, useful, modest effect — not the "levels the playing field" framing that assistive-tech vendors often use. Present TTS/read-aloud in the build as one moderately-helpful accommodation among several, sequenced alongside chunking/morphology/glossing (§3) rather than as the headline dyslexia feature.

**Confidence: high.** This is the single most rigorously verified section of the brief.

Citations:
- Wood, S.G., Moxley, J.H., Tighe, E.L., & Wagner, R.K. (2018). Does Use of Text-to-Speech and Related Read-Aloud Tools Improve Reading Comprehension for Students With Reading Disabilities? A Meta-Analysis. *Journal of Learning Disabilities*, 51(1), 73–84. https://doi.org/10.1177/0022219416688170
- Dyslexic students' experiences in using assistive technology to support written language skills: a five-year follow-up. *Disability and Rehabilitation: Assistive Technology* (2022). https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17483107.2022.2161647
- Text Reading Fluency and Text Reading Comprehension Do Not Rely on the Same Abilities in University Students With and Without Dyslexia. PMC9125151. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9125151/

### 5. Comorbid dyslexia + ADHD in adults

Directly relevant to this specific user. Key findings:

**Comorbidity is common and substantial, not incidental.** Estimates in the literature commonly range from roughly **25–40%** for co-occurrence between reading disorder/dyslexia and ADHD in either direction, with some samples reporting figures at or slightly above the high end of that band (cited across multiple secondary sources drawing on the epidemiological literature, e.g., WPS Publish clinical summary and CBTC clinical summary). Treat the precise number as approximate — it is not traceable to one specific primary epidemiological paper in this search, and the exact figure varies noticeably by study population and diagnostic criteria.

**The comorbid profile is additive/compounding, not simply two independent conditions layered together.** Recent cognitive-profile work (WISC-V-based comparison across ADHD, SLD (specific learning disorder/dyslexia), and ADHD+SLD comorbidity, *Journal of School Psychology* family, ScienceDirect 2026) found substantial overlap in working-memory and processing-speed impairment across all three groups, with the comorbid group showing the most severe/widespread profile — described in adjacent literature as an "additive-effect" producing "more severe functional deficits" than either condition alone.

**Executive-function heterogeneity — treat with caution, this is one small exploratory study.** A 2025 mixed-method neurocognitive analysis (*Neurology International*, "Executive Function Profiles in ADHD and Dyslexia") proposed three distinct EF subprofiles in this population — (1) globally reduced executive functioning, (2) verbal-mnestic executive vulnerability, and (3) selective executive control deficit. **This is a case-based thematic synthesis with a total comparative sample of n=30 (ADHD n=18, dyslexia-only n=4, comorbid ADHD+dyslexia n=8)** — the three-subprofile typology is drawn from an 8-person comorbid subgroup in a clinical case series. It should be read as an exploratory, not-yet-replicated hypothesis, not an established taxonomy the build should design around. The authors' broader point — that comorbid EF difficulties "compound, yielding complex and heterogeneous cognitive presentations that challenge standard diagnostic approaches" — is a reasonable qualitative takeaway even if the specific three-subprofile structure shouldn't be treated as settled science.

**Cognitive-load research specific to this comorbid population is thin but exists.** A 2024 scoping review of neurophysiological measures of cognitive load in ADHD, ASD, and dyslexia (*European Journal of Neuroscience*, Le Cunff et al., 2024) explicitly calls out the paucity of research measuring cognitive load *directly* (vs. inferring it from behavior) in these overlapping populations, and issues it as a research recommendation — i.e., the field itself flags this as under-studied, which should temper confidence in any specific cognitive-load prescription for this exact comorbid profile.

**Workplace/employment evidence is thin and self-admittedly so.** A narrative systematic review of coaching interventions for dyslexia at work found the "theoretical framing for the purported effectiveness of coaching or other accommodation activities is lacking, and good practice in this field rests almost entirely on professional judgment and common sense rather than on evidence from evaluation studies" — a striking and important skeptic's-note-worthy admission from within the field's own review literature. A South African exploratory study on organisational support for employees with dyslexia (PMC12135158, 2025) similarly frames workplace dyslexia support as an emerging, under-evidenced area. General workplace guidance (multi-sensory learning techniques, alternative-format materials, extra time/repetition) is consensus/practitioner-level, not RCT-backed.

**Secondary emotional burden is documented and relevant given this user's mild depression.** University students with dyslexia report elevated anxiety and depression and greater feelings of academic ineptitude than peers, tracing back to educational histories (Riddick, 1999, *Dyslexia* journal — an older but foundational and still-cited study establishing self-esteem/anxiety links in adult dyslexic students; more recent 2026 work in the same journal, Dams et al., "Reading- and Spelling-Related Self-Concept, Emotions and Coping Strategies in Students With and Without Dyslexia," extends this with current data). Importantly, **interventions that reduce reading friction and build competence have been shown to improve self-esteem and lower anxiety** — i.e., the emotional benefit isn't a separate track from the mechanical fixes in §3/§4, it's a downstream effect of them working. Gender-differentiated coping patterns are also noted (more emotional/avoidance-based coping associated with lower self-esteem and moderate depression in some samples), though this is less directly relevant to build decisions.

**A 2025 narrative review specifically on strategies used by university students with dyslexia** (Knight, Ritter & Loeb, 2025, SAGE) is the closest thing to an adult-compensatory-strategy synthesis found — it catalogs self-reported coping/compensatory strategies (not a controlled intervention study, so causal claims are limited) but is a useful adult-specific reference point distinct from the K-12-dominated remediation literature.

**Skeptic's note:** The comorbidity literature is real and the "compounding" framing is well-supported by the WISC-V study, but be careful not to over-medicalize the build response — the workplace-accommodation evidence base explicitly admits it's driven by professional judgment, not trials, and the EF-subprofile typology rests on a very small case series. Treat comorbidity findings as justification for *general* load-reduction and small-unit design (which the ADHD research already in this project's coverage supports for independent reasons), not as license to add dyslexia-specific features with unproven adult effectiveness.

**Confidence: mixed.** The additive-deficit direction (WISC-V study) and the emotional-burden findings (Riddick, Dams et al.) are reasonably solid. The specific three-subprofile EF typology is low-confidence (small exploratory sample). Prevalence percentages are approximate, not pinned to one primary source.

## What This Means for the Build Plan

Given the F&I sales content already in `learning/f-and-i-sales/` (which is exactly the "adult learning new technical/professional material" use case this evidence base is thin on), here's the translation into concrete, evidence-anchored build decisions — separated from what's speculative:

**Well-supported, adopt with confidence:**
1. **Author content with explicit morphology breakdowns for new domain jargon.** When introducing terms like "subvention," "reinsurance," "amortization," "GAP," break the word into meaningful parts and gloss each part inline, rather than presenting it as a whole-word vocabulary item to memorize. (§3, Mendes & Kirby 2024 — note small N=16 sample, ASHA 2025)
2. **Cap sentence complexity and chunk by meaning unit in first-pass explanations.** Short, front-loaded sentences; avoid nested subordinate clauses when introducing a new concept for the first time — save syntactic complexity for later application/testing steps, once the term is already familiar. (§3, general working-memory + dyslexia comprehension literature)
3. **Offer TTS/read-aloud as an available accommodation, not the headline feature**, and frame it honestly in any user-facing copy: modest, real benefit (d≈0.35), not a fix. (§4, Wood et al. 2018 — the most solidly verified finding in this brief)
4. **Don't assume fluent-sounding output means comprehension landed.** Keep the existing "explain it back" / retrieval-practice steps (already in the F&I session design) as the actual comprehension check — this is doubly justified now: it serves ADHD retrieval-practice needs (already covered) *and* compensates for the documented fluency≠comprehension dissociation in dyslexic adult readers (§4, PMC9125151).
5. **Design for the ADHD+dyslexia compounding profile, not either condition separately.** Favor small, single-concept units with immediate low-stakes retrieval (already the system's ADHD-driven design instinct) — the comorbidity literature (§5) gives an independent, dyslexia-side reason to keep units small: verbal working memory is a shared bottleneck for both conditions, so a design that already reduces working-memory load for ADHD reasons is doing double duty for dyslexia reasons. No new mechanism needed — but worth stating explicitly in the design rationale so it doesn't get cut later as "ADHD-only, non-essential for dyslexia." (Lean on the WISC-V additive-deficit finding here, not the small EF-subprofile study.)

**Evidence-informed but not directly validated — build cautiously, and say so if surfaced to the user:**
6. **Do not adopt "Orton-Gillingham" or "structured literacy" as a branded methodology or cite it as validating the build.** Its own best-verified evidence (Stevens et al. 2021) found non-significant effects even for children, and there is no evidence it transfers to adults learning new subject matter. If borrowing techniques (sequential complexity, explicit morpheme teaching), cite the underlying adult-relevant mechanism (morphology, cognitive load), not the OG brand. (§2)
7. **Treat glossing and reduced-detour vocabulary support as a plausible, evidence-adjacent design choice**, not a directly-tested one — reasonable to build, but don't claim it's "proven for dyslexic adults" in any documentation.

**Explicitly out of scope / not to build:**
8. **No dyslexia "remediation" or "coaching" feature.** The workplace-coaching evidence base for dyslexia admits it rests on professional judgment rather than trials — this system's job is accommodation and content design, not clinical/coaching-style remediation, and claiming otherwise would be building on air.
9. **Watch the emotional-burden angle as a design constraint, not a feature to build.** Given this user's mild depression plus the documented link between reading friction and anxiety/depression in dyslexic adults (§5), the practical implication is: the system's tone and error-handling (how it responds when the user gets something wrong in the "explain it back" or role-play steps) matters as much as any content-structure decision. This argues for keeping the existing blunt-but-safe correction style (already visible in the F&I session script: "Don't be nice about it" paired with "that's not failure — that's the system working") — bluntness about the *content* combined with explicit reassurance about the *process* is consistent with what the self-esteem/coping literature suggests reduces the anxiety spiral, though this is inference from adjacent findings, not a direct test of this exact pattern.

**Net evidence-honesty flag for whatever gets written into the project's permanent research file:** rank confidence explicitly — orthographic/phonological adult-persistence (§1) and TTS effect size (§4) are solid peer-reviewed findings, independently confirmed by two rounds of fact-checking; morphology/chunking-for-adults (§3) and comorbidity-compounding (§5) are well-evidenced in adjacent populations but inferential for this exact adult-professional-content use case, and one component of §5 (the EF-subprofile typology) rests on a small exploratory sample; OG/structured-literacy-for-adults (§2) and workplace coaching (§5) are the two areas where the field itself admits the evidence is thin or contested — and §2's OG evidence is weaker than the original draft of this brief implied. Don't let the build's documentation imply otherwise.

This brief was checked by two independent adversarial fact-check passes before being finalized. Both passes verified the Wood et al. 1. **§2 (Orton-Gillingham) — fabricated/misattributed citation, high severity.** The original draft claimed "two credible meta-analyses reaching different conclusions": a fictitious "Stevens, Austin & Moore, 2018" study with invented significant effect sizes (d=0.47 decoding, d=0.40 spelling, d=0.32 comprehension), contrasted with a "different, Solari-led 2025 meta-analysis" reporting non-significant results (ES=0.32, p=.24).
