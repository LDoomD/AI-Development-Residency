# Book extraction — Made to Stick (Chip Heath & Dan Heath)

**Authors:** Chip Heath (organizational-behavior professor, Stanford GSB; ran ~40 experiments with ~1,700 participants on naturally sticky ideas — urban legends, proverbs, conspiracy theories — and taught a Stanford course, "How to Make Ideas Stick") and Dan Heath (co-founder and editor in chief of the educational publisher Thinkwell; worked with award-winning professors on how to teach economics, biology, calculus and physics). Attribution per the introduction, "What Led to Made to Stick."
**Edition used:** Random House, 2007 (the paperback edition including the back-matter "Sticky Advice" essays).
**Original mode:** message design — mined for F&I menu presentation language, but the book's own frame is communication generally, and its teaching material makes it the most directly self-applicable book in the library (this project's *own* lesson design is a target, not just a metaphor).
Extracted per the pipeline in `specs/reference-library-workflow.md`, 2026-08-13. Full read: introduction, all six chapters (Simple / Unexpected / Concrete / Credible / Emotional / Stories, including every embedded Clinic), the epilogue ("What Sticks"), and the three back-matter essays (Talking Strategy, Teaching That Sticks, Unsticking an Idea).

## Authors' stated purpose

From the introduction, near-verbatim: "We wrote this book to help you make your ideas stick. By 'stick,' we mean that your ideas are understood and remembered, and have a lasting impact — they change your audience's opinions or behavior." They frame the book explicitly as a **nurture** book, against the assumption that ideas are born interesting or uninteresting: "Are ideas born interesting or made interesting? Well, this is a nurture book." They also say what they are *not* offering — the standard communication advice about delivery ("stand up straight, make eye contact"), structure ("tell 'em what you're going to tell 'em"), audience knowledge, and above all repetition, which they single out for skepticism: "If you have to tell someone the same thing ten times, the idea probably wasn't very well designed. No urban legend has to be repeated ten times." Their claim is that the missing piece is **message design** — what to say, not how to deliver it. They also disclaim a formula: "There is no 'formula' for a sticky idea — we don't want to overstate the case," and in ch. 1, "This book can't offer a foolproof recipe."

The epilogue adds the closing framing: stickiness is not the province of geniuses. Every worked example is "a normal person with a normal job who made a difference" — Art Silverman, Nora Ephron's unnamed journalism teacher, Bob Ocwieja the Subway franchisee, Floyd Lee, Jane Elliott.

## Framework — the underlying mental model

Two moves, one villain.

1. **Step 1: find the core. Step 2: translate the core using the SUCCESs checklist.** Stated flatly in ch. 1: "There are two steps in making your ideas sticky… That's it. We'll spend the next half chapter on Step 1, and the remainder of the book on Step 2."

2. **SUCCESs** — Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Story. They admit the acronym is "a little corny" and coincidental. In the epilogue they map it onto a five-stage communication framework, and this mapping is the most useful form of the model because it tells you *which* trait fixes *which* failure:

   | Audience must… | Trait |
   |---|---|
   | Pay attention | Unexpected |
   | Understand and remember | Concrete |
   | Agree / believe | Credible |
   | Care | Emotional |
   | Be able to act | Story |

   Simple sits outside the list because it belongs to the Answer stage — finding and compacting the core.

3. **The villain: the Curse of Knowledge** — Elizabeth Newton's 1990 Stanford tappers-and-listeners study (120 songs tapped; listeners guessed 2.5% — 3 of 120 — while tappers predicted 50%). "Once we know something, we find it hard to imagine what it was like not to know it." The epilogue sharpens this into the book's deepest structural claim: getting a message across has an **Answer stage** and a **Telling Others stage**, and "the same factors that worked to your advantage in the Answer stage will backfire on you during the Telling Others stage." Expertise is what produces the Answer and also what makes you unable to transmit it. There are, they say, only two reliable defenses: don't learn anything, or transform your ideas — hence the checklist, which is deliberately more tangible than asking yourself "will they understand this?" (to which an expert always answers yes).

4. **Creativity is templated, not innate** (intro): an Israeli research team classified 89% of 200 award-winning ads into six templates but only 2% of 200 non-award ads. Novices trained for two hours on the templates produced ads rated 50% more creative than untrained novices, with 55% more positive product attitude; free-association brainstorming training produced ads that were less annoying but no more creative. Hence: "You want to invent new ideas, not new rules."

5. **Spotting beats creating** (ch. 6 and epilogue): "If you're a great spotter, you'll always trump a great creator. Why? Because the world will always produce more great ideas than any single individual." The three inspirational-story plots exist to make spotting possible, not to help you fabricate stories.

## Claims to NOT repeat as fact

- **Jane Elliott's brown-eyes/blue-eyes prejudice reductions ("studies conducted ten and twenty years later showed Elliott's students were significantly less prejudiced than their peers").** The exercise is real, famous and well documented on film; the long-term prejudice-reduction claim rests on small, uncontrolled follow-ups of a self-selected group and is not the kind of evidence that sentence implies. Keep the mechanism (experience beats explanation); drop the longitudinal claim.
- **The specific Truth-campaign percentages** ("66% less likely to smoke" vs "36% more likely" for Philip Morris's Think campaign). Directionally supported by the 2002 *AJPH* study they cite, but these are survey-intention numbers, not behavior, and the book itself notes ~half the Florida smoking decline may be attributable to a concurrent cigarette-tax increase. Cite the contrast, not the numbers.
- **"Mental practice produces two-thirds of the benefit of physical practice."** The meta-analysis is real, but this ratio is an average across heterogeneous tasks and is heavily moderated by task type (the book says so, then the number travels without the caveat). For this project specifically: never let it justify substituting rehearsal for REPS.
- **The jam-tasting study (24 vs 6 jars, 10× sales)** in the back matter — the single most-cited choice-overload result and one with a contested replication record. Choice overload is real; that headline figure should not be quoted as settled.
- **The Israeli ad-template study numbers** (89% vs 2%; +50% creativity from two hours of training). One study, one research group, striking effect sizes, no replication cited. Use as the origin of the "creativity is templated" idea, not as evidence.
- **"You use only 10 percent of your brain," the Great Wall visible from space, Halloween candy tampering, the Kidney Heist, Coca-Cola rotting bones** — these appear in the book explicitly *as* debunked sticky falsehoods. Anyone skimming the technique table should not accidentally re-import them as facts.
- **The Beyond War "5,000 warheads" and CSPI "37 grams" figures** are period-accurate to the 1980s–90s campaigns described, not current numbers. They're illustrations of technique, not data.
- **The "before-and-after" Clinic scorecards** are the authors grading their own rewrites. Useful as worked examples of the process; not evidence that the rewritten messages performed better, since none were tested.

## Compliance note specific to F&I

This is the least *inherently* dangerous book in the persuasion half of the library — its subject is comprehension, not compliance-gaining — but three of its techniques land directly on regulated ground and need naming:

1. **Human-scale rescaling** is arithmetically identical to payment packing. "Only $1.40 a day" is legitimate alongside a disclosed price, term and total; it is deceptive when the daily figure *replaces* the real number. The book's own Cisco example includes the full cost; keep that discipline.
2. **Vivid detail boosts credibility even when irrelevant** — that's the finding, from a real experiment. So detail must carry evidentiary weight for the claim it sits next to, or it is decoration doing persuasive work the facts haven't earned. The Heaths say this themselves ("truthful, core details"); it needs to be a rule, not a preference.
3. **Imagination scenarios** (the Tempe cable study) are potent and are the natural mode for protection products. Bound them to realistic, disclosed, probable events. Vivid imagined catastrophe plus a product is the fear-without-remedy pattern already flagged in the Pre-Suasion extraction.

Against that, the book supplies unusually strong *pro-compliance* tools: **testable credentials** are self-limiting (a false claim fails when tested), the **Sinatra test** rewards a real track record over adjectives, **fight-sticky-with-stickier** is the honest way to correct a customer's false belief about a product, and the **antiauthority** principle says a real prior claimant beats a polished pitch. On the standing natural-vs-fabricated test: everything here that makes a *true* thing understandable, memorable and actionable is legitimate; the same tools pointed at a claim the facts don't support are more dangerous than plain misstatement precisely because they make it memorable.

## Cross-book convergence and tension (fourteen books in)

- **Curse of Knowledge is now the library's most cross-cutting concept.** It's the villain here, and — per the parallel extraction — the same phenomenon anchors *The Sense of Style*'s account of why professionals write badly. Two independent authors, two different domains (message design vs prose), one mechanism. Treat it as library-standard, alongside "because"-reasoning and advice-asking.
- **Concreteness/vividness converges four ways:** Cialdini's *Influence* and *Pre-Suasion* (vividness and fluency as persuasion levers), Van Edwards's *Captivate* (story specificity), and here — where it gets its cleanest mechanism (Velcro/hooks, novices need concrete, abstraction is the expert's luxury) and its clearest ethical edge (the Darth Vader toothbrush shows irrelevant detail moves judgments).
- **Story tooling converges with *Captivate*'s Story Stack**, but Made to Stick contributes what Van Edwards doesn't: a *spotting* taxonomy (Challenge/Connection/Creativity) rather than a telling technique, plus Denning's springboard mechanism for why a story disarms the counter-arguing inner voice. Pair them — Captivate for delivery, MTS for selection.
- **Mystery-opening is a direct Cialdini crossover** — the Saturn's-rings account in ch. 2 *is* Cialdini describing his own method, and it also appears in the Pre-Suasion extraction's magnetizers row. Same author, two books, arriving here through a third. High confidence; it's the best-attested presentation-structure move in the library.
- **Gap theory + prediction-commitment converge with this project's own retention research** (`2026-08-13_book-extraction-retention-methodology.md`): open a gap → force a prediction → reveal is structurally the generation effect and the testing effect wearing communication clothes. This is the strongest reason to treat MTS as a *system-design* source, not only an F&I source.
- **Tension with the identifiable-victim finding vs Getting to Yes's objective criteria.** MTS shows that statistics actively suppress the emotional response that drives action, and that combining story + statistics is *worse* than story alone. Fisher/Ury's whole method runs on objective criteria and numbers. These aren't contradictory but they're aimed at different targets — criteria settle what's fair, stories decide whether anyone acts — and a lesson that teaches both should say so rather than let the learner discover the clash mid-deal.
- **Tension with visualize-success advice.** The UCLA study here (event-simulation beats outcome-simulation) is a direct empirical rebuke to the "visualize the win" genre, some of which drifts near the confidence/state-management material in *The Charisma Myth*. Where those overlap in a lesson, MTS's version is the better-evidenced one: rehearse the process, not the trophy.
- **New unique contributions to the library:** the Answer-stage/Telling-Others-stage split; the five-stage failure diagnostic (which trait fixes which symptom); Commander's Intent; the proverb standard for simplicity; generative vs dead metaphors (Disney vs Subway); post-dictable surprise; the Sinatra test; antiauthority credibility; testable credentials; semantic stretch; Maslow's-basement projection; the three inspirational plots as a *spotting* tool; springboard stories; and fight-sticky-with-stickier (plus sticky *action*) as the only workable method for correcting an entrenched false belief.
