# Book extraction — Sizing People Up (Robin Dreeke & Cameron Stauth)

**Author:** Robin Dreeke — former head of the FBI Counterintelligence Behavioral Analysis Program (his second book on this list), with Cameron Stauth.
**Edition used:** Portfolio/Penguin, 2020. ISBN 9780525540434.
**Original mode:** fast behavioral prediction of strangers for F&I (per `specs/research/2026-08-08_f-and-i-reading-list.md`, ranked #3 — "literally his first 60 seconds with every customer").
Extracted per the pipeline in `specs/reference-library-workflow.md`, 2026-08-13. Full read: framework chapters 1–2, all six sign chapters (3–8), afterword. The book conveniently ends every chapter with an author-written "Debriefing" (key quote, key message, takeaways) — those were used verbatim as the author-stated layer.

## Author's stated purpose

From chapters 1–2, near-verbatim: teach the reader to "think like an FBI behavioral analyst — objectively, rationally, systematically, and free from emotional distractions" — replacing intuition, gut feelings, and luck with a six-sign system for predicting what people will do. His stated claim for why it matters: conventional/intuitive judgment of trustworthiness is right "only about half the time" (his "50 Percent Rule" — presented as coming from "the best studies in behavioral science" but not specifically cited; treat the number as the author's claim, not a verified statistic). The one-line motto he repeats throughout: **Doveryai no Proveryai — trust, but verify.**

## Framework — the underlying mental model

**Trust is predictability, not morality.** The single load-bearing premise: *the one thing you can almost always predict people to do is act in their own best interests — as THEY perceive them.* So sizing someone up = discovering what they think is in their best interest. Corollaries the whole system hangs on:

- **Keep feelings out of it.** You can like someone and not trust them, and trust someone you don't like. Emotion-based assessment ("I have a good feeling about him") is the main failure mode — Dreeke calls emotional override "emotional hijacking."
- **Their perception is the only one that matters.** Assessing from your own perspective is "projection, not empathy."
- **Context = person + situation.** People don't change much; situations change constantly. Past patterns crumble under new temptation, new immunity from consequences ("the power paradox"), or desperation. Desperation is "the kryptonite of trust" — desperate people will do almost anything.
- **Baseline, then deviations.** Even flawed people are predictable if you establish their normal (e.g., a habitual pessimist being pessimistic about your idea is signal-free noise).
- **Trust is task-specific.** "You'd never trust an airline pilot to do your brain surgery." Someone can be fully trusted in one domain and not at all in another.

## Claims to NOT repeat as fact

- **The "50 Percent Rule"** (intuitive trust-prediction is right half the time) — presented as research-backed but never specifically cited. Use as the author's framing device, not a statistic.
- **The love/fear "only two basic emotions" model** — attributed loosely to Dan Baker; it's a simplification contested by mainstream affect research. Useful lens, not neuroscience.
- The various engagement/productivity percentages (50/30/20 engagement, 2% of GDP) are loosely-cited poll numbers, not verified here.

## Compliance note specific to F&I

This book is assessment/defense skill, so nearly everything is compliant — with one line worth drawing explicitly: behavioral "tells" are for **rapport and communication calibration**, not for decisions. Using hunches about a customer's trustworthiness/stability to alter the *substance* of what they're offered (pricing, products, credit posture) would collide with fair-lending obligations (Reg B/ECOA — already mapped in `2026-08-08_california-f-and-i-law.md`). Read people to communicate better; never to treat them adversely on a hunch.

## Cross-book convergence (three books in, the library is starting to pay off)

- **Discovery questions ≈ Voss's calibrated questions ≈ Dreeke's own How/When/What/Who** — three independent extractions now converge on open, other-directed questioning as the single most load-bearing technique. That convergence upgrades practical confidence in it even though each individual source grades Extrapolated/Moderate.
- **The Code of Trust is the same framework as *It's Not All About Me*** (same author) — ego suspension, validation, nonjudgment. Book 1 teaches the behaviors; this book turns them into an assessment lens (look FOR them in others).
- **Incongruence-as-flag** appears in both Voss (label the mismatch) and here (words/music/dance) — same technique, two sources, no reliance on the shaky 7-38-55 numbers.
- New unique contribution of this book: **the six-sign taxonomy and per-sign tells checklists** — the only structured *assessment* system in the library so far (the other books teach influence; this one teaches vetting).
