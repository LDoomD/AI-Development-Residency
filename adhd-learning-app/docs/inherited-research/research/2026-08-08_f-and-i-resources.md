## B. Trust-building and behavioral influence — corrected: this is FBI, not CIA

**The material Orlando asked for as "CIA tactics" is, in every credentialed case that actually
exists, FBI.** There is no comparably documented, named, verifiable "CIA trust-building"
literature. This isn't a technicality — the specific bureau matters, because the FBI figures
below built their material inside a *counterintelligence-recruitment and hostage-negotiation*
context, which is the actual closest real-world analog to "earn a stranger's trust fast," not
CIA fieldcraft.

### Robin Dreeke

Naval Academy grad, Marine Corps, then 21 years FBI Special Agent, counterintelligence. Per his
own bio (independently image-credited by the FBI's own Law Enforcement Bulletin, some
corroboration beyond self-marketing) he led the FBI's Counterintelligence Behavioral Analysis
Program (BAP) for ~3 years and was lead instructor at the FBI Counterintelligence Training Center
for 5 years. Now runs "People Formula."

Books: *It's Not All About "Me"* (revised/reissued by **Simon & Schuster**, 2026, ISBN
9781637748466 — original self-pub year unconfirmed). *The Code of Trust*, with Cameron Stauth,
**St. Martin's Press, 2017**.

Technique: rapport-building mnemonics developed for human-source recruiting, repackaged for
business. **Evidence basis: practitioner lore, not peer-reviewed** — no independent study
validates the packaged "10 techniques"/"5 rules" frameworks as such. **Grade: Moderate** —
credible field-derived skeleton, seasoned-practitioner advice, not science.

### Jack Schafer

PhD psychology (Fielding Graduate University). 15 years FBI counterintelligence/counterterrorism,
7 as a Behavioral Analysis Program analyst. Currently professor, Western Illinois University
(independently confirmed via Newswise coverage — stronger third-party corroboration than a bio
alone).

Book: *The Like Switch*, with Marvin Karlins PhD, **Touchstone/Atria (Simon & Schuster), 2015**.

Technique: the "Friendship Formula" (proximity, frequency, duration, intensity), nonverbal rapport
cues. **Evidence basis: practitioner lore with partial grounding** — his academic background lets
him draw on real published psychology (e.g., mere-exposure effect) more than the others, but the
packaged system itself isn't independently peer-reviewed. **Grade: Moderate** — nonverbal cues
transfer well to a seated conversation; the frequency/duration parts of the formula fit a single
F&I visit poorly and should be discounted.

### Chris Voss

24 years FBI, joined the Crisis Negotiation Unit in 1992, lead FBI negotiator NYC Division, FBI's
lead international kidnapping negotiator pre-2008. **Most independently corroborated bio of the
four** — named, cross-confirmed cases (the "Blind Sheikh"/TERRSTOP case, TWA Flight 800, the 2006
Jill Carroll and Steve Centanni hostage negotiations). CEO, The Black Swan Group.

Book: *Never Split the Difference*, with Tahl Raz, **Harper Business, 2016**.

**This is the one finding worth slowing down for.** The broader model Voss draws on — the FBI
Crisis Negotiation Unit's Behavioral Change Stairway Model (active listening → empathy → rapport
→ influence → behavioral change) — has real peer-reviewed backing: Vecchi, Van Hasselt & Romano
(2005), *Aggression and Violent Behavior*, 10(5), 533–551. But Voss's specific packaged techniques
(mirroring word-count, "that's right" framing, labeling scripts) are his own articulation on top
of that model, not independently tested themselves. There is also a **published peer-reviewed
critique** in *Negotiation Journal* ("Building on the Differences: A Rejoinder to Chris Voss")
arguing his hostage-derived tactics can backfire in relationships with long-term mutual
dependency, and that his framework skips cultural/legal/ethical dimensions relevant to ordinary
business negotiation.

Applied to F&I specifically: **mirroring and labeling transfer well** — low-risk active-listening
tools usable in any one-shot stranger conversation. **Tactical empathy transfers as a mindset**
(understand the objection before responding to it) but using empathy *instrumentally* to extract
a concession is the exact ethical friction point the published critique raises — a worse fit for
F&I, where a customer's sense of having been "handled" shows up in CSI survey scores and repeat
business. **The "that's right" vs "you're right" distinction is the shakiest transfer** — built
to detect genuine buy-in under adversarial pressure (a hostage-taker deciding to surrender), not
a customer's politeness while signing routine paperwork. Reading it as a close signal risks
mistaking politeness for commitment. **Grade: Moderate** for mirroring/labeling and tactical
empathy as a mindset (with the ethical-fit caution above); **Extrapolated** for "that's
right"/"you're right" as a closing signal.

### Chase Hughes — excluded

Markets himself as former Navy behavioral-analyst/interrogation expert. Multiple independent
sources (a dedicated critic site, a former-intelligence-community critic's Substack, a behavioral
podcast) converge on the same specific challenges: his documented Navy rating was Quartermaster
(navigation/administration), reaching Chief late in a ~20-year career — not an interrogation
specialty — and his claimed Harvard/Duke coursework and "30,000+ hours observing interrogations"

older NLP/hypnosis material rebranded with military-sounding terms. **This is a materially
different category from the other three, all of whom have independently corroborable FBI

FBI-credentialed expertise.

**Discarded:** any claim of a comparable named CIA trust-building literature. Dreeke's exact
tenure/team-size figures (sourced only to his own site). *It's Not All About "Me"*'s original

peer-reviewed) or Schafer's/Dreeke's packaged frameworks as such.

## C. Persuasion science — what actually replicates

Cialdini, *Influence* (Harper Business, "New and Expanded" ed. 2021, adds the 7th principle
Unity; original 1984) and *Pre-Suasion* (2016 — content/year confirmed by multiple independent
sources; **publisher not independently verified, do not cite a publisher name for it**).

**Do not treat all seven principles as equally solid — they are not.** Ranked by actual replicated
evidence:

| Principle | Grade | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Social proof** | **High** | Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius (2008), *J. Consumer Research* — hotel towel-reuse field experiment, ~26% lift from descriptive-norm messaging over standard appeals. Independently replicated in German hotels (N=723, N=175). Real behavioral outcome, not self-report. |
| **Scarcity** | **High/Moderate** | Lynn (1992) meta-analysis of 41 studies: reliable effect on perceived value. Classic 1982 field study (wholesale beef buyers) showed large behavioral effects. Caveat: effect is moderated by how much the buyer is already scrutinizing the deal — less certain for high-elaboration F&I buyers. |
| **Authority** | **Moderate** | Underlying phenomenon (Milgram, replicated multiple times incl. cross-cultural syntheses) is real and strongly replicated — but that's evidence about obedience to instructions, not directly tested evidence that flashing credentials moves a purchase decision. Cialdini's sales translation is an extrapolation from the base finding. |
| **Reciprocity** | **Moderate** | Strong in adjacent literatures (trust games, organizational reciprocity); the specific "free gift → bigger reciprocal purchase" retail mechanism rests on older studies not found independently re-replicated in this search. |
| **Commitment/consistency (foot-in-the-door)** | **Moderate — weaker than commonly sold** | Burger (1999) synthesis of 100+ studies confirms the effect exists but via multiple competing mechanisms; an earlier quantitative synthesis found only a modest effect (r≈0.15), with nearly half of studies null or reversed. Present as directional, not a reliable lever. |
| **Liking** | **Moderate/Extrapolated** | Real classic findings (Aronson & Linder 1965 gain-loss liking) exist, but no meta-analysis found isolating liking's causal effect on purchase/compliance specifically. |
| **Unity** | **Extrapolated** | Newest (2016), thinnest evidence — largely Cialdini's own illustrative examples, not an independent replicated body of work testing "unity" as distinct from ordinary in-group liking. |

Sources praising the principles as uniformly durable after 40 years (Forbes/consultant blogs) were
**not used as evidence** — they're written by people who sell persuasion training. Only real
experiments/meta-analyses were used for grading.

**Other relevant, well-evidenced findings:**

- **Anchoring — High.** Replicated in the Many Labs Replication Project (Klein et al. 2014).
  First number stated in a negotiation (e.g., initial payment or add-on price) anchors the
  customer's subsequent estimate.
- **Framing (gain/loss) — High.** Long-replicated prospect-theory tradition. Loss-framed GAP/
  warranty pitches ("protect against owing $8,000 with nothing to show for it") plausibly land
  differently than gain-framed ones, though the exact magnitude in an auto-finance context
  specifically wasn't independently verified.
- **Defaults/opt-out framing — Moderate.** Johnson & Goldstein (2003) organ-donation default
  effect replicated well (N=1,920); a related default-effect paradigm (Johnson, Bellman & Lohse
  2002) failed to replicate elsewhere. Real but context-dependent.
  **Regulatory flag, current as of this writing:** the FTC's CARS Rule, which would have
  restricted default-inclusion/opt-out framing of add-ons like GAP and extended warranties, was
  **vacated by the Fifth Circuit on January 27, 2025** on procedural grounds and is not currently
  in effect. That does **not** make default-framing tactics safe — GAP/warranties/credit
  insurance remain legally optional add-ons under separate, current CFPB guidance, and CFPB
  enforcement against hard-to-cancel add-on schemes is active (one lender ordered to pay $60M).
  Presenting add-ons as required, or making cancellation an obstacle course, is live legal risk
  independent of the CARS Rule's status.
- **General social priming — discard as a class.** This is "the poster child of the replication
  crisis" — one large replication effort found 94% of 70 close replications showed smaller
  effects than the originals. None of it should be used to justify any sales tactic. Keep this
  distinct from Cialdini's principles (mixed but partly solid) and anchoring/framing (solid).

**Discarded:** *Pre-Suasion*'s publisher name (unverified). "40 years of scrutiny, still holds up"
framing (sourced only to commercial persuasion-consulting blogs). Reciprocity-specific retail
studies (e.g., Regan 1971) — referenced only in secondary sources, no independent replication
found. All social-priming tactics (money priming, warmth priming, elderly-word/walking-speed
priming).

## D. Charisma and communication literature

### Olivia Fox Cabane, *The Charisma Myth* (Portfolio/Penguin, 2012, ISBN 9781591845942)

Not a research psychologist — a coach/consultant (Fortune 500 executive coaching, Stanford
StartX). Core claims: charisma = presence + power + warmth, mostly signaled nonverbally, and is
learnable. **One central, checkable claim is broken:** the book leans on Amy Cuddy's "power pose"
research (expansive posture → testosterone up, cortisol down). That hormonal claim is one of the
most prominent failed replications of the post-2015 crisis — Cuddy's own co-author publicly
disavowed the hormone findings in 2016. Later, better-controlled work does support a narrower
claim (posture raises *self-reported* feelings of power), but not the hormonal/behavioral claim
the book uses. The rest (presence via visualization, warmth/power balance) draws loosely on real
psychology but isn't itself an empirical work — no original data, anecdotal examples.
**Grade: Moderate** for the presence/warmth/power framing as a usable mental model; drop or flag
the power-pose/hormone claim specifically — don't present it as validated science.

### Vanessa Van Edwards, *Captivate* (Portfolio/Penguin, 2017, ISBN 9780399564482) and *Cues*
(Portfolio/Penguin, 2022, ISBN 9780593332191)

Founder of "Science of People" (2012) — a coaching/content business, not a peer-reviewed research
lab; no evidence its output has been published in academic journals. Studied sociology/Chinese/
international relations at Emory (no advanced psychology degree found). The "Harvard University
instructor" claim in some of her bios could not be independently verified — **do not cite it as
fact.** Core claims: interaction has decodable patterns (micro-expressions, tone, gestures) that
predict likability and trust, learnable and deployable deliberately. Reviewer critique flags at
least one cited "priming effect" study that hasn't held up under replication — same
replication-crisis pattern as elsewhere in this genre. Mitigating point: one independent reviewer
noted she generally avoids the single-cue-means-one-thing overreach common in the genre (e.g.,
doesn't claim one gesture definitively signals lying). **Grade: Moderate** for practical tactics
(first impressions, small-talk openers, reading engagement) — reasonable to try, low cost if
wrong; **Extrapolated** for specific "this cue decodes to this meaning" claims — treat as
heuristics to tune by feel, not fixed rules.

**Discarded:** claims that either author's books underwent peer review (they're trade books, not
academic publications). The "Harvard instructor" claim. Whether either book specifically invokes
the debunked Mehrabian "7-38-55" statistic — flagged as a genre-level risk only, not confirmed
present in either book.

## E. The ethics/compliance line — mandatory, highest-consequence section

All items below verified against a primary regulator page (CFPB, FTC, IRS) or federal statute
text (Cornell LII / eCFR), except where a fetch was blocked (noted explicitly, not silently
dropped).

### Payment packing

Charging for add-ons the customer didn't knowingly, separately agree to, absorbed into an
inflated monthly payment. Violates FTC Act §5 (unfair/deceptive practices); becomes an ECOA issue
too if applied disproportionately by protected class. **Real enforcement:**
*FTC v. Asbury Automotive Group* (Docket 222-3135, 2024) — alleges payment packing plus
discriminatory targeting; **contested, litigation stayed, not an adjudicated finding.**
*FTC & Illinois AG v. Leader Automotive Group/AutoCanada* (Dec 2024) — **$20M settlement**, the
largest FTC monetary judgment against an auto dealer group to date; requires clear price
disclosure and express consent before charging for any add-on. The line: disclosed, itemized,
declinable products a customer can see broken out, versus anything silently absorbed into a
payment number. **Grade: High.**

### Powerbooking

Misrepresenting a vehicle's features/condition/trade value to a lender to inflate financeable
value. No FTC/CFPB page uses this specific trade term — it's industry-press language for conduct
prosecuted under general fraud statutes (18 U.S.C. §1014, §1344). **Grade: Moderate** — mechanism
and consequences well-documented across independent sources; no single regulator page names it.

### Straw purchase

A qualified buyer's identity/credit used on the application while a different person is the real
owner/payer, with the lender misled about who the actual borrower is. Prosecuted under **18
U.S.C. §1014** (Cornell LII, verified): false statement or willful overvaluation to influence a
federally-related lender — **up to 30 years and/or $1,000,000 fine.** Real DOJ prosecutions
confirmed via justice.gov: a $74M vehicle-financing fraud scheme (N.D. Ill., 2013, ~$56M in
lender losses); a Buick/GMC dealership straw-buyer scheme (W.D. Pa.); a Houston straw-buyer/
money-laundering auto scheme (S.D. Tex.). **Grade: High.**

### Reg Z (Truth in Lending Act) — APR disclosure

Verified directly at consumerfinance.gov, 12 CFR Part 1026. §1026.18(e): APR/finance charge must
be disclosed **more conspicuously** than any other required item. §1026.24: any advertised rate
must be stated as a true APR. Who's the disclosing "creditor" under §1026.2(a)(17): if the retail
installment contract is initially payable to the dealer (the normal case — even though it's then
assigned to a bank), **the dealer is the disclosing creditor at point of sale.** In practice, F&I
is very likely the one legally obligated to get the APR disclosure right, not just the bank.
**Grade: High.**

### Reg B / ECOA — non-discrimination and dealer markup

Baseline (consumerfinance.gov, 12 CFR §1002.4): no discrimination on a prohibited basis (race,
color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age, public-assistance income, exercising
CCPA rights) in any part of a credit transaction — this is disparate-*treatment* law and is
untouched by anything below.

**Dealer reserve/markup theory — verify carefully, status has changed:**
CFPB Bulletin 2013-02 warned indirect auto *lenders* that dealer discretion to mark up the buy
rate (and be paid on the markup) creates disparate-*impact* fair-lending risk if it statistically
falls harder on protected classes — recommending lenders eliminate/cap dealer discretion or move
to flat compensation. **This bulletin was disapproved by Congress in 2018 and is no longer in
effect** — historical, not current guidance. It did produce real settlements in its day (e.g., the
well-documented DOJ/CFPB action against Ally Financial in 2013), targeting lenders, with dealer
conduct as the mechanism.

**Major recent change:** on **April 22, 2026**, the CFPB finalized a rule removing the
disparate-impact "effects test" from Reg B entirely, effective **July 21, 2026** — confirmed via
Federal Register, corroborated by multiple law-firm trackers. This does **not** make markup
discretion safe: intentional discrimination (§1002.4(a)) remains fully illegal; state AGs/state
UDAP law can still pursue disparate-impact-style theories; lenders' own fair-lending compliance
programs (caps/monitoring) will likely stay in place contractually regardless of federal
enforcement posture. **Grade: Moderate** — the core prohibition is High confidence and unaffected;
the 2026 rule's real-world downstream effect is untested since it isn't yet in force.

### Red Flags Rule (FACTA)

FTC page content confirms auto dealers are explicitly listed as covered "creditors" (alongside
finance companies, mortgage brokers) requiring a written Identity Theft Prevention Program.
**Grade: High** on applicability; **Moderate** on granular program-content detail — full FTC page
fetch was blocked (403) this session, so this relies on the FTC's own indexed page text rather
than the complete document.

### GLBA Safeguards Rule

Most dealers that arrange financing/leasing are "financial institutions" under GLBA and must
maintain a written information-security program: a designated Qualified Individual, a written
risk assessment, access controls, encryption of nonpublic personal info, multi-factor
authentication for most dealers, an incident-response plan, annual reporting to leadership, and
(since a 2023 amendment) breach reporting to the FTC. F&I handles the most sensitive customer
data in the building — SSNs, bank routing numbers, income docs — making this rule directly
relevant to daily habits (no unencrypted email of SSNs, no personal-device storage of credit
apps). **Grade: High** on applicability/structure; **Moderate** on the granular checklist — same
403-fetch caveat as Red Flags above.

### IRS Form 8300 — cash over $10,000

Verified directly at irs.gov. Any cash payment over $10,000 in a single or **related**
transaction must be reported within **15 days**. "Cash" includes certain monetary instruments
under $10,000 face value (cashier's checks, money orders) when used in place of currency;
instruments over $10,000 face value and wire transfers are excluded. Payments within 24 hours are
automatically "related"; beyond that, related if the dealership knows/has reason to know they're
connected. A customer splitting payments to dodge the threshold is a **structuring** red flag —
still reportable, and knowingly helping a customer structure payments is itself a federal offense.
**Grade: High.**

### Technique-to-violation map

| Technique | Legitimate use | Where it crosses the line |
|---|---|---|
| Rapport-building before the numbers | Standard, legal — trust isn't deception | When used to rush past or obscure the APR disclosure, or get a signature before the customer processes the number — undermines a Reg Z requirement even with no false statement spoken |
| Menu presentation | The compliant way to sell — this is literally what the Leader Automotive settlement now requires (itemized, disclosed, initialed accept/decline) | If the menu is a prop while products get verbally bundled into "your payment" without separate acknowledgment — that's payment packing with extra steps |
| Payment-first selling ("what payment works for you?") | Common, not illegal alone | If it's used to avoid ever stating price/APR clearly, or to backfill add-ons into the payment gap — the exact fact pattern in the FTC's largest recent cases |
| Discretionary rate markup | Legal if applied uniformly and disclosed per your lender agreement | Illegal the moment the amount correlates with a protected class rather than deal risk — "I could tell they wouldn't push back" is not a defense if the pattern lines up with race/sex/age |
| Selling products the customer didn't ask about | Legal — that's the job | Illegal when persuasion bypasses affirmative, informed consent to the specific item and price |
| Favorable description of a vehicle/trade to a lender | Legitimate if accurate | Fraud (18 U.S.C. §1014) the instant it's inaccurate — no legitimate version exists once the statement is false |
| Helping a friend/relative "get approved" | Fine if the applicant is the true intended owner/payer | Straw purchase fraud the moment the real owner isn't the applicant and everyone in the room knows it |
