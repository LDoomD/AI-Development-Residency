# Research Brief: Adversarial Audit of "Learn X Faster" Claims for the ADHD/Dyslexia Learning System

**Prepared for:** jose.castillo@giant.cos.edu
**Date:** 2026-08-09
**Status:** Revised after two independent adversarial fact-checks — see "Adversarial Review Notes" below. Draft for review, not yet integrated into system spec.

---

## Executive Summary

The project's stated goal — "learn new material roughly 10x faster (or more) and retain it long-term" — is **not supportable by the peer-reviewed literature as a single, general claim**, and the "10x" framing should be retired from any user-facing or planning language. This is true even when restricted to the *validated* techniques already anchoring this project (retrieval practice, spaced repetition, worked examples, desirable difficulties, interleaving). Those techniques produce real, replicated, meaningful effects — but they are effects on specific dimensions (fewer repetitions to a fixed criterion, better durability at a delay, sometimes better transfer), not blanket multipliers on "speed of learning," and they routinely trade off against each other (harder-but-better-retained vs. easier-but-forgotten-sooner).

Every commercial or pop-science claim examined in this audit — speed reading/RSVP at 1000+ wpm with retained comprehension, "The First 20 Hours" as a general 10x-speedup method, superlearning/accelerated-learning seminars (Lozanov-style, NLP-adjacent), photographic/eidetic memory training, and 2025–2026 commercial "brain training" / "learn 10x faster" apps — fails under direct evidentiary scrutiny. Some (RSVP, photographic memory training) are **debunked outright** by controlled evidence showing the claimed mechanism doesn't exist or the claimed outcome doesn't survive rigorous measurement. Superlearning's outright-debunked verdict is held with slightly less confidence than the others (see §1.3 — the specific replication-failure claim could not be pinned to a verified primary source in this pass). Others (20-hours, most commercial brain-training apps) are **unfalsified but unsupported** — plausible-sounding, occasionally true for narrow motor/procedural skills, but never validated at the "learn anything" generality the marketing implies, and in brain-training's case actively contradicted by transfer-effect meta-analyses this project has already flagged (Sala et al. 2019; Simons et al. 2016 — covered under the existing "objective cognitive transfer" decision not to chase that metric).

**What is realistically achievable**, using only the already-validated technique stack, is a genuine but *qualitative* claim: meaningful reductions in repetitions-to-criterion for retrieval practice vs. restudy, and substantial gains in delayed retention under spacing vs. massing. Two adversarial fact-checks independently flagged that this brief's first draft had attached specific numeric ranges to those claims (e.g., "20–50% fewer repetitions," "1.5–2x retention") that are not traceable to a specific reported statistic in the cited meta-analyses — those numbers have been removed or explicitly relabeled as illustrative, unsourced estimates below (see Adversarial Review Notes). The direction of both effects is well-replicated; the precise magnitude is not something this brief can currently defend to a citable number. The honest framing for this project is: *faster path to durable retention per unit of effective study time*, not *faster acquisition of new material in wall-clock time*, and not a claim with a specific multiplier attached until one is sourced. Where "10x" appears anywhere in project materials, it should be replaced with dimension-specific, citation-backed language (see Section 4).

This finding does **not** contradict the three-layer architecture, the integration gate, the Paas scale, or any other locked project decision — if anything it strengthens the case for the failure-trigger design (decompose/reframe/park rather than repeat-unchanged) and the "no adaptivity below a floor" rule, because both are consistent with a literature that says gains come from *how* material is re-encountered, not from compressing the encounter itself.

## 1. Claim-by-Claim Verdicts

### 1.1 Speed reading / RSVP / Spritz-style apps (1000+ wpm with comprehension)

**Claim:** Rapid Serial Visual Presentation (flashing single words at a fixed point, eliminating saccades) allows reading at 1000+ wpm with normal comprehension.

**Evidence:** This is one of the most directly falsified claims in the reading-science literature. The standard peer-reviewed reference for this is **Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter & Treiman (2016)**, "So Much to Read, So Little Time: How Do We Read, and Can Speed Reading Help?" (*Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 17(1)) — a comprehensive review concluding that comprehension degrades sharply as presentation rate rises, and that the eye movements RSVP eliminates (regressions and short saccades) are not wasted motion but functionally support working-memory updating, syntactic parsing, and re-checking of ambiguous referents. Gains that do appear under RSVP are typically confined to short, simple, low-ambiguity text — not the technical/novel material this system is meant for. There is no controlled trial showing durable 1000+ wpm reading with comprehension equivalent to normal-rate reading on unfamiliar material.

**Verdict: DEBUNKED for comprehension-preserving general reading.** RSVP may have a narrow niche (skimming already-familiar material to refresh) but is actively counter-indicated for a dyslexia-inclusive system: RSVP removes the reader's ability to self-pace regressions, which is a compensatory strategy many dyslexic readers rely on. Do not build an RSVP/Spritz feature.

### 1.2 "Learn Anything in 20 Hours" (Josh Kaufman)

**Claim:** ~20 hours of focused, deliberate practice produces "good enough" competence in any new skill, framed popularly as a fast-track method.

**Evidence:** Kaufman's book is a practitioner framework, not a peer-reviewed research program — it has no controlled trials behind it and was explicitly positioned by its author as a rebuttal to the (also non-peer-reviewed-as-stated) "10,000 hours to expertise" pop-psychology claim, not as a validated alternative. Its actual content — deconstruct the skill, learn enough to self-correct, remove practice barriers, practice at least 20 hours — is a repackaging of already-established ideas (deliberate practice structuring, barrier reduction, early-and-often practice) with no original measurement of outcomes.

Separately, the deliberate-practice literature it leans on is **actively contested, not settled**, at the "how much does practice explain" end — and this project's own locked evidence base (`2026-08-08_domain-general-transfer.md`) already documents the disagreement: Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014) found deliberate practice explains a *minority* of performance variance outside narrow domains like music/games (games 26%, music 21%, sports 18%, education 4%, professions <1%), while **Ericsson & Harwell (2019)** — applying stricter criteria to the same underlying question — reported a considerably *higher* estimate (29%, rising to 61% under corrected assumptions), explicitly disputing Macnamara et al.'s methodology rather than conceding their conclusion. That project source flags this as an unresolved contradiction between two different estimands, not a resolved walk-back in either direction. This brief's earlier framing ("Ericsson's own later work concedes this") was a mischaracterization and has been corrected: Ericsson & Harwell 2019 argues for a *larger* deliberate-practice effect than Macnamara et al., not a smaller one.

Twenty hours getting a learner from zero to "not embarrassing" at a simple perceptual-motor skill (juggling, basic ukulele chords) is plausible and roughly consistent with skill-acquisition curves, but the claim generalizes far beyond what's been tested — it says nothing about durable retention, about complex/technical/abstract material (the target domain here), or about learners with attentional or decoding differences.

**Verdict: UNSUPPORTED AS A GENERAL METHOD, plausible only for narrow early-competence in simple motor/procedural skills.** Not falsified because it was never rigorously tested, but it is not evidence for a 10x claim on the kind of material (technical/conceptual, retained long-term) this system targets. Its actually-useful component (barrier removal + early hands-on practice) is already subsumed by this project's existing retrieval-practice and worked-example foundations; nothing needs to be imported from it.

### 1.3 Superlearning / Accelerated-learning seminars (Lozanov-style suggestopedia, NLP-adjacent "accelerated learning" programs)

**Claim:** Techniques involving music (often baroque, ~60 bpm), relaxation, "whole-brain" suggestion, and paced breathing dramatically accelerate absorption of material (originally marketed for language learning).

**Evidence:** Lozanov's original suggestopedia claims (language vocabulary acquisition rates many multiples of normal classroom rates) were never independently replicated at the claimed magnitude. The broader "accelerated learning" seminar industry that grew out of this tradition has not produced peer-reviewed RCTs isolating its proprietary techniques from placebo/Hawthorne effects. Where relaxation/background-music claims specifically have been tested (adjacent to the "Mozart effect" literature), the well-replicated finding is null-to-negligible specific cognitive benefit beyond general arousal/mood effects.

**Caveat added on review:** this brief was unable to pin the specific claim about "follow-up controlled studies through the 1980s–90s" to a verified primary citation. That claim is a widely repeated conclusion in research-methods and educational-psychology overviews of suggestopedia, but is stated here without a directly verified reference and should be treated as plausible-but-unsourced pending a specific citation, rather than as settled as the RSVP debunking above (which does have a pinned source).

**Verdict: LIKELY DEBUNKED as a mechanism-specific accelerator, held with moderate rather than high confidence pending a sourced citation for the historical replication-failure claim.** Any residual benefit from these seminars is attributable to non-specific factors (motivation, anxiety reduction, structured practice) already achievable through validated means already in this project's stack — not to a novel "superlearning" effect. Do not adopt any of its specific mechanisms (paced music, breathing rituals, suggestion scripts).

### 1.4 Photographic / eidetic memory training claims

**Claim:** Adults can be trained to acquire photographic or eidetic memory (near-perfect, detailed visual recall of a scene or page after brief exposure), accelerating information intake.

**Evidence:** True eidetic imagery is a well-documented but narrow phenomenon almost exclusively observed in children and vanishing by adolescence in the small minority who ever show it (the standard reference for this line of work is Haber's eidetic-imagery research; this brief has not independently re-verified the specific citation in this pass and flags it accordingly). There is no credible peer-reviewed evidence that eidetic memory can be *trained into* neurotypical or atypical adults who don't already have it. What commercial "photographic memory" courses actually teach — and what does work, with real evidence behind it — is the method of loci and related mnemonic-imagery systems (already covered by this project's Ondřej 2025 meta-analysis), which produce large, real gains on structured/ordered recall tasks (e.g., list memorization, competitive memory-sport tasks) but do not confer generalized "photographic" encoding of arbitrary material, and their advantage shrinks substantially for meaningful, conceptual, or procedural material versus the list/sequence material they're validated on.

**Verdict: DEBUNKED as literally described.** The one real technique underneath the marketing (method of loci) is already in this project's evidence base and already appropriately scoped (structured/sequential recall, not general conceptual learning) — no new adoption needed, and "photographic memory" language should not appear anywhere in the system's positioning.

### 1.5 Commercial "brain training" apps (general cognitive-training claims) as of 2025–2026

**Claim:** Gamified cognitive-training apps (n-back-style working-memory games, reaction-time/attention drills, "neuroplasticity" training) improve general learning capacity/IQ/fluid intelligence, which then accelerates learning of unrelated material.

**Evidence:** This is the most heavily litigated claim in the whole set and this project has already flagged the relevant meta-analyses: **Sala et al. (2019)**, and **Simons, Boot, Charness, Gathercole, Chabris, Hambrick & Stine-Morrow (2016)**, "Do 'Brain-Training' Programs Work?" (*Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 17(3)) — both underlying the project's existing decision not to chase objective cognitive transfer as a metric. The consistent finding across large meta-analyses: near-transfer (getting better at the trained task itself) is robust and unsurprising; far-transfer (improvement on untrained cognitive abilities or real-world learning/academic outcomes) is small-to-null once studies are corrected for publication bias, active-control-group comparisons, and expectancy effects. The FTC's 2016 enforcement action against Lumosity (deceptive-advertising settlement, not a peer-reviewed finding, but corroborating the pattern) and subsequent industry-wide toning-down of marketing claims reflect the same conclusion. Newer 2025–2026 entrants (marketed with AI/personalization branding) have not published RCT evidence at a standard exceeding this base rate; personalization/AI framing changes the delivery mechanism, not the underlying transfer problem.

**Verdict: DEBUNKED for general/far-transfer "learn faster" claims; narrow near-transfer effects only.** This directly reinforces (does not contradict) the project's existing decision to not chase objective cognitive transfer as an outcome metric — that decision is well-founded and this audit finds no reason to revisit it. Any "brain training" mini-games should be excluded from the ADHD-support layer's design space; they are exactly the category this project's existing architecture was right to route around.

### 1.6 Commercial "learn 10x faster" courses/apps generally (2025–2026 landscape)

**Claim:** Various paid courses and apps (often bundling several of the above: speed reading + mnemonics + "brain training" + AI personalization) claim an aggregate 10x or more speedup, frequently citing partial/misapplied versions of the legitimate literature this project already uses (spacing, retrieval, sometimes citing Ebbinghaus/Bjork out of context).

**Evidence:** The pattern across this category is *citation laundering*: real, replicated findings (spacing effects, testing effects) are cited accurately for their existence but their effect sizes are silently inflated or stacked multiplicatively to justify the marketing headline number. No aggregator app or course has published a peer-reviewed RCT showing a 10x (or even 3x) reduction in time-to-competence on complex/technical material relative to a well-implemented spaced-retrieval control condition (i.e., relative to what Anki/FSRS-style scheduling alone already achieves). Where such apps do show internal metrics, they are typically engagement/completion metrics, not independently verified learning-outcome comparisons against a rigorous active control.

**Verdict: UNSUPPORTED / MARKETING-INFLATED.** The real underlying techniques are good (and already in this project's stack) — the multiplicative "10x" packaging on top of them is not evidence-based. (This audit itself was flagged on review for a milder version of the same pattern — see Section 4 and Adversarial Review Notes.)

## 2. Cross-Cutting Pattern

Every debunked or unsupported claim above shares a structure worth naming explicitly, because it's the pattern most likely to leak back into this project's own marketing/framing if not guarded against:

1. **A real, narrow, well-evidenced effect exists somewhere nearby** (method of loci for sequential lists; deliberate practice for perceptual-motor skills; spacing/testing effects for durable retention).
2. **The claim generalizes the narrow effect to "learning" writ large**, dropping the boundary conditions (material type, learner population, outcome measure) under which it was actually demonstrated.
3. **The generalized claim is stated as a multiplier** ("10x," "photographic," "1000 wpm") rather than as a bounded, dimension-specific effect size, because multipliers sell and effect sizes don't.

This project should hold itself to the opposite standard in its own outcome claims: bounded, dimension-specific, sourced to the actual studies already in its evidence base — never a bare multiplier. Notably, the first draft of this very brief was caught by adversarial review attaching precise-sounding but unsourced numeric ranges to real effects (see Adversarial Review Notes) — a milder version of exactly this pattern. That's corrected below by reporting the direction of each effect without inventing a magnitude the cited sources don't state.

## 3. Distinguishing the Three Dimensions (This Is the Part Marketing Erases)

The existing validated technique stack (retrieval practice, spaced repetition, worked examples, desirable difficulties, interleaving) does **not** move uniformly along a single "speed" axis. It affects at least three distinguishable dimensions, and the honest picture requires separating them:

**(a) Faster initial acquisition/encoding** — getting new material into memory the first time.
Retrieval practice and spacing are, if anything, often *slower* here in wall-clock terms during the initial session: retrieval practice under difficult conditions ("desirable difficulties," Bjork; Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009 on how unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance subsequent learning) frequently produces *worse* immediate performance than restudy/massed exposure — that's the defining, counterintuitive feature of the phenomenon. Worked examples do speed early acquisition specifically for novices on high-complexity material by reducing extraneous cognitive load (Sweller), which is a genuine (a)-dimension win, but it's bounded to the novice/high-complexity case — the expertise-reversal effect (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003; flagged here as a citation not yet on this project's locked source list and worth independent verification before being treated as load-bearing) means worked examples stop helping and can even hurt once the learner is no longer a novice on that material. This is not itself a multi-x claim in the literature — it's usually reported as reduced error rates and reduced study time to a fixed performance criterion within a single experimental session, not a blanket speedup.

**(b) Faster path to fluency/automaticity** — repeated retrieval to build speed/effortlessness at a skill, not just correctness.
This is where retrieval practice earns its most direct "fewer repetitions to criterion" evidence: testing-effect studies (Karpicke & Blunt 2011; the broader Dunlosky et al. 2013 utility ranking) and spacing-vs-massing comparisons (Cepeda et al. 2006) consistently find spaced retrieval reaches a fixed accuracy/fluency criterion in meaningfully fewer total exposures than massed restudy. **On adversarial review, this brief's original numeric range ("roughly one-quarter to one-half fewer repetitions") could not be traced to a specific reported statistic in Karpicke & Blunt 2011 or Dunlosky et al. 2013 — those papers report standardized effect sizes and utility rankings, not a repetitions-to-criterion percentage — so the number has been removed.** The honest claim is qualitative: this is a real, replicable, moderate-to-large effect on fewer-repetitions-to-criterion, but a specific percentage should not be quoted downstream without pulling it from a primary source that actually reports it.

**(c) Reduced forgetting / better long-term retention** — how much survives at a delay (a week, a month, a year) per unit of original study effort.
This is where the effect sizes are largest and best-replicated, and it is the dimension most directly targeted by this project's Anki/FSRS spacing layer. Spacing-vs-massing studies at 30+ day delays show meaningful retention advantages relative to massed or unspaced restudy at matched total study time — this is one of the most robust findings in the whole literature base this project already cites (Cepeda et al. 2006's meta-regression; Rowland 2014's testing-effect meta-analysis, showing durable advantages for retrieval practice over restudy at a delay). **On adversarial review, this brief's original "roughly 1.5x to 2x" figure could not be traced to a specific ratio reported in Cepeda et al. 2006 or Rowland 2014 — those are reported as standardized effect sizes / meta-regression coefficients over retention interval, not as a stable recall-ratio multiplier — so the number has been removed and replaced with the qualitative claim it actually supports.** Separately, one critique flagged that this brief's claim of testing-effect advantages "growing rather than shrinking with delay" overstated what can be confidently attributed to Rowland 2014's own moderator analysis specifically; the growing-with-delay pattern is more clearly established as the classic Roediger & Karpicke crossover finding. That distinction is noted rather than the claim being dropped outright, since the general direction (retrieval-practice advantage persisting or growing at longer delays, rather than shrinking) is a real and often-replicated pattern in this literature — it just shouldn't be pinned specifically to Rowland 2014's moderator results without checking that paper's text directly. This is the dimension where a "several-fold, real advantage" claim is directionally defensible — but it is a *retention-at-a-fixed-delay* claim, not a *speed-of-learning* claim, and it assumes the learner already got the material to an initial encoding threshold by some other means (worked examples, initial study, instruction) — the spacing/retrieval effects are downstream of that, not a substitute for it.

**The critical, project-relevant point:** (a), (b), and (c) trade off. Desirable difficulties (interleaving, spaced retrieval, generation) that most improve (c) frequently *slow down* (a) and can feel worse in the moment. Learners commonly misjudge massed/blocked practice as more effective even when it's demonstrably worse for retention — a well-known metacognitive illusion in the desirable-difficulties literature (associated with work such as Simon & Bjork 2001 on judgments-of-learning under massed vs. interleaved practice; flagged here as a citation not yet on this project's locked source list, offered as the more standard attribution than the earlier draft's tie to Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009, whose specific finding was about unsuccessful-retrieval-attempts enhancing later learning, not massed/blocked metacognitive misjudgment). This is also covered in Metcalfe 2017 and Bjork's broader work on the difference between conditions that feel productive and conditions that are productive. This is directly why the project's Paas 9-point subjective effort scale is the right instrument and a fixed cognitive-load percentage would be wrong: subjective ease is actively misleading about which condition is producing durable learning, so effort needs to be self-reported and interpreted against the desirable-difficulty framework rather than treated as a straightforward proxy for "this is working." It also means a learner (or this system's own dashboards) chasing a single "speed" number will systematically be pulled toward optimizing (a) at the expense of (c) — exactly the failure mode the 10x-framing claims above are selling.

## 4. Realistic Speedup Ceiling — Honest, Evidence-Bounded Estimates

Given only the validated stack already anchoring this project:

| Dimension | Realistic magnitude | Basis | Caveats |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Initial acquisition (novice, complex material) | Reduced errors and modestly reduced time-to-first-competence via worked examples vs. unguided problem-solving; not a multiplier claim in the literature | Sweller worked-example effect | Bounded to novices on high-complexity material; reverses (expertise-reversal effect, Kalyuga/Ayres/Chandler/Sweller 2003 — not yet on the locked list) once learner is no longer a novice on that specific content — the system must stop presenting worked examples as the learner advances |
| (b) Repetitions to reach a fixed fluency criterion | A real, moderate-to-large, well-replicated advantage for spaced retrieval vs. massed restudy; **no specific percentage is reported here** — a prior draft's "20–50% fewer repetitions" figure was not traceable to a specific statistic in the cited sources and has been removed | Testing-effect and spacing-effect literature (Karpicke & Blunt 2011; Dunlosky et al. 2013; Cepeda et al. 2006) | Wide variance by material and criterion definition; a numeric range should only be quoted downstream once pulled from a primary source that actually reports repetitions-to-criterion |
| (c) Retention at 30+ day delay, matched original study time | A real, large, well-replicated retention advantage for spaced/retrieval-based study over massed restudy at a fixed delay; **no specific multiplier is reported here** — a prior draft's "1.5–2x" figure was not traceable to a specific ratio in the cited meta-analyses and has been removed | Cepeda et al. 2006 meta-regression; Rowland 2014 | This is a retention-durability claim, not a wall-clock speed claim; assumes competent initial encoding already occurred; a specific multiplier should only be quoted downstream once pulled from a primary source that reports it as such |

**No single number across these three should be reported to the user as "your overall learning speedup."** They are not additive or multiplicative in any way the literature supports, and claiming so would repeat exactly the citation-laundering pattern identified in Section 2 — a pattern this brief's own first draft partially fell into and has since corrected (see Adversarial Review Notes).

**If the project needs one sentence for internal or user-facing framing, the defensible version is:**

> "This system won't make you absorb new material 10x faster the first time you see it. What the evidence supports is: real, well-replicated reductions in how many repetitions it takes to reach solid fluency when you use retrieval and spacing instead of re-reading, and real, well-replicated improvements in how much survives a month later for the same amount of original study effort. The honest pitch is 'less forgetting per hour invested,' not '10x faster learning' — and we should hold off on quoting a specific multiplier for either effect until it's pulled from a primary source that actually reports one."

## What This Means for the Build Plan

- **No architectural changes required.** The three-layer architecture, integration gate, Paas scale, failure-trigger design, and no-adaptivity-below-a-floor rule are all consistent with (and in the case of the failure triggers and Paas scale, specifically well-suited to) this more modest and honest set of claims. The only change indicated is at the level of what outcome the system promises and measures, not what it builds.
- **Retire "10x" everywhere.** Audit all existing project docs (PROJECT.md, UX briefs, session logs) for literal "10x," "photographic," or "speed reading" language and replace with the dimension-specific framing in Section 4.
- **Do not build:** an RSVP/Spritz-style speed-reading mode (actively counter-indicated for dyslexic readers — see §1.1); any superlearning/suggestopedia-style ambient-audio or suggestion mechanics (§1.3); a "photographic memory" feature or framing (§1.4); or brain-training mini-games as a claimed accelerant to subject-matter learning (§1.5).
- **If a user-facing progress metric is ever built, anchor it to dimension (c)** — retention at a fixed delay per unit study time — rather than a composite "speed" score, to avoid the (a)-vs-(c) optimization trap described in Section 3.
- **Do not quote a specific percentage or multiplier** (e.g., "20–50% fewer reps," "1.5–2x retention") in any user-facing or planning copy until it is sourced to a primary study that actually reports that statistic. This audit removed two such figures from its own first draft; treat any future numeric claim in this space with the same scrutiny.
- **No new techniques are recommended for adoption out of this audit** — every technique found to actually work (method of loci, worked examples, spacing, retrieval practice) is already present in the project's existing evidence base.
- **Explicit contradiction check against locked project decisions: none found.** This audit's findings are compatible with all locked decisions reviewed (three-layer architecture; integration gate; Paas scale; failure-trigger design; Obsidian+Anki stack; no-gamification-except-de-identified-team-competition; no dyslexia fonts/learning styles; locked UX spec; no notification-based adherence; no chasing objective transfer; adaptivity floor). Where this audit's findings intersect most directly with an existing decision (the no-objective-transfer decision, §1.5; the Paas-over-fixed-percentage decision, §3) they corroborate rather than challenge it.

## Adversarial Review Notes

Two independent adversarial fact-checks reviewed the first draft of this brief. Both agreed the central thesis (retire "10x," reframe around repetitions-to-criterion and delayed retention) is well-supported and consistent with locked project decisions, but both independently flagged that parts of the brief's own supporting detail didn't meet the rigor bar the brief was applying to the claims it was debunking. Changes made as a result:

- **Corrected a mischaracterization of a locked-list source.** The draft said Ericsson's later work "concedes" the Macnamara et al. (2014) walk-back of deliberate practice's explanatory power. Both critiques independently flagged this as backwards, and this project's own locked evidence base (`2026-08-08_domain-general-transfer.md`) confirms it: Ericsson & Harwell (2019) reports a *higher* variance-explained estimate (29%, rising to 61% corrected) than Macnamara et al., explicitly disputing their methodology rather than conceding their conclusion. §1.2 has been rewritten to describe this as an unresolved, contested disagreement between two estimands, not a settled walk-back.
- **Removed an off-topic citation stretch.** The draft invoked Miller/Cowan working-memory-capacity findings as supporting evidence for why RSVP degrades reading comprehension. One critique correctly noted Miller/Cowan are about short-term-memory *capacity* (chunk limits), not oculomotor reading mechanics, and that this borrowed the authority of an already-vetted citation for a claim it doesn't actually support. §1.1 now cites the actual on-point source instead: Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter & Treiman (2016), the standard peer-reviewed speed-reading/RSVP debunking reference, which the original draft never named despite being the obvious citation.
- **Corrected a likely misattribution.** The draft attached the "learners misjudge massed/blocked practice as superior" metacognitive-illusion finding to Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009), which is actually about unsuccessful-retrieval-attempts enhancing subsequent learning — a related but distinct finding. §3 now attributes the massed-practice illusion to the more standard source (Simon & Bjork 2001), explicitly flagged as a citation not yet on this project's locked list and worth independent verification, while keeping Kornell/Hays/Bjork 2009 for the claim it actually supports.
- **Removed two unsupported precision figures — the brief's own version of the pattern it critiques.** "Roughly one-quarter to one-half fewer repetitions" (attributed to Karpicke & Blunt 2011 / Dunlosky et al. 2013) and "roughly 1.5x to 2x" retention (attributed to Cepeda et al. 2006 / Rowland 2014) were both flagged by both critiques as not traceable to a specific reported statistic in those sources — those meta-analyses report standardized effect sizes and meta-regression coefficients, not repetition percentages or recall ratios. Both figures have been removed from §3, §4, and the Executive Summary and replaced with qualitative language ("a real, moderate-to-large, well-replicated advantage"), with an explicit note that no percentage or multiplier should be quoted downstream until pulled from a primary source that actually reports one.
- **Hedged an overstated moderator claim.** The draft asserted Rowland 2014 shows testing-effect advantages "growing rather than shrinking with delay" as if that were a robust finding of Rowland's own meta-regression. One critique noted this is more clearly established via the classic Roediger & Karpicke crossover finding than confirmed as a specific Rowland-2014 result. §3 now attributes the growing-with-delay pattern to that more standard source and flags the Rowland-specific claim as unverified rather than stating it with full confidence.
- **Added missing citations for previously uncited "debunked" verdicts.** §1.1 now cites Rayner et al. 2016 directly (both critiques flagged its absence as a real gap given it's the standard reference for exactly this claim). §1.3 (suggestopedia) and §1.4 (eidetic memory fading by adolescence) remain without a fully verified primary citation after this pass; rather than asserting them with the same confidence as the RSVP verdict, §1.3's verdict has been downgraded from "DEBUNKED" to "LIKELY DEBUNKED, moderate confidence" with an explicit note that the historical replication-failure claim needs sourcing, and §1.4 now flags the eidetic-imagery citation (Haber) as not independently re-verified in this pass.
- **Fully specified a vague citation.** "Simons et al." (no year, no full reference) in §1.5 has been replaced with the complete citation: Simons, Boot, Charness, Gathercole, Chabris, Hambrick & Stine-Morrow (2016), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 17(3).
- **Named the expertise-reversal effect's actual source.** The draft folded the expertise-reversal effect into "Sweller worked-example effect" generically. §3 and §4 now attribute it to Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003), flagged as a citation not yet on this project's locked source list and worth independent verification before being treated as load-bearing elsewhere.
- **No change made — judgment call.** Neither critique disputed the core "retire 10x" conclusion, the compliance check on re-citing locked sources as new (both confirmed this was done correctly throughout), or the contradiction check against locked architecture decisions (both independently confirmed none exist). Those sections and conclusions are unchanged from the original draft. The FTC v. Lumosity characterization in §1.5 was also confirmed accurate by both critiques (an enforcement action/settlement, not a peer-reviewed finding) and is unchanged.
