Scenario-based use cases for the job-seeker audience: the problem today, when it happens, how we want it to change, and what we must support.

| Problem aspect (the challenge today) | Customer experience (when it happens) | Business goals (how we want it to change) | Future scenarios to support |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Job seekers can't tell if they're actually qualified for a role before applying, so they apply blindly or self-reject. | While scrolling listings; when deciding whether to apply; after a string of rejections or silence; when a post lists more than they have. | Know before applying whether a role truly fits; stop wasting effort on wrong-fit roles; focus where they can win; apply with confidence. | Show role fit and what's expected that's missing; give a clear go / stretch / no-go call; recommend which gaps to close first; prioritize a shortlist of winnable roles. |
| Job titles don't reliably describe the actual job, so seekers misjudge fit, seniority, and pay. | Comparing listings with the same title; when a 'manager' role is really a doer; deciding what level to target; pay or level negotiation. | See the real role behind the title; understand true seniority; avoid under- or over-applying; negotiate from accurate information. | Score how closely the post matches the standard for that title; detect title inflation or under-leveling; read true seniority from the duties; compare the post to the seeker's level. |
| Job descriptions are full of jargon and corporate-speak, so seekers can't tell what the role really means. | Reading a dense or vague post; unsure what a buzzword implies day to day; especially for less-experienced seekers. | Understand a post in plain language; see the real day-to-day work; feel confident they grasp the role. | Translate jargon into plain English and its implications; show the expected deliverables, tools, activities, and skills; surface the real day-to-day expectations. |
| Seekers don't know the unwritten expectations of a role, the duties expected but left off the post. | When a post looks simple but the job is bigger; after being surprised by duties on the job; when experts/coaches charge to reveal this. | See hidden expectations for free; walk in without blind spots; have the insider knowledge coaches charge for. | Reveal duties expected for the title but absent from the post; flag extra responsibilities beyond the normal scope; explain why each hidden duty matters. |
| Resumes aren't tailored to what the role actually values, so strong candidates get filtered out. | Applying with one generic resume; unsure what to emphasize; competing against many applicants. | Highlight the right experience; prove they can do the real duties; raise their interview rate honestly. | Show which experiences, skills, and deliverables to highlight for each expected duty; prioritize must-haves vs nice-to-haves; flag hidden duties worth proving. |
| Seekers don't know how to prepare for the specific interview, so they rely on generic advice. | After landing an interview; the night before; prepping with generic question lists; when anxiety is high. | Prep for the actual role; reduce interview anxiety; perform at true ability. | Generate likely questions from the role's real duties; show what the post will probe and why; provide a role-specific prep checklist; suggest questions to ask the employer. |
